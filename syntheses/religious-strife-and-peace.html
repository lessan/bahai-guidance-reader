Religious Strife and the Peace of the World: The Fourth Issue in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

Part II of The Promise of World Peace, the statement the Universal House of Justice addressed “To the Peoples of the World” in October 1985, reviews the declarations and conventions of the United Nations and then picks out “a few such issues” that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven. Religious strife is the fourth, after racism, the disparity between rich and poor, and unbridled nationalism. Its paragraph reads in full:

“Religious strife, throughout history, has been the cause of innumerable wars and conflicts, a major blight to progress, and is increasingly abhorrent to the people of all faiths and no faith. Followers of all religions must be willing to face the basic questions which this strife raises, and to arrive at clear answers. How are the differences between them to be resolved, both in theory and in practice? The challenge facing the religious leaders of mankind is to contemplate, with hearts filled with the spirit of compassion and a desire for truth, the plight of humanity, and to ask themselves whether they cannot, in humility before their Almighty Creator, submerge their theological differences in a great spirit of mutual forbearance that will enable them to work together for the advancement of human understanding and peace.” — Universal House of Justice, October 1985

This article follows that paragraph outward. It reads the paragraph closely and places it in the statement. It then sets out what the Writings say about where religious strife comes from, what it has cost, and how its question — “How are the differences between them to be resolved, both in theory and in practice?” — is answered in the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh, in His laws, and in the conduct of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá and the Bahá’í community. It traces how the House of Justice took the question further in its April 2002 letter to the world’s religious leaders, and it closes with notes for a songwriter.

A companion page, ReligionInTheLifeOfSociety, treats the constructive role of religion in society: religion as the means of order, the non-partisan stance, and the community’s contribution to public discourse. This page is narrower. It treats religion when it becomes the cause of war, and the remedy the Bahá’í teachings propose for that condition.

The paragraph, read closely

The paragraph has four sentences, and each does something the other six paragraphs in the series do not.

It names a constituency of every faith and none. Religious strife is “increasingly abhorrent to the people of all faiths and no faith.” The statement does not treat secular people as bystanders to a religious problem. They are among those who suffer from it and among those who reject it. The phrase anticipates the House of Justice’s description, in its 18 January 2019 message, of Bahá’í devotional meetings to which the friends “invite … followers of all faiths and none.”

It asks questions instead of giving a prescription. The other paragraphs in the series state remedies: legal measures for racism, consultation with experts for poverty, a wider loyalty for nationalism, education for girls. This one asks “Followers of all religions” to “face the basic questions which this strife raises, and to arrive at clear answers,” and then states the question: “How are the differences between them to be resolved, both in theory and in practice?” The words “in theory” concern doctrine — how one religion understands the truth claims of another. The words “in practice” concern conduct — how believers of different faiths live beside one another. Both halves are needed, and the rest of this article is organised around them.

It names the people who must act. “The challenge facing the religious leaders of mankind” is placed on those who hold authority in religious communities. No other paragraph in the series addresses religious leaders; of the other six, only the paragraph on education names its addressees, and they are governments and “decision-making agencies.”

It asks for a posture before it asks for a programme. The leaders are asked to contemplate “the plight of humanity” with “compassion and a desire for truth,” and, “in humility before their Almighty Creator,” to “submerge their theological differences in a great spirit of mutual forbearance.” The word is submerge, not abandon. The differences remain; they are placed below a shared purpose — “to work together for the advancement of human understanding and peace.”

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

Part I already made the long argument

The religious-strife paragraph is short because Part I of the statement has already dealt with religion at length. Part I opens with the claim that “No serious attempt to set human affairs aright, to achieve world peace, can ignore religion,” concedes that “the perversion of this faculty has contributed to much of the confusion in society and the conflicts in and between individuals,” and then assigns responsibility:

“If, therefore, humanity has come to a point of paralyzing conflict it must look to itself, to its own negligence, to the siren voices to which it has listened, for the source of the misunderstandings and confusion perpetrated in the name of religion. Those who have held blindly and selfishly to their particular orthodoxies, who have imposed on their votaries erroneous and conflicting interpretations of the pronouncements of the Prophets of God, bear heavy responsibility for this confusion—a confusion compounded by the artificial barriers erected between faith and reason, science and religion.” — Universal House of Justice, October 1985

The next sentence states the evidence: “from a fair-minded examination of the actual utterances of the Founders of the great religions, and of the social milieus in which they were obliged to carry out their missions, there is nothing to support the contentions and prejudices deranging the religious communities of mankind and therefore all human affairs.” The Golden Rule, “an ethic variously repeated in all the great religions,” is offered as a sign of the unity that the religions share and that “mankind in its disjointed view of history has failed to appreciate.”

Part I then names the fanaticism of its own decade. “The resurgence of fanatical religious fervor occurring in many lands cannot be regarded as more than a dying convulsion.” The statement observes that such fanaticism is “undermining not only the spiritual values which are conducive to the unity of mankind but also those unique moral victories won by the particular religion it purports to serve.” Fanaticism, on this account, damages the religion that claims it first.

So by the time Part II reaches religious strife, the diagnosis is in place: the Founders are not the source of the conflict; their followers’ orthodoxies and interpretations are. The paragraph in Part II can therefore move straight to the question and the challenge.

The paralysis of will

The seven issues in Part II answer a specific problem. The statement says the world does not lack knowledge about peace: “There is, however, a paralysis of will.” That paralysis is “rooted … in a deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind.” Religious strife feeds this conviction more directly than any of the other six issues. When the institutions that claim to speak for God are seen to fight one another, the conclusion that human beings are incurably aggressive becomes easy to draw. Part I had already called that conclusion a falsehood whose roots lie in materialism; the religious-strife paragraph asks the religions to stop supplying evidence for it.

The two points that follow the seven

After the seventh issue the statement adds two points that apply to all of them. Peace is “not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols”; and “the primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism. For, in essence, peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude.” The religious-strife paragraph already works at this level. It asks for compassion, a desire for truth, and humility — inner states — before it mentions working together.

Parts III and IV

Part III lists the prejudices that recognition of the oneness of mankind requires people to abandon: “prejudice of every kind—race, class, color, creed, nation, sex, degree of material civilization.” Creed is on the list. Part III also quotes Shoghi Effendi’s description, from The Goal of a New World Order (28 November 1931), of the world community Bahá’u’lláh anticipated, one “in which the clamor of religious fanaticism and strife will have been forever stilled.”

Part IV finds a sign of hope in “the remarkable movement towards ecumenism by which members of historically antagonistic religions and sects seem irresistibly drawn towards one another,” and offers the Bahá’í community as a model for study — a community “cherishing equally all the great outpourings of divine guidance in human history.” The statement ends with words Bahá’u’lláh spoke to the Cambridge orientalist Edward Granville Browne at Bahjí in April 1890: “These fruitless strifes, these ruinous wars shall pass away, and the ‘Most Great Peace’ shall come.”

Where religious strife comes from

The Writings and the statement locate the source of religious strife in the followers of the religions, not in their Founders. Several distinct mechanisms are named.

Exclusive claims to God’s favour

In Paris on 27 October 1911, in a talk recorded as “Religious Prejudices,” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá named what He called “the principal reason of the unrest among nations”:

“The chief cause is the misrepresentation of religion by the religious leaders and teachers. They teach their followers to believe that their own form of religion is the only one pleasing to God, and that followers of any other persuasion are condemned by the All-Loving Father and deprived of His Mercy and Grace. Hence arise among the peoples disapproval, contempt, disputes and hatred.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks

At Green Acre in Eliot, Maine, on 17 August 1912, He described the same mechanism with an image. “Each one of the divine religions considers itself as belonging to a goodly and blessed tree, the tree of the Merciful, and all other religious systems as belonging to a tree of evil, the tree of Satan. For this reason they heap execration and abuse upon each other.” Bahá’u’lláh’s answer, as ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá reported it in the same talk, was: “Ye are all the fruits of one tree. There are not two trees: one a tree of divine mercy, the other the tree of Satan.”

Imitation of ancestral forms

The second mechanism is the substitution of inherited practice for the teaching itself. To the Japanese Young Men’s Christian Association in Oakland on 7 October 1912, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said:

“The foundation of all the divine religions is one. … All have summoned souls to peace and accord. All have proclaimed the virtues of humanity. All have guided souls to the attainment of perfections, but among the nations certain imitations of ancestral forms of worship have arisen. These imitations are not the foundation and essence of the divine religions. Inasmuch as they differ from the reality and the essential teachings of the Manifestations of God, dissensions have arisen, and prejudice has developed. Religious prejudice thus becomes the cause of warfare and battle.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

The remedy this implies is the first principle ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá listed in His talks: the independent investigation of reality. In Sacramento on 25 October 1912 He said that “these variations and imitations have ever been productive of warfare, sedition and strife,” and that “Reality is one; and when found, it will unify all mankind.”

Clerical authority over interpretation

Bahá’u’lláh, in the Kitáb‑i‑Íqán, describes the part religious leaders have played in every age: “Leaders of religion, in every age, have hindered their people from attaining the shores of eternal salvation, inasmuch as they held the reins of authority in their mighty grasp. Some for the lust of leadership, others through want of knowledge and understanding, have been the cause of the deprivation of the people.”

One Common Faith (2005), prepared under the supervision of the House of Justice, connects this to sectarian hatred. “In the absence of scriptural texts that established unarguable institutional authority, clerical elites succeeded in arrogating to themselves exclusive control over interpretation of the Divine intent.” Among the effects it lists: to “too often engender hatred and prejudice towards those following a different sectarian path from that of self-appointed spiritual leaders.”

Shoghi Effendi’s caution matters here, and the article does not depart from it. In The Promised Day Is Come (28 March 1941) he writes that the followers of Bahá’u’lláh do not “seek to degrade or even belittle the rank of the world’s religious leaders, whether Christian, Muslim, or of any other denomination, should their conduct conform to their professions, and be worthy of the position they occupy.” The 2002 letter likewise honours “the unheralded inner victories won … by unnumbered clerics all down the ages.” The criticism is aimed at a use of authority, not at an office or a faith.

Manipulation for political ends

The fourth mechanism is deliberate. A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 27 December 2017, answering a question about religious prejudice, says that this prejudice “is often deliberately fostered and exploited through manipulation and propaganda, using methods that ignore truth and promote self-serving agendas for political or other expediencies.” The 2002 letter makes the same point about inherited culture: “What cannot be morally justified is the manipulation of cultural legacies that were intended to enrich spiritual experience, as a means to arouse prejudice and alienation.”

The Founders are not to blame

Across these mechanisms the Writings keep one distinction fixed. In the Paris talk, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá recalls Christ’s words to Peter in the garden — “Put up thy sword into the sheath” — and continues: “Yet, in spite of the direct command of the Lord they profess to serve—men still dispute, make war, and kill one another, and His counsels and teaching seem quite forgotten. But do not therefore attribute to the Masters and Prophets the evil deeds of their followers.” The 2017 letter states the same thing in institutional language: when “in the name of religion, conflict, prejudice, and hatred are engendered among humanity, this is due to fallible human interpretations and impositions that can be overcome by seeking the divine truth that lies at the heart of every religion.”

What it has cost

The Writings describe the cost of religious strife in plain terms and sometimes in specific scenes.

Bahá’u’lláh, in a passage gathered in Gleanings (CXXXII) and in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, gives the image that the House of Justice has since quoted repeatedly:

“Gird up the loins of your endeavor, O people of Bahá, that haply the tumult of religious dissension and strife that agitateth the peoples of the earth may be stilled, that every trace of it may be completely obliterated. … Religious fanaticism and hatred are a world-devouring fire, whose violence none can quench. The Hand of Divine power can, alone, deliver mankind from this desolating affliction.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXXXII

Two points in the passage are easy to pass over. The task of stilling religious strife is given to the “people of Bahá” themselves, as a duty. And the remedy named is divine power, not human management. The House of Justice drew the same conclusion in 2002: “Such a danger civil government, unaided, cannot overcome.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, in His first Tablet to the Central Organisation for a Durable Peace at The Hague (17 December 1919), counts the wars of recorded history:

“During these 6,000 years the world of humanity has not been free from war, strife, murder and bloodthirstiness. In every period war has been waged in one country or another and that war was due to either religious prejudice, racial prejudice, political prejudice or patriotic prejudice.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Tablets to The Hague

He also spoke of what He had seen. In Oakland in October 1912, while war was gathering in the Balkans, He said: “Some years ago when I was living in Rumelia, war broke out among the religious peoples. … They pillaged the properties of each other, burning each others’ homes and houses, slaughtering men, women and children, imagining that such warfare and bloodshed was the means of drawing near to God.” (Rumelia was the Ottoman name for its Balkan provinces; the talk does not say which year He meant.) At Green Acre two months earlier He described how Christians and Muslims had treated the Jews: “Great numbers of Jews were killed, their houses burned and pillaged, their children carried into captivity.”

The 2002 letter gives a single historical figure: “Europe’s sixteenth century wars of religion cost that continent the lives of some thirty percent of its entire population.” One Common Faith records that after the Second World War “even so influential a figure as Mohandas Gandhi proved unable to mobilize the spiritual power of Hinduism in support of his efforts to extinguish sectarian violence on the Indian subcontinent.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá drew from all this a test that applies to every religion, including His own. In Paris He said:

“Religion should unite all hearts and cause wars and disputes to vanish from the face of the earth, give birth to spirituality, and bring life and light to each heart. If religion becomes a cause of dislike, hatred and division, it were better to be without it, and to withdraw from such a religion would be a truly religious act. For it is clear that the purpose of a remedy is to cure; but if the remedy should only aggravate the complaint, it had better be left alone.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks

The same test appears in the Tablet to The Hague: “If this prejudice and enmity are on account of religion consider that religion should be the cause of fellowship, otherwise it is fruitless.” The image of religion as medicine sets the standard: a religion is judged by whether it heals.

The answer in theory: one Source, one Faith, progressive revelation

The 1985 paragraph asks how differences between religions are to be resolved “in theory.” The Bahá’í answer is a doctrine of religion, not a negotiated truce. It is set out in ManifestationAndProgressiveRevelation; here only the passages that bear directly on strife are gathered.

One Source, and laws suited to their age

The passage the House of Justice chose as the centre of its 2002 letter is from Gleanings CXI. It begins “O CONTENDING peoples and kindreds of the earth!” and continues:

“There can be no doubt whatever that the peoples of the world, of whatever race or religion, derive their inspiration from one heavenly Source, and are the subjects of one God. The difference between the ordinances under which they abide should be attributed to the varying requirements and exigencies of the age in which they were revealed. All of them, except a few which are the outcome of human perversity, were ordained of God, and are a reflection of His Will and Purpose. Arise and, armed with the power of faith, shatter to pieces the gods of your vain imaginings, the sowers of dissension amongst you. Cleave unto that which draweth you together and uniteth you.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXI

The passage explains the differences between religions without denying them. Their ordinances differ because the ages in which they were revealed differed. The “gods of your vain imaginings” are not the Founders or the scriptures; they are what the passage calls “the sowers of dissension amongst you.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, at Temple Emmanu‑El in San Francisco on 12 October 1912, explained the same point by dividing each religion into two parts. One “concerns the world of morality and the ethical training of human nature”; the second “comprises social laws and regulations applicable to human conduct. This is not the essential spiritual quality of religion. It is subject to change and transformation according to the exigencies and requirements of time and place.” Most religious disputes, on this account, are disputes about the second part conducted as though they were about the first.

The unity of the Manifestations

Bahá’u’lláh describes the Founders of the religions as one in essence: “If thou wilt observe with discriminating eyes, thou wilt behold Them all abiding in the same tabernacle, soaring in the same heaven, seated upon the same throne, uttering the same speech, and proclaiming the same Faith” (Gleanings, XXII). He also forbids His own followers to rank them: “BEWARE, O believers in the Unity of God, lest ye be tempted to make any distinction between any of the Manifestations of His Cause … This indeed is the true meaning of Divine Unity” (Gleanings, XXIV).

In Gleanings CXXXII He puts the matter at the centre of His Faith:

“That the divers communions of the earth, and the manifold systems of religious belief, should never be allowed to foster the feelings of animosity among men, is, in this Day, of the essence of the Faith of God and His Religion. These principles and laws, these firmly established and mighty systems, have proceeded from one Source, and are the rays of one Light.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXXXII

The phrase “of the essence of the Faith of God” means that ending religious animosity is not an application of the Bahá’í teachings to a social problem. It is part of what the Faith is.

Religious truth is relative, and no revelation is final

Shoghi Effendi stated the principle beneath all of this in The Golden Age of the Cause of Bahá’u’lláh (21 March 1932):

“Its teachings revolve around the fundamental principle that religious truth is not absolute but relative, that Divine Revelation is progressive, not final. Unequivocally and without the least reservation it proclaims all established religions to be divine in origin, identical in their aims, complementary in their functions, continuous in their purpose, indispensable in their value to mankind.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh

The Guardian applied the principle to the Bahá’í Faith itself. In The Dispensation of Bahá’u’lláh (8 February 1934) he wrote that the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh “emphatically repudiates the claim to be regarded as the final revelation of God’s will and purpose for mankind,” and that “the categorical rejection by the followers of the Faith of Bahá’u’lláh of the claim to finality which any religious system inaugurated by the Prophets of the past may advance is as clear and emphatic as their own refusal to claim that same finality for the Revelation with which they stand identified.” This is the point on which the Bahá’í answer to religious strife depends. A Faith that asked other religions to give up claims to finality while keeping such a claim for itself would be asking for submission, not peace.

In The Promised Day Is Come the Guardian lists what the Faith does not seek. It “disclaims any intention to belittle any of the Prophets gone before Him, to whittle down any of their teachings, to obscure, however slightly, the radiance of their Revelations, to oust them from the hearts of their followers, to abrogate the fundamentals of their doctrines, to discard any of their revealed Books, or to suppress the legitimate aspirations of their adherents.” Its aim, he writes, is “to widen the basis of all revealed religions,” and it “separates the God-given truths from the priest-prompted superstitions.”

The fourth candle

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, in a Tablet that Shoghi Effendi quotes in The Goal of a New World Order, describes seven candles of unity that will be lit in this age. The fourth is religion:

“The fourth candle is unity in religion which is the corner-stone of the foundation itself, and which, by the power of God, will be revealed in all its splendor.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, quoted in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh

Among the seven, only unity in religion is called “the corner-stone of the foundation itself.” (These seven candles are a different list from the seven issues of The Promise of World Peace, though four themes appear on both: religion, nations, races, and language.)

The answer in practice: laws, conduct, community

The paragraph’s second question is how differences are to be resolved “in practice.” Here the Bahá’í answer is a set of laws, a pattern of conduct, and a community.

Laws that remove the grounds of religious separation

In the Bishárát (Glad-Tidings), revealed in ‘Akká after the Kitáb‑i‑Aqdas, the first two glad-tidings concern religious conflict directly:

“The first Glad-Tidings which the Mother Book hath, in this Most Great Revelation, imparted unto all the peoples of the world is that the law of holy war hath been blotted out from the Book.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Bishárát

“It is permitted that the peoples and kindreds of the world associate with one another with joy and radiance. O people! Consort with the followers of all religions in a spirit of friendliness and fellowship.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Bishárát

Later in the same Tablet, Bahá’u’lláh expresses the hope that “the weapons of war throughout the world may be converted into instruments of reconstruction and that strife and conflict may be removed from the midst of men.”

In the Kitáb‑i‑Aqdas He abolishes the concept of ritual impurity, which in some earlier communities had applied to people of other faiths:

“God hath, likewise, as a bounty from His presence, abolished the concept of “uncleanness,” whereby divers things and peoples have been held to be impure. … Verily, all created things were immersed in the sea of purification when, on that first day of Riḍván, We shed upon the whole of creation the splendors of Our most excellent Names and Our most exalted Attributes. … Consort ye then with the followers of all religions.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb‑i‑Aqdas, ¶75

And in paragraph 144: “Consort with all religions with amity and concord, that they may inhale from you the sweet fragrance of God. Beware lest amidst men the flame of foolish ignorance overpower you.”

In the Lawḥ‑i‑Dunyá (Tablet of the World) He says what the command to consort has already done: “Whatsoever hath led the children of men to shun one another, and hath caused dissensions and divisions amongst them, hath, through the revelation of these words, been nullified and abolished.”

The Kitáb‑i‑’Ahd, the Book of His Covenant, applies the same principle to His own followers: “O ye that dwell on earth! The religion of God is for love and unity; make it not the cause of enmity or dissension.” In the Ishráqát He gives the same instruction in nearly the same words: “The purpose of religion as revealed from the heaven of God’s holy Will is to establish unity and concord amongst the peoples of the world; make it not the cause of dissension and strife.” The House of Justice quoted that warning in its 18 January 2019 message on peace. The Covenant is what the Writings provide to keep the Bahá’í community from dividing into sects as earlier religions did, so the instruction against religious enmity appears in the document that founds the community’s own unity.

“It is better for you to be killed than to kill”

In Memorials of the Faithful, in the account of Ṭáhirih, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá contrasts the violence of the early Bábí period with what followed. In those early days, He writes, “no one knew that the Manifestation of the Báb would culminate in the Manifestation of the Blessed Beauty and that the law of retaliation would be done away with, and the foundation-principle of the Law of God would be this, that “It is better for you to be killed than to kill”.” He then describes the massacre of Bahá’ís in Yazd during His own ministry, when “men, women and children were made the targets of enemy fire or were put to the sword,” and records that “not one of the friends of God lifted a hand against them.” One of the martyrs, “when he saw the drawn sword flashing over him, thrust sugar candy into his murderer’s mouth and cried, “With a sweet taste on your lips, put me to death—for you bring me martyrdom, my dearest wish!””

The Bahá’í community has been, in the words of the 2017 letter, a community that “has suffered the direct consequences of religious prejudice for nearly two centuries, particularly in the land of its birth.” Its response has been the one ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá describes: no retaliation. The history is set out in bahai-iran-persecution. The 1985 statement itself ends by recalling “the persecution they still endure in the land in which their Faith was born.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá in the synagogue

The clearest example of the practice is ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s own conduct. At Temple Emmanu‑El in San Francisco on 12 October 1912 He spoke to a Jewish congregation about Moses, Christ and Muḥammad. He argued that Christ “did not deny the Prophethood of Moses; on the contrary, He proclaimed and ratified it,” and that Muḥammad’s first address to His tribe affirmed Moses and the Torah. He then asked what harm it does a believer to honour the Prophets of another faith:

“Verily, I now declare to you that Moses was the Interlocutor of God and a most noteworthy Prophet, that Moses revealed the fundamental law of God and founded the real ethical basis of the civilization and progress of humanity. What harm is there in this? Have I lost anything by saying this to you and believing it as a Bahá’í? On the contrary, it benefits me.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

He closed with a sequence of declarations:

“The age has dawned when human fellowship will become a reality. The century has come when all religions shall be unified. The dispensation is at hand when all nations shall enjoy the blessings of international peace. The cycle has arrived when racial prejudice will be abandoned by tribes and peoples of the world. The epoch has begun wherein all native lands will be conjoined in one great human family.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

Here the “practice” half of the 1985 question is answered by example. A religious leader stands in another faith’s house of worship, affirms that faith’s Prophet without reservation, and says that doing so costs him nothing.

What the community does now

The 27 December 2017 letter describes the present Bahá’í practice. Bahá’í children “are taught from the earliest age about the common foundation of all the world religions, to accept and love the Founders of all of them as their own, and to embrace those of all religions or none with friendliness and fellowship.” The letter quotes the House of Justice’s standard for individual conduct: “So free must be your thoughts and actions of any trace of prejudice—racial, religious, economic, national, tribal, class, or cultural … that even the stranger sees in you loving friends.” It cites Shoghi Effendi’s instruction that every Bahá’í community “nurture, encourage, and safeguard every minority belonging to any faith, race, class, or nation within it,” and it names the community’s participation in public discourses on “the role of religion in society, religious coexistence, and freedom of religion or belief.”

The 18 January 2019 message describes the communities the friends are building as places that “cultivate environments in which children can be raised untainted by any form of racial, national, or religious prejudice,” where devotional meetings are open to “followers of all faiths and none.” It presents these communities as a working form of the model the 1985 statement offered for study.

From forbearance to a break with the past: the 2002 letter

The 1985 paragraph asked religious leaders to “submerge their theological differences in a great spirit of mutual forbearance.” In April 2002 the House of Justice wrote directly to the world’s religious leaders, and the letter goes further. Reading the two together shows how the House of Justice’s treatment of the issue developed over seventeen years.

What the century showed

The letter begins by observing that the prejudices of race, sex and nation had, during the twentieth century, lost their standing in public opinion, even where they persisted in practice. Religious prejudice had not. “As the twentieth century opened, the prejudice that seemed more likely than any other to succumb to the forces of change was that of religion.” The interfaith movement that began with the “Parliament of Religions” at the World’s Columbian Exposition of 1893 had flourished; yet, the letter says, “these initiatives lack both intellectual coherence and spiritual commitment.” It describes the result:

“Yet, the greater part of organized religion stands paralyzed at the threshold of the future, gripped in those very dogmas and claims of privileged access to truth that have been responsible for creating some of the most bitter conflicts dividing the earth’s inhabitants.” — Universal House of Justice, April 2002

The word paralyzed recalls the “paralysis of will” of 1985. In 1985 the paralysis was humanity’s; in 2002 the letter locates one form of it in organised religion.

The letter states the problem bluntly: “organized religion, whose very reason for being entails service to the cause of brotherhood and peace, behaves all too frequently as one of the most formidable obstacles in the path; to cite a particular painful fact, it has long lent its credibility to fanaticism.”

What the letter asks for

The letter quotes Gleanings CXI — the “one heavenly Source” passage — and draws its consequence:

“Such an appeal does not call for abandonment of faith in the fundamental verities of any of the world’s great belief systems. Far otherwise. Faith has its own imperative and is its own justification. What others believe—or do not believe—cannot be the authority in any individual conscience worthy of the name. What the above words do unequivocally urge is renunciation of all those claims to exclusivity or finality that, in winding their roots around the life of the spirit, have been the greatest single factor in suffocating impulses to unity and in promoting hatred and violence.” — Universal House of Justice, April 2002

This is the clear answer the 1985 paragraph asked religious leaders to reach. The letter names claims to exclusivity or finality as “the greatest single factor” behind religious hatred, and asks that they be renounced. It protects each believer’s faith in its own “fundamental verities” in the same passage.

The letter then says plainly that forbearance alone is not enough:

“Nor should we delude ourselves that appeals for mutual tolerance can alone hope to extinguish animosities that claim to possess Divine sanction. The crisis calls on religious leadership for a break with the past as decisive as those that opened the way for society to address equally corrosive prejudices of race, gender and nation.” — Universal House of Justice, April 2002

The two letters do not conflict. The 1985 paragraph asks for a posture — compassion, humility, forbearance — that would let leaders work together. The 2002 letter names the doctrine that keeps the conflict alive while that posture is maintained: an animosity that believes God sanctions it will outlast any appeal to tolerance, because tolerance does not touch the belief. Forbearance is the condition for beginning; renouncing claims to exclusivity is the step that ends the conflict.

The letter also addresses three related matters. It anticipates the objection that recognising all religions as divine would encourage conversion, and calls this “surely of peripheral importance” against the opportunity history has opened. It asks religious leaders to accept that “religious convictions, no matter how cherished they may be, must submit, willingly and gratefully, to impartial testing by scientific methods,” which echoes the 1985 statement’s complaint about “artificial barriers erected between faith and reason.” And it raises, “with some diffidence,” the temptation of “exercising power in matters of belief.” It states the individual’s task in one sentence: “The primary task of the soul will always be to investigate reality, to live in accordance with the truths of which it becomes persuaded and to accord full respect to the efforts of others to do the same.”

What followed

In its foreword to One Common Faith (Naw‑Rúz 2005), the House of Justice reported that Bahá’í institutions had delivered “thousands of copies” of the letter to figures in the major faith communities, that it had been “dismissed out of hand in a few circles” but “in general” warmly welcomed, and that some recipients had reproduced and distributed it within their own traditions. It explained that it had written the letter “out of awareness that the disease of sectarian hatreds, if not decisively checked, threatens harrowing consequences that will leave few areas of the world unaffected.” It then turned the responsibility back to the Bahá’ís, who “must draw on a deep understanding of the process by which humanity’s spiritual life evolves.”

In its 18 January 2019 message on peace, the House of Justice observed that “Religious fundamentalism is warping the character of communities, even nations,” and that religious leaders “can also incite violence by using their influence to stoke the fires of fanaticism and prejudice.” The diagnosis of 1985 had not lost its relevance thirty-four years later.

The present field

External sources — description of the present situation only, not authority for any Bahá’í principle. Figures current as of October 2026 and will age.

The Pew Research Center has measured religion-related hostility across 198 countries and territories each year since 2007. Its sixteenth annual report, published 15 June 2026 and covering 2023, counted 55 countries with high or very high levels of social hostilities involving religion — hostility by private individuals, groups and organisations, including terrorist groups — up from 45 the previous year. The report states that this remained below the highest number recorded since the study began, 65. It also found that government harassment of religious groups occurred in 185 of the 198 countries and territories studied. (Pew Research Center, “More Countries Had Elevated Levels of Social Hostilities Involving Religion in 2023,” 15 June 2026)

These figures describe the condition the 1985 paragraph named. They say nothing about its remedy, which in the Bahá’í view operates at the level of belief and attitude that such measurements do not reach.

Open questions and limits

  • Talks are not Tablets. Many of the most vivid passages above — the two trees, Rumelia, the synagogue in San Francisco, “better to be without it” — come from Paris Talks and The Promulgation of Universal Peace, which are compiled from notes and transcripts of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s spoken addresses. They carry less weight than His written Tablets. Where a principle matters, this article pairs the talk with a written source: the Tablet to The Hague for religion that does not cause fellowship being “fruitless”, and the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh for the oneness of the Manifestations.
  • The Browne interview is Browne’s recollection. The words “these fruitless strifes, these ruinous wars shall pass away” are Browne’s English record of what Bahá’u’lláh said to him at Bahjí in April 1890. Shoghi Effendi quotes them in God Passes By, and the House of Justice quotes them to close the 1985 statement, but they are not a revealed text.
  • “That diversity of religion should cease.” In the same interview Bahá’u’lláh named among His desires “that diversity of religion should cease.” Read alone, this could suggest that peace requires one religion to absorb the rest. The Guardian’s statement that the Faith disclaims any intention “to oust them from the hearts of their followers,” and the 2002 letter’s insistence that its appeal “does not call for abandonment of faith in the fundamental verities” of any religion, set the frame for reading it. The sources do not spell out in detail what the eventual unity of religions will look like in practice.
  • The Rumelia war is undated in the text. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá says only “Some years ago when I was living in Rumelia.” This article does not attempt to identify the conflict.
  • The 2002 letter speaks of all religions as equally divine in origin while the Bahá’í Faith presents Bahá’u’lláh as the Manifestation for this age. These are not in conflict on the Bahá’í account: progressive revelation holds both that every Revelation is from God and that each later one meets the needs of a later age, and the Guardian denies finality to Bahá’u’lláh’s Revelation as well. A reader new to the teaching may still find the two statements hard to hold together, and the sources answer the question at the level of principle rather than case by case.
  • The present-day figures are descriptive and dated. The Pew counts measure reported incidents and restrictions; they will change with each annual report.

For the song

The heart of it, in one sentence

Religious strife comes from the followers and not the Founders, and it ends when believers recognise that their religions “derive their inspiration from one heavenly Source,” stop claiming that God favours them alone, and “submerge their theological differences in a great spirit of mutual forbearance” to work together for peace.

Images, people, places and moments

  • The two trees. Each religion believing it grows on the tree of mercy and the others on the tree of Satan; Bahá’u’lláh saying there is one tree. (Green Acre, Maine, 17 August 1912.)
  • The sun on the horizon. In the same Paris series, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said the sun rises at different points on the horizon in summer and winter but is “always the self-same sun”; people of enlightened heart “worship truth on whatever horizon it appears.” (Theosophical Society, Paris.)
  • Religion as medicine. A remedy that makes the patient sicker should be put down; a religion that breeds hatred is better left. (Paris Talks; Tablet to The Hague.)
  • Peter’s sword. “Put up thy sword into the sheath” — the command of the Lord whose followers still make war. (Paris Talks, 27 October 1911.)
  • The synagogue in San Francisco. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, a Persian exile, honouring Moses before a Jewish congregation and asking what harm there is in it. (12 October 1912.)
  • Rumelia. Neighbours of different faiths burning each other’s houses, “imagining that such warfare and bloodshed was the means of drawing near to God.”
  • The sugar candy. A Bahá’í in Yazd, facing the sword, putting sweets in his killer’s mouth. “It is better for you to be killed than to kill.” (Memorials of the Faithful.)
  • The sea of purification. On the first day of Riḍván, all created things washed clean, so that no person may call another unclean. (Kitáb‑i‑Aqdas.)
  • Weapons into instruments of reconstruction. (Bishárát.)
  • The fourth candle. Unity in religion, “the corner-stone of the foundation itself.”
  • The Parliament of Religions, 1893 — the hope at the start of the century that religious prejudice would be the first to fall, and the House of Justice’s finding a century later that it had become the last. (2002 letter.)
  • Europe’s wars of religion, which cost “some thirty percent” of the continent’s population. (2002 letter.)
  • Gandhi unable to stop sectarian killing in the Indian subcontinent after the Second World War. (One Common Faith.)
  • A devotional meeting open to “followers of all faiths and none.” (18 January 2019.)

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • Blaming religion itself. The statement says religion is indispensable to peace (“No serious attempt to set human affairs aright, to achieve world peace, can ignore religion”). The fault lies with “particular orthodoxies” and “erroneous and conflicting interpretations,” and with materialism as much as with fanaticism. A song that says “religion causes war” contradicts its source.
  • Blaming the Founders. “Do not therefore attribute to the Masters and Prophets the evil deeds of their followers.”
  • Singling out one faith. Every passage above treats the problem as common to all religions. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s Paris talk includes an anecdote about a sermon He heard in Tiberias; taken out of context it would read as an attack on one community, which is the opposite of His point. The 2002 letter says that “no one could convincingly argue that doctrines attached to one particular belief system have been either more or less prolific in generating bigotry and superstition than those attached to any other.”
  • Calling for all religions to dissolve into one, or into none. The 1985 paragraph asks leaders to submerge differences, not erase them, and the 2002 letter says its appeal “does not call for abandonment of faith.” Nor is the answer secular: the Writings say “The Hand of Divine power can, alone” deliver mankind from fanaticism.
  • Presenting tolerance as the whole answer. The 2002 letter says appeals for tolerance cannot “alone” extinguish animosities that claim divine sanction. The remedy reaches doctrine: giving up claims to exclusivity and finality.
  • Presenting the Bahá’í Faith as the final religion that ends the others. Shoghi Effendi says the Faith “emphatically repudiates” finality for itself.
  • Turning lament into despair. The statement calls fanaticism “a dying convulsion” and ends with a promise. Its stance is that peace is “not only possible but inevitable.”
