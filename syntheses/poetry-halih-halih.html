Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat

Last updated 17 October 2026

Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat is a Persian poem by Bahá’u’lláh. Its refrain, repeated after each line, is hálih, hálih, hálih, yá bishárat, which translators render “Hallelujah, Hallelujah, Hallelujah, O Glad Tidings!” It has 24 one-line verses and a closing prose paragraph, and it dates from the last months of Bahá’u’lláh’s time in Baghdad. There is no authorised English translation. Two provisional translations exist, by Stephen Lambden and Sen McGlinn, and this page quotes both only in short extracts. Related pages: Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets and Baghdad and the Bábí gatherings.

The poem

Title and form. The title comes from the refrain. Lambden explains that the poem “derives its title from the refrain which follows each internally rhyming line (halih = Rejoice! or [loosely] Hallelujah!).” (Lambden, provisional translation, 1995, introduction.) Sen McGlinn counts “24 one-line verses and a final (and striking) concluding verse of 4 or 5 lines”, with the refrain after every verse except the last. (McGlinn, 1996, introduction.) The Persian text kept in this library has the same shape: 24 verse lines, each ending in the refrain, then one prose paragraph without it. The Bahaipedia article calls it “a twenty-six verse Persian poem”, which does not match the printed text; the discrepancy is probably one of counting.

Language and source text. The Persian is printed in Ishráq-Khávarí’s Ganj-i-Sháyigán, pp. 33–35. Lambden revised that printing against a manuscript in the INBA collection (INBAMC vol. 35, pp. 455–6). The Persian text used for this page is the Ganj-i-Sháyigán text as saved from oceanoflights.org ([source]), whose lines are not separated into stanzas.

When and where. The sources agree on Baghdad and on the final period there, and differ in precision:

  • Lambden: “Composed towards the end of Bahá’u’lláh’s residence in Baghdad (late 1862 or early 1863 CE?)”. The question mark is his.
  • McGlinn’s list of Bahá’u’lláh’s Writings dates it “late Baghdad (1862-3?)”.
  • Bahaipedia places it in the spring of 1863, “shortly before the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh in Baghdad”, and cites Julio Savi, Bahá’u’lláh’s Persian Poems Written Before 1863, in Lights of Irfan 13. No passage on this poem was found in the Savi article in the corpus, so this date rests on Bahaipedia’s citation.
  • Adib Taherzadeh lists it among the odes of the Baghdad years: “Another ode in Persian revealed during this period is known as Halih-Halih-Ya-Bisharat, which is very similar in its contents to Az-Bagh-i-Ilahi.” (Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, p. 219.) He describes Az-Bágh-i-Iláhí as “an ode revealed not long before the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh” and as “one of His most joyous odes”.

Bahá’u’lláh left Baghdad for the Garden of Riḍván in April 1863. None of the sources consulted says that Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat was revealed at Riḍván or is read or sung at Riḍván today, and this page does not say so.

What the Writings and the Guardian say. No statement was found by Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi or the Universal House of Justice about this poem in the corpus. Everything above about its date and meaning is scholarship. The closing paragraph of the poem itself states its purpose (see “The text in English”).

What scholars say of it. Lambden reads the poem as “expressive of his claim to special communion with God through a celestial maiden (hur…) and of his assuming leadership of the Babi community”, drawing “on Qur’anic, sufi and Babi imagery”. The same source calls it a “mystically oriented ode” that invites the Bábís to “listen to his ‘wondrous new melodies’”. Taherzadeh’s remark that the Baghdad odes are “very difficult to describe and may well prove impossible to translate” applies to this poem as to the others. That is Taherzadeh’s statement, not the Guardian’s. Franklin Lewis, writing in a different article, lists it among the Persian poems of the Baghdad period and notes that Shoghi Effendi did not translate any of Bahá’u’lláh’s versified poems. Shoghi Effendi did render one passage of the Ode of the Dove in God Passes By (GPB VII.31).

Use among Bahá’ís. Taherzadeh and the Guardian’s account of Baghdad (see Baghdad and the Bábí gatherings) describe the companions chanting Bahá’u’lláh’s odes at night. No source in the corpus ties this ode by name to a particular gathering. The well-known story of the evening when Bahá’u’lláh entered with rose-water concerns Az-Bágh-i-Iláhí, not this poem. In English, McGlinn’s article on the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár quotes a believer who walks on the land of a future House of Worship and thinks “maybe hum a few lines of the Halih”; the article footnotes Lambden’s translation. McGlinn also writes that his own version was made for chanting to a melody used by Lora McCall for the first line of her setting of Lambden’s translation. This indicates that a chanted English setting of Lambden’s translation existed by 1996.

The text in English

There is no authorised translation. The two provisional translations are by living or recently living translators, so printing more than a short extract in a published lyric needs permission (see “For songwriting”).

Provisional translation by Stephen Lambden (1983, revised 1995). A literal version, first printed in Bahá’í Studies Bulletin 2:3 (December 1983), pp. 105–112, and revised in 1995. Lambden marks tentative readings with asterisks and adds notes on a number of lines, including 5, 7, 9, 11, 19 and 24. The poem opens:

“The Maid of Eternity came from the Exalted Paradise;” “Hallelujah, Hallelujah, Hallelujah, O Glad Tidings!”

(Lambden, provisional translation by Stephen Lambden (1995), verse 1 and refrain; bahai-library.com.)

The Maid, Lambden says, is “at times” the “celestial Self” of Bahá’u’lláh. In verse 7 she carries the “snow-white hand” of Moses and comes like his rod turned “dragon”. Lambden’s note says she thereby “manifests the signs of true prophethood”. The final paragraph says the “Wondrous Remembrance” has come so that the lovers of the Beloved, in tranquillity, “might be shaken with ecstasy and remember their divine and sacred homeland” (verse 25, the closing paragraph). Lambden adds that reciters who dislike the evangelical overtones of “Hallelujah” could use “Rejoice, Rejoice, Rejoice, O Glad Tidings!” instead.

Provisional translation by Sen McGlinn (1996). A verse version made to be chanted, with rhythm first, accuracy second and rhyme third. It adds words in square brackets where the rhythm needs them and explains departures in footnotes. It covers only verses 1–14 in the library copy; McGlinn’s own list of the Writings calls it “a partial poetic rendering”. It opens:

“The Maid eternal came from paradise on high,”

(McGlinn, provisional translation by Sen McGlinn (1996), verse 1.)

McGlinn reads some verses differently from Lambden. In verse 5 he takes the subject of “came” to be the Maid’s “bewitching projectiles” and not the Maid herself. In verse 6 he reads “the universe of death came”. In verse 14 he reads the “sabre” as falling on the neck of the Beloved from fidelity’s court, and in a long note he interprets verses 13–14 as Bahá’u’lláh’s offering of His human self. That interpretation is McGlinn’s own reading, not a statement of the Writings.

Machine draft. A machine-drafted provisional translation (Gemini 4 Argon, 2026-10-17), not reviewed by a translator, is kept in the library at `kb/library/provisional-translations/halih-halih-ya-bisharat.md`. It is not quoted here.

Warning about both versions. McGlinn’s list of Bahá’u’lláh’s Writings says of the Lambden version and McGlinn’s own: “Both have since proven to be very defective”, because they rest on the Ganj-i-Sháyigán and INBA manuscript readings, and it prefers a Persian text posted on Juan Cole’s web page with significant variants. That text was not checked for this page. Anyone preparing a lyric from either English version should treat it as a working draft.

Themes and images

The poem has one structure: each verse says who or what “came” (Persian ámad), and the refrain answers with joy. What comes is the Maid of Eternity (ḥúr-i-baqá), then a series of figures who stand for the same arrival:

  • The Maid and her beauty. Harp, song, a crimson goblet, musky tresses, ruby lips, eyebrows like daggers and lashes like arrows (verses 2–5). Lambden’s Lewis-inspired correction reads du sayf, “two daggers”, for the Ganj printing’s du ṭayf, “two phantoms”. The language is that of Persian love poetry, which Lambden connects to Bahá’u’lláh’s other Maid-of-Heaven works, the Qaṣídiy-i-Varqá’íyyih, Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb and Lawḥ-i-Ḥúríyyih.
  • Signs of earlier Prophets. Moses’ white hand and rod (verse 7), David’s voice and the spirit of Christ (verse 8), Mount Sinai (verse 10), Midian (verse 20), the letter from Sheba carried by the hoopoe (verse 15, Lambden: an allusion to the Queen of Sheba’s message to Solomon).
  • Places and birds. The Divine Lote-Tree, the Tree of Blessedness, the rose garden, the “Hijazi Falcon” with “Iraqi accents” (verse 17), the nightingale. Lewis explains that Hijazi and Iraqi were also names of musical modes, and suggests the pair may allude to the Báb as a Siyyid and to Bahá’u’lláh’s exile in Iraq. These are scholarly suggestions.
  • Sacrifice. Verses 13–14 turn to a lover who “came as a sacrifice in the Path of the Beloved” and a sword “upon the neck of the Beloved”. Lambden suggests these lines allude to Bahá’u’lláh’s sufferings and to the condition of many Bábís, with question marks.
  • Rejoicing and reunion. Souls and hearts are drawn to the Maid’s “reunion” and “Bestowal” (verse 24). The closing paragraph speaks of “wondrous new melodies” and of ecstasy.

What is not settled

  • Date. Late 1862 or early 1863 (Lambden), “1862-3?” (McGlinn’s list), spring 1863 (Bahaipedia, after Savi). No source gives a day.
  • Text. The Ganj-i-Sháyigán text is judged corrupt at several points by Lambden and Lewis, and McGlinn’s list judges both English versions defective. Disputed readings include verse 5 (sayf or ṭayf), verse 7 (sawdá, which the Ganj text reads as trade or profit and McGlinn reads as black and passion, his note 13), verse 19 (Lambden reads kaff-i vujdán, hand of ecstasy; Lewis proposes kaff u ṣalá, an invitation to a feast; the Ganj has kaff-i vaṣl) and verse 24 (Lambden calls the sense of kána rabb ‘alá uncertain).
  • Reading of the Maid. Lambden treats her as at times Bahá’u’lláh’s “celestial Self”. McGlinn reads verses 13–14 as self-sacrifice. These are interpretations, and the sources do not claim authority for them.
  • Who the lovers are. Lambden suggests the “lovers” in verses 11–12 are the Bábís; he marks this as possible, not certain.
  • Length. 24 verses and a closing paragraph in the printed text; “twenty-six” in Bahaipedia.
  • Use at Riḍván. Not stated in any source found.

For songwriting

Licence summary. The Persian is Bahá’u’lláh’s own text; the page that supplied the saved copy claims copyright over its own presentation, so credit Ganj-i-Sháyigán, pp. 33–35, and treat the licence as `unknown` until checked. Lambden’s and McGlinn’s English is `permission-needed` for anything beyond a short extract; their posting on bahai-library.com is not a licence to print them in a published lyric. A lyric should either contact the translators or make its own English from the Persian with a Persian-reading reviewer.

Images the poem supplies. A heavenly figure arriving with harp and song, a red cup, dancing, Moses’ white hand and rod, David’s voice, Sinai, a letter carried by a bird from Sheba, a falcon with an Iraqi accent, a nightingale in a rose garden, and a closing call to remember a homeland. Every verse after the first is a new arrival, which suits a cumulative or call-and-answer form.

A possible arc. Start with the arrival of the Maid and the refrain. Move through the signs of earlier Prophets, which the poem uses to say that what arrives fulfils what they brought. Pause at the verses of sacrifice (13–14), where the mood changes from delight to cost. Return to the refrain, then end with the poem’s own closing aim: ecstasy that makes the listener remember the homeland.

What a lyric must not claim:

  • That the poem has an authorised English translation, or that Lambden’s or McGlinn’s English is the text of the Writings.
  • That it was revealed at Riḍván, or is traditionally sung at Riḍván; no source here says so.
  • That the Maid is a woman, or that the poem is a love song to one. Lambden reads the Maid as a figure of revelation and, at times, of Bahá’u’lláh’s own spiritual self; McGlinn presents his reading of the sacrifice verses as his own.
  • That “Hallelujah” is Bahá’u’lláh’s word. The Persian is hálih, which Lambden glosses as “Rejoice!” or, “loosely”, “Hallelujah”.
  • Any of the contested readings (verse 5 “phantoms” versus “daggers”, verse 19, verse 24) as settled.
  • That the Baghdad companions sang this poem to a known tune; no source says so.
