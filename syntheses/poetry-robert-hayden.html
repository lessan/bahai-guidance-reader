Robert Hayden as a poet

Last updated 17 October 2026

Robert Earl Hayden (1913–1980) was an American poet and a Bahá’í from 1943. He wrote in English. He served as Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress in 1976–78, the post now called US Poet Laureate, and was the first African American appointed to it. Most of his poems are about American history, race, memory and art. A smaller group draws on the Bahá’í Faith, and the corpus discusses four of them: “Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Ridwan”, “‘From the Corpse Woodpiles, from the Ashes’”, “The Night-Blooming Cereus” and the sequence “Words in the Mourning Time”. This page is part of the series introduced in Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

Hayden is an ancillary poet. His poems are not scripture and carry no authority in the Faith (tier C). His poems are under copyright (Liveright and earlier publishers), so this page quotes only the short lines that critics in the corpus quote, and cites the critic’s file and line. The corpus contains criticism and biography, not the poems themselves.

The poet

Life. Buck writes that Hayden was born Asa Bundy Sheffey on 4 August 1913 in Paradise Valley, a Detroit neighbourhood, and was given at eighteen months to neighbours William and Sue Ellen Hayden, who raised him (`buck_robert_hayden.md` line 57). He was very nearsighted, and Buck says this turned him to reading and writing (line 61). He attended Detroit City College, worked for the Federal Writers’ Project, and took a master’s degree at the University of Michigan in 1944, studying with W. H. Auden (Greenberg, `greenberg_anb_robert_hayden.md` line 53). From 1946 he taught at Fisk University in Nashville. In 1969 he moved to the University of Michigan, where he taught until his death in Ann Arbor on 25 February 1980 (Greenberg, lines 53 and 59; Bahá’í Chronicles, `robert-hayden.md` lines 196–198 and 209).

Becoming a Bahá’í. Buck says that his wife Erma Morris, whom he married in 1940, became a Bahá’í soon after they moved to Ann Arbor in 1941, and that Hayden “joined the Bahá’ís in 1943, while still a graduate student” (`buck_robert_hayden.md` line 81). Williams, in the 1981 World Order tribute, says his decision “was neither hasty not easy”, and that he gave several reasons: progressive revelation, a Bahá’í World Order that could join religious thought and scientific discovery, and “most important, the belief in the transcendentalist principle of universal brotherhood” (`S2_V16_I01.md` lines 1802–1822). Greenberg adds a statement of Hayden’s from the late 1970s: “I find myself sustained in my attempts to be a poet . . . because I have the assurance of my faith that this is of spiritual value and it is a way of performing some kind of service” (`greenberg_anb_robert_hayden.md` line 56).

Service to the community. Williams says Hayden became poetry editor of World Order, the Bahá’í journal of the United States, in 1968 (`S2_V16_I01.md` line 2209). Bahá’í Chronicles, citing The Bahá’í World, volume 18, says he held the post for a decade (`robert-hayden.md` line 209).

Recognition and the dispute over labels. A Ballad of Remembrance won the Grand Prize for Poetry at the First World Festival of Negro Arts in Dakar in 1966 (Buck, `buck_robert_hayden.md` line 101). The same year, at a writers’ conference at Fisk, younger poets attacked him for refusing to call himself a “black poet” (Buck, line 109; Greenberg, line 56). Hayden’s position, first stated in 1948, was that poetry should not be confined to one ethnic identity. Buck says this position fitted what he calls Hayden’s “Bahá’í-inspired vision of racial harmony” (line 113). Herrmann gives the 1948 statement as ending “We believe in the oneness of mankind and the importance of the arts in the struggle for peace and unity” (`herrman_robert_hayden.md`, paragraph beginning “The position statement ended”). Friedlander quotes Hayden from a Bicentennial-year interview: “The Bahá’í Teachings assure us that America will be an instrument for peace in the future” (`friedlander_hayden_epic_community.md` line 236).

Books. The titles named in the sources are Heart-Shape in the Dust (1940), The Lion and the Archer (1948, with Myron O’Higgins), Figure of Time (1955), A Ballad of Remembrance (1962), Selected Poems (1966), Words in the Mourning Time (1970), The Night-Blooming Cereus (1972, London; Greenberg gives 1973), Angle of Ascent (1975) and American Journal (1978, enlarged 1982) (Buck, lines 188–194; Greenberg, lines 56 and 59). Frederick Glaysher edited the Collected Prose (1984) and Collected Poems (Greenberg, line 82).

Critical studies in the corpus. The corpus holds several studies of his Bahá’í themes. Gerald Parks’s “The Bahá’í Muse: Religion in Robert Hayden’s Poetry” (World Order 16:1, Fall 1981) is the fullest, and this page relies on it. For others the corpus has only the abstract: Hatcher, “Racial Identity and the Patterns of Consolation in the Poetry of Robert Hayden” (Journal of Bahá’í Studies 3:2, 1990); Boyles, “The Angle of Ascent” (Journal of Bahá’í Studies 4:4, 1992); Herrmann, Hidden Meanings in the Poetry of Robert Hayden (2012); and the 1975 interview with Douglas Ruhe edited by Hatcher (Journal of Bahá’í Studies 24:3–4, 2014). Where this page gives a reading, it attributes it to the critic who made it. These are scholars’ readings and not authoritative interpretations of the Faith.

What the corpus says of the Faith poems

All four poems below appear in Angle of Ascent (Liveright, 1975) in Parks’s page references, except the sequence “Words in the Mourning Time”, which Parks cites from the 1970 book (pp. 41–51). None of the four is quoted in full anywhere in the corpus. The extracts are those Parks and Lester print.

“Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Ridwan”

Buck says the poem first appeared as “The Prophet” in Figure of Time (1955) and was later retitled; he calls it Hayden’s “purest and fullest testimony of faith” (`buck_robert_hayden.md` line 97). Parks places it among the poems in which the religious element is explicit, “almost propaganda pieces, or would be if they were not fine poems” (`S2_V16_I01.md` line 2580). He gives it as Angle of Ascent p. 117.

The title names the Garden of Ridván. Parks’s summary of the Faith says that in 1863 Bahá’u’lláh announced to the followers of the Báb that He was the chosen Manifestation of God for the age (line 1825). Parks quotes these lines:

  • “The midnight air is forested / with presences that shelter Him” (line 2982)
  • “The auroral darkness which is God” (line 2984)
  • “Eternal exile whose return / epiphanies repeatedly / foretell.” (line 2988)
  • “Within the rock the undiscovered suns / release their light.” (line 3077)

Parks reads the poem as an expression of “loving belief”, and says that “In no poem is the Bahá’í Faith treated with anything but loving belief” (lines 2995–2996). The phrase “auroral darkness” is also the title of John Hatcher’s book on Hayden, From the Auroral Darkness (1984), listed in Greenberg’s bibliography (line 82).

Related Writings: for the declaration in the Garden of Ridván see Baghdad and the Bábí gatherings.

“‘From the Corpse Woodpiles, from the Ashes’”

Parks lists it with the explicit Bahá’í poems (Angle of Ascent p. 116). He quotes two fragments, which he introduces as expressing “this same awe” about Bahá’u’lláh: “He, who is man beatified / And Godly mystery” and “His pain / our anguish and our anodyne” (lines 3062–3071). Parks does not say which events the title refers to, and the extracts do not name them. The title phrase suggests the destruction of the Second World War, but no source in the corpus says so.

“The Night-Blooming Cereus”

Parks calls it the poem that “most completely states Hayden’s attitude to the religion he has adopted”, though it has no overt reference to the Faith (line 3026). He reads it as a poem about the unfolding of the Faith, likened to the opening of the cereus flower, which blooms at night. He says two people, perhaps the poet and his wife, wait for the bud to open. One is unsettled by it and the other is calm, and both are united in awe. He quotes:

  • “packed / tight with its miracle” (the bud)
  • “lunar presence” and “moonlight petals” (the flower)
  • “foredoomed, already dying” (the bloom)
  • “older than human / cries, ancient as prayers” (its age)
  • “We spoke / in whispers when / we spoke / at all” (the last stance)

(lines 3039–3060). Parks reads “foredoomed, already dying” as a reference to the Bahá’í teaching that every religion starts to decline once established and is eventually replaced by another, and he reads the lunar images as a reminiscence of the “Full Moon” that shone on Bahá’u’lláh. He reads the night as perhaps an allusion to the ignorance of nineteenth-century Iran, and marks that point with a question mark. These are Parks’s readings, offered tentatively. The poem names Osiris, Krishna and Tezcátlipóca (Parks, line 3058), so it places the Faith beside other religions, as the Bahá’í teaching of progressive revelation does.

“Words in the Mourning Time”

This is a sequence of poems. Williams says the book of that title (1970) was a response to the confrontation at Fisk with the black militants, an affirmation of his humanism and a rejection of what Hayden saw as evil (`S2_V16_I01.md` line 2225). Parks cites the sequence at pp. 41–51 of the 1970 book and says its religious content is implicit, not stated outright (line 2583). Williams, Parks and Julius Lester (Hayden’s former student) each quote lines from the ninth part of the sequence. Lester quotes them to show what Hayden’s religion gave him:

  • “We must go on struggling to be human, / though monsters of abstraction / police and threaten us.” (Williams, line 2235; Lester, line 3647)
  • “but man / permitted to be man.” (Parks, line 2739; Lester, line 3658)

Parks connects another passage of the sequence to Shoghi Effendi. Hayden writes that the “disastrous quest / for meaning” of history is “process, major means whereby, / oh dreadfully, our humanness must be achieved”. Parks says a reader who has not read Shoghi Effendi’s The Promised Day Is Come may not follow this. His summary of the idea is that the world’s suffering is necessary to give birth to a new world order (lines 2722–2730). This is Parks’s paraphrase. The passages of The Promised Day Is Come that speak of this are not quoted by him or on this page.

Other poems the sources mention

Parks also names “Dawnbreaker” (p. 80), “and all the atoms cry aloud” (pp. 61–62) and “Full Moon” (p. 79, first published in Selected Poems, p. 15) among the explicit Bahá’í poems (lines 2580, 2957–2966), and “Stars” as “clearly based on Bahá’í imagery (especially the Nine-Pointed Star)”. From “and all the atoms cry aloud” he quotes “toward Him our history in its disastrous quest / for meaning is impelled” (line 2724). From “Full Moon” he quotes “And spread its radiance on the exile’s path / of Him Who was The Glorious One” (lines 2959–2960). In “The Broken Dark”, a hospital poem about doubt, Parks says it ends with a prayer: “Alláh’u’Abhá. O Healing Spirit, / Thy nearness our forgiving cure.” (line 2830). He suggests that the poet may be recalling Bahá’u’lláh in the Síyáh-Chál. That is his guess. The corpus does not say what Hayden intended.

Themes and images

The critics in the corpus identify these recurring ideas, each with the reader’s name attached.

Light and darkness. Parks says the word “dark” in Hayden usually means darkness of soul, and “star” is a spiritual symbol (lines 2707–2716). In the Ridwan lines above, the darkness is called “auroral”, the dark before dawn. In the Cereus poem the light is moonlight. Williams says that in Hayden’s later volume he wrote of the past as “semi-dark” (line 2250).

Exile. The phrases “the exile’s path” and “Eternal exile whose return” refer to Bahá’u’lláh’s banishments (Parks’s summary, line 1825). Hayden also wrote of alienation in general. Mullen quotes “Alien, at home, as always / everywhere I roamed” from “Kodachromes of the Island” and relates it to his sense of estrangement (`mullen_hayden_collected_poems.md` line 543). Parks quotes the same lines as his search for a theme (line 2746).

Suffering that leads to a human world. Parks and Lester both stress the passage in “Words in the Mourning Time” that hopes for “a human world where godliness / is possible”. Lester says Hayden’s religion “helped him maintain his center” under attack (`S2_V16_I01.md` line 3632 onwards).

Unity of religions. Parks says Hayden is able to “lend it credence” only in a universal faith, and that this allows him to treat other religions with fondness and irony without condemning them (lines 3000–3011). Parks cites “Akhenaten”, which ends “Aten Jahveh Allah God”.

Craft. The critics agree that he worked with great care over each line. Lester says the lines are “so tightly compressed as to be unbearable” (lines 3490–3491). Buck lists coined words such as “soulscape” and “snowlight” (`buck_robert_hayden.md` line 141).

What is not settled

  • Date of the Ridván poem. Buck says “The Prophet” in Figure of Time (1955) is the same poem as “Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Ridvan” (his spelling) in later books. Parks gives only the Angle of Ascent page. The corpus does not say when it was first written, or how much it changed.
  • Dates of books. Buck dates The Night-Blooming Cereus to 1972, Greenberg to 1973. Parks cites the 1972 London edition.
  • Years at Fisk. Greenberg says twenty-three years at Fisk (1946–1969). Buck says he stayed “over two decades” but also places his move to Michigan one year after his promotion to full professor, which he dates 1961, so the sources differ.
  • Parks’s readings. The Cereus and “Corpse Woodpiles” readings are interpretations by a critic, who was a classicist teaching translation. They are not statements by Hayden. Parks marks several as guesses (“perhaps”, “an allusion to ... particularly in nineteenth-century Iran?”).
  • The poems’ full texts. The corpus has no full text of any of the four poems. A lyric project would need the books (Angle of Ascent; Words in the Mourning Time) and permission from the publisher.
  • Critical disagreement. Mullen, writing for a literary journal, doubts the label “transracial” for Hayden and says he does not “so much transcend race” as use it as “a metaphor for the opacity of the self” (`mullen_hayden_collected_poems.md` lines 531–535). Hatcher’s and Parks’s Bahá’í-centred readings and Mullen’s are different views.

For songwriting

These are notes, not lyrics. Every phrase below is exact, taken from the critic named, and licence `permission-needed`.

Shortest singable phrases

  • “Thy nearness our forgiving cure” (“The Broken Dark”, Parks line 2830). A prayer line.
  • “permitted to be man” (“Words in the Mourning Time”, Parks line 2739). A fragment; the full sentence is “a human world where godliness is possible and man is neither ... but man permitted to be man”. A lyric using it should not reproduce the slurs in the middle of that sentence.
  • “Within the rock the undiscovered suns / release their light” (“Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Ridwan”, Parks line 3077).
  • “The midnight air is forested / with presences that shelter Him” (the same poem, Parks line 2982).
  • “packed / tight with its miracle” (“The Night-Blooming Cereus”, Parks line 3039).
  • “We must go on struggling to be human” (“Words in the Mourning Time”, Lester line 3647).

Concrete images in the sources

  • A night garden with unseen presences; a rock holding hidden suns.
  • A bud waiting to open at night; moonlight petals; whispers.
  • A hospital room with shadows on the wall at night; a prayer at the end.
  • A moon that lights the path of an exile.

A possible arc. Waiting in darkness, then a sign of something larger than the watcher (the bud, the hidden suns), then quiet or prayer. Parks reads the Cereus poem and “The Broken Dark” in this order: unsettled, then awe, or doubt, then prayer.

What a lyric must not claim

  • It must not present Hayden’s lines as scripture, or as Bahá’u’lláh’s words. The Ridván poem is a poet’s tribute, not a record of the Declaration.
  • It must not present Parks’s interpretations (the cereus as the Faith’s unfolding, the night as nineteenth-century Iran) as Hayden’s stated meaning. They are one critic’s readings.
  • It must not use Hayden’s lines beyond the extracts without permission, and it must not paraphrase his poems so closely that the result is a derivative of them.
  • It must not treat Hayden’s remark that the Bahá’í teachings assure us America “will be an instrument for peace” as a statement of Bahá’í doctrine. It is an answer in an interview. For the teachings themselves see Promises of the future.
