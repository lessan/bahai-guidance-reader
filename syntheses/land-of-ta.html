The Land of Ṭá: Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶91–93, Ṭihrán, and the Promise for Iran

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page covers

In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, between warnings to the rulers of America, Constantinople and Berlin, Bahá’u’lláh addresses His native city in three short paragraphs (¶91–93). He tells it not to grieve. He says God has chosen it to be “the source of the joy of all mankind”. He says that God will, “if it be His Will”, bless its throne with a just ruler who will “gather together the flock of God which the wolves have scattered”. He says the city is the “dayspring” of God’s light because the Manifestation of God was born in it. And He says that its “agitation” will be “transmuted into peace and quiet calm”.

This page does six things.

  1. It gives the full text of ¶91–93, with the official notes and a reading of each phrase.
  2. It shows how Bahá’u’lláh Himself later quoted and handled the passage.
  3. It describes Ṭihrán as the birthplace of Bahá’u’lláh and the place of the Síyáh-Chál, with His own words and Shoghi Effendi’s account.
  4. It collects what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice have written about Iran’s future.
  5. It sets out what the authoritative texts say about the “just ruler”, and what they leave open.
  6. In a separate section, clearly marked as description and not authority, it reports Iran’s situation as of mid-October 2026, with dated and cited sources.

The Writings are quoted verbatim from the texts held in this knowledge base. Where a passage reaches us second-hand (for example, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quoted in a letter of Shoghi Effendi or of the Universal House of Justice), the page says so. The section on the present (§8) is drawn from news and human-rights sources and is never offered as a Bahá’í teaching.

1. The text

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas was revealed in ‘Akká “around 1873”, about twenty years after Bahá’u’lláh received the first intimation of His Revelation in the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán (introduction to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, [source], line 68). The passage sits in the part of the Book where Bahá’u’lláh turns from laws to address kings, peoples and places. The paragraphs just before it are addressed to “O Rulers of America” (¶88), “O people of Constantinople” (¶89) and “O banks of the Rhine” (¶90).

91 “Let nothing grieve thee, O Land of Ṭá, for God hath chosen thee to be the source of the joy of all mankind. He shall, if it be His Will, bless thy throne with one who will rule with justice, who will gather together the flock of God which the wolves have scattered. Such a ruler will, with joy and gladness, turn his face towards, and extend his favors unto, the people of Bahá. He indeed is accounted in the sight of God as a jewel among men. Upon him rest forever the glory of God and the glory of all that dwell in the kingdom of His revelation.”

92 “Rejoice with great joy, for God hath made thee “the dayspring of His light,” inasmuch as within thee was born the Manifestation of His Glory. Be thou glad for this name that hath been conferred upon thee—a name through which the daystar of grace hath shed its splendor, through which both earth and heaven have been illumined.”

93 “Erelong will the state of affairs within thee be changed, and the reins of power fall into the hands of the people. Verily, thy Lord is the All-Knowing. His authority embraceth all things. Rest thou assured in the gracious favor of thy Lord. The eye of His loving-kindness shall everlastingly be directed towards thee. The day is approaching when thy agitation will have been transmuted into peace and quiet calm. Thus hath it been decreed in the wondrous Book.”

— Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶91–93 ([source], lines 292–296)

The next two paragraphs are the nearest context:

94 “O Land of Khá! We hear from thee the voice of heroes, raised in glorification of thy Lord, the All-Possessing, the Most Exalted. …”

95 “None must contend with those who wield authority over the people; leave unto them that which is theirs, and direct your attention to men’s hearts.”

(¶94 is addressed to Khurásán; ¶95 is quoted in full in §6 because it bears on how Bahá’ís hold the promise.)

The official notes

The authorised edition has two notes on these paragraphs (notes 122 and 123, `kitab-i-aqdas.md`, lines 2705–2711):

““Ṭá” is the initial letter of Ṭihrán, the capital of Iran. Bahá’u’lláh has often chosen to represent certain place names by reference to their initial letter. According to the abjad system of reckoning, the numerical value of Ṭá is nine, which equals the numerical value of the name Bahá.”

“This is a reference to the birth of Bahá’u’lláh in Ṭihrán on 12 November 1817.”

So the notes establish three facts: “Land of Ṭá” means Ṭihrán; the letter Ṭá has the same numerical value (nine) as the name Bahá; and “within thee was born the Manifestation of His Glory” refers to Bahá’u’lláh’s birth there. The notes say nothing about the ruler or the “reins of power”.

A reading, phrase by phrase

The readings below stay with what the words say and with what authoritative texts later say about them. Anything further is marked.

“Let nothing grieve thee.” The passage opens by addressing grief. Bahá’u’lláh does not say that nothing will go wrong. He says nothing should grieve the city, and gives reasons: God has chosen it, a ruler will come, the Manifestation was born in it, and its state will change.

“God hath chosen thee to be the source of the joy of all mankind.” The ground of the joy is that Ṭihrán is the birthplace of the Manifestation (¶92). Shoghi Effendi lists this phrase among the titles Bahá’u’lláh gave the city: “the Mother of the world,” the “Day-spring of Light,” the “Dawning-Place of the signs of the Lord,” the “Source of the joy of all mankind” (God Passes By, `god-passes-by.md`, line 405). He adds that the Báb had called the city the “Holy Land”.

“He shall, if it be His Will, bless thy throne with one who will rule with justice.” The promise is conditioned on God’s Will, and no person, date or dynasty is named. The text speaks of “one”, not of a line of rulers. It uses “throne”, so the language is that of kingship; §6 returns to that.

“who will gather together the flock of God which the wolves have scattered.” The ruler’s work is to gather. The text does not say who the flock is beyond the next sentence, which says the ruler will turn “towards, and extend his favors unto, the people of Bahá.” The scattering is attributed to “wolves”. Bahá’u’lláh does not name them in this paragraph.

“a jewel among men.” The ruler’s standing “in the sight of God” is stated; no outward title is.

“the dayspring of His light.” The city’s title rests on one fact: “within thee was born the Manifestation of His Glory”. Taherzadeh, whose commentary is not authoritative but is a careful reading of the notes, adds that the city’s initial letter has the numerical value of Bahá, the Greatest Name, and “this in the sight of God is a great distinction” (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, pp. 47–48, [source]).

“Erelong will the state of affairs within thee be changed, and the reins of power fall into the hands of the people.” This is a separate statement from the promise of a ruler in ¶91. It says that a change of the state of affairs is near (“erelong”) and that power will pass to “the people”. The authoritative texts reviewed for this page do not explain how this sentence relates to the “throne” and “ruler” of ¶91 (see §6).

“The day is approaching when thy agitation will have been transmuted into peace and quiet calm.” The word “agitation” is Bahá’u’lláh’s. The promise is of peace and calm, and the verb “transmuted” says the agitation itself is changed into peace.

2. How Bahá’u’lláh Himself handled the passage

In the Gleanings

Shoghi Effendi included the whole passage in Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh as section LVI. It reads the same as the Aqdas text, with the city named in brackets: “LET nothing grieve thee, O Land of Ṭa (Ṭihrán)…” (`gleanings-writings-bahaullah.md`, line 578). The two sections before it speak to the same city (§3).

In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf

In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, Bahá’u’lláh quotes ¶91 and then adds something:

“In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas the following hath been revealed: “O Land of Tá (Ṭihrán)! Let nothing grieve thee, for God hath chosen thee to be the source of the joy of all mankind. He shall, if it be His Will, bless thy throne with one who will rule with justice, who will gather together the flock of God which the wolves have scattered. Such a ruler will, with joy and gladness, turn his face towards, and extend his favors unto, the people of Bahá. He indeed is accounted in the sight of God, as a jewel among men. Upon him rest forever the glory of God, and the glory of all that dwell in the kingdom of His revelation.” These verses were revealed previously. Now, however, the following verse hath been sent down: “O God, my God! Bahá beseecheth Thee and imploreth Thee, by the lights of Thy countenance and the billows of the ocean of Thy Revelation, and the effulgent splendors of the Sun of Thine utterance, to aid the Sháh to be fair and equitable. If it be Thy wish, bless Thou, through him, the throne of authority and sovereignty. Potent art Thou to do what pleaseth Thee. There is none other God but Thee, Who hearest, Who art ready to answer.” “Rejoice with great joy, O Land of Tá (Ṭihrán), for God hath made thee the dayspring of His light…“ — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf (`epistle-son-wolf.md`, line 440)

Three things follow from this text, and they are all that it supports.

  • Bahá’u’lláh Himself returned to the promise late in His ministry, after the Aqdas, and quoted it again.
  • He added a prayer, in His own name (”Bahá beseecheth Thee“), that God aid the reigning Sháh ”to be fair and equitable“ and, ”if it be Thy wish“, bless the throne through him. That is a prayer with a condition, not a statement that the Sháh was or would be the promised ruler.
  • Bahá’u’lláh calls the earlier verses and the prayer separate revelations (”These verses were revealed previously. Now, however, the following verse hath been sent down“). The passage does not say the promise was fulfilled, in whole or in part, by this Sháh. (Náṣiri’d-Dín Sháh was assassinated in 1896; Shoghi Effendi’s account of the fall of the Qájár dynasty is in The Promised Day Is Come, `promised-day-come.md`, lines 387–397.)

In the Tablet of the World

In the Lawḥ-i-Dunyá (Tablet of the World), Bahá’u’lláh refers back to the Aqdas passage:

”Referring to the land of Tá [Ṭihrán] We have revealed in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas that which will admonish mankind. They that perpetrate tyranny in the world have usurped the rights of the peoples and kindreds of the earth and are sedulously pursuing their selfish inclinations.“ — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh Revealed after the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (`tablets-bahaullah.md`, line 500)

The remark shows how Bahá’u’lláh Himself placed the passage. It is not only a consolation to the city: He says the Aqdas contains ”that which will admonish mankind“, and the sentence that follows connects it to tyranny and to those who have ”usurped the rights of the peoples and kindreds of the earth.“

In Shoghi Effendi

Shoghi Effendi refers to the passage in three places in the books held here.

  • In God Passes By, in his survey of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, he lists among its contents ”the words of cheer and comfort He addresses to His native city, assuring her that God had chosen her to be ‘the source of the joy of all mankind’“ (`god-passes-by.md`, line 953).
  • In The Advent of Divine Justice (1938) he writes of ”a country whose capital has been hailed by Bahá’u’lláh as the “mother of the world” and the “dayspring of the joy of mankind””, while “a civil authority, as yet undivorced officially from the paralyzing influences of an antiquated, a fanatical, and outrageously corrupt clergy, pursues relentlessly its campaign of repression against the adherents of a Faith” (`advent-divine-justice.md`, line 39). He names the epithets and places them beside the persecution.
  • In The Promised Day Is Come, he quotes ¶91 and gives the most direct authoritative reading of its ruler (§6).

3. Ṭihrán: birthplace, prison and “mother of the world”

Birth

Bahá’u’lláh was born in Ṭihrán on 12 November 1817 (note 123 to the Aqdas, quoted in §1).

The Síyáh-Chál

In August 1852, after an attempt on the life of Náṣiri’d-Dín Sháh by a few Bábís, Bahá’u’lláh was arrested at Níyávarán and taken to Ṭihrán. Shoghi Effendi describes the journey and the prison:

“Delivered into the hands of His enemies, this much-feared, bitterly arraigned and illustrious Exponent of a perpetually hounded Faith was now made to taste of the cup which He Who had been its recognized Leader had drained to the dregs. From Níyávarán He was conducted “on foot and in chains, with bared head and bare feet,” exposed to the fierce rays of the midsummer sun, to the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán. On the way He several times was stripped of His outer garments, was overwhelmed with ridicule, and pelted with stones.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By (`god-passes-by.md`, line 289)

The same passage quotes Bahá’u’lláh’s account from the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf:

“We were consigned for four months to a place foul beyond comparison.… Upon Our arrival We were first conducted along a pitch-black corridor, from whence We descended three steep flights of stairs to the place of confinement assigned to Us. The dungeon was wrapped in thick darkness, and Our fellow-prisoners numbered nearly one hundred and fifty souls: thieves, assassins and highwaymen. Though crowded, it had no other outlet than the passage by which We entered.” — quoted in God Passes By (`god-passes-by.md`, line 289)

Shoghi Effendi adds that “Bahá’u’lláh’s feet were placed in stocks, and around His neck were fastened the Qarih-Kahar chains of such galling weight that their mark remained imprinted upon His body all the days of His life.” The prison “originally had served as a reservoir of water for one of the public baths of the capital.”

The Revelation begins there

Bahá’u’lláh describes what happened in that prison in His own words:

“During the days I lay in the prison of Ṭihrán, though the galling weight of the chains and the stench-filled air allowed Me but little sleep, still in those infrequent moments of slumber I felt as if something flowed from the crown of My head over My breast, even as a mighty torrent that precipitateth itself upon the earth from the summit of a lofty mountain. Every limb of My body would, as a result, be set afire. At such moments My tongue recited what no man could bear to hear.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf (`epistle-son-wolf.md`, line 80)

And, in the Súriy-i-Haykal:

“While engulfed in tribulations I heard a most wondrous, a most sweet voice, calling above My head. Turning My face, I beheld a Maiden—the embodiment of the remembrance of the name of My Lord—suspended in the air before Me. So rejoiced was she in her very soul that her countenance shone with the ornament of the good pleasure of God, and her cheeks glowed with the brightness of the All-Merciful.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, Súriy-i-Haykal ¶6 (`summons-lord-hosts.md`, line 58)

Shoghi Effendi writes that such a Revelation “was, as already noted, born amidst the darkness of a subterranean dungeon in Ṭihrán” (God Passes By, `god-passes-by.md`, line 397). He sets the sequence of places out in one passage: “The first dawnings of that Light of peerless splendor had, as already described, broken in the city of Shíráz. The rim of that Orb had now appeared above the horizon of the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán.” (line 405).

Two further details from Shoghi Effendi’s narrative, which a songwriter or reader may want. First, he notes that the imprisonment lasted “no less than four months”, and that Bahá’u’lláh “set out, a month later, for Baghdád, on the first stage of a memorable and life-long exile” (`god-passes-by.md`, line 419). Second, he records that the Grand Vizir’s envoy, Ḥájí ‘Alí, was so angered by the sight of the prisoner that he offered his own mantle, “a request which He emphatically refused, preferring to appear, attired in the garb of a prisoner, before the members of the Imperial government.” (line 421). Of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s childhood in the same events, Shoghi Effendi writes that He had been “seared with the ineffaceable vision of a Father, haggard, dishevelled, freighted with chains, on the occasion of a visit, as a boy of nine, to the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán”, and that “a mob of street urchins … pelted Him with stones” (line 1087).

Taherzadeh notes that the Rashḥ-i-‘Amá was, as far as can be gathered, the only Tablet revealed in the land of Bahá’u’lláh’s birth (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, p. 46, `revelation_v1.md`). This is a historian’s statement, not an authoritative one.

Bahá’u’lláh’s address to the city from exile

In section LV of the Gleanings, Bahá’u’lláh speaks to the city and remembers those who died there:

“CALL thou to remembrance, O Land of Ṭa (Ṭihrán), the former days in which thy Lord had made thee the seat of His throne, and had enveloped thee with the effulgence of His glory. How vast the number of those sanctified beings, those symbols of certitude, who, in their great love for thee, have laid down their lives and sacrificed their all for thy sake! Joy be to thee, and blissfulness to them that inhabit thee. I testify that out of thee, as every discerning heart knoweth, proceedeth the living breath of Him Who is the Desire of the world. In thee the Unseen hath been revealed, and out of thee hath gone forth that which lay hid from the eyes of men. Which one of the multitude of thy sincere lovers shall We remember, whose blood hath been shed within thy gates, and whose dust is now concealed beneath thy soil? The sweet savors of God have unceasingly been wafted, and shall everlastingly continue to be wafted upon thee. Our Pen is moved to commemorate thee, and to extol the victims of tyranny, those men and women that sleep beneath thy dust.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, LV (`gleanings-writings-bahaullah.md`, line 567)

The next paragraph mentions “Our own sister”, who “returned to her God” in “a state of resignation”, and which only God has known (line 569). Taherzadeh identifies her as Sárih Khánum and says she died in Ṭihrán in about 1879 (`revelation_v1.md`, “The Sisters of Bahá’u’lláh”); this is the historian’s identification, not the text’s.

The same section then speaks of the future:

“O Land of Ṭa! Thou art still, through the grace of God, a center around which His beloved ones have gathered. … The glory of their station, however, is as yet undisclosed. The Hand of Divine power will, assuredly, lift up the veil, and expose to the sight of men that which shall cheer and lighten the eye of the world.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, LV (line 571)

Section LXIII gives the greeting He told a traveller to speak when the city first came into view:

“O THOU whose face is turned towards Me! As soon as thine eyes behold from afar My native city (Ṭihrán), stand thou and say: I am come to thee out of the Prison, O Land of Ṭa, with tidings from God, the Help in Peril, the Self-Subsisting. I announce unto thee, O mother of the world and fountain of light unto all its peoples, the tender mercies of thy Lord, and greet thee in the name of Him Who is the Eternal Truth, the Knower of things unseen. I testify that within thee He Who is the Hidden Name was revealed, and the Unseen Treasure uncovered.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, LXIII (line 649)

Section LXIV opens: “IT is Our wish to remember the Abode of supreme blissfulness (Ṭihrán), the holy and shining city” (line 656). Taherzadeh gathers further titles Bahá’u’lláh gave the city: “mother of the world”, “Day-spring of the Cause of God”, “fountain of His Revelation”, “holy and shining city”, “Abode of supreme blissfulness”, “land of resplendent glory”, and “source of the joy of all mankind” (vol. 1, pp. 46–47).

The other side of the city

Bahá’u’lláh’s writings about Ṭihrán are not only praise. In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf He writes:

“I was walking in the Land of Tá (Ṭihrán)—the dayspring of the signs of thy Lord—when lo, I heard the lamentation of the pulpits and the voice of their supplication unto God, blessed and glorified be He.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf (`epistle-son-wolf.md`, line 378)

Here the same city is called “the dayspring of the signs of thy Lord”, and the complaint is about what is preached from its pulpits. The praise and the lament are about one city and are in the same Tablets.

4. What the Writings and the Guardian say about Iran’s future

Bahá’u’lláh

Besides the passages in §2 and §3, the Gleanings LV passage (“The Hand of Divine power will, assuredly, lift up the veil…”) and the Aqdas ¶93 are the main statements. Shoghi Effendi’s Promised Day Is Come also gathers Bahá’u’lláh’s predictions about kings. These are in §6.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá

Two statements about Iran’s future reach us second-hand, in letters of the Guardian and the Universal House of Justice. The original Tablets are not named in either, and this page has not located them.

In a letter of Shoghi Effendi (translated from the Persian, in the compilation Fire and Light prepared by the Universal House of Justice in 1986; the letter is undated in the file), the Guardian quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá directly after quoting the Aqdas passage:

“Moreover, the following significant and soul-stirring words have been set down by the pen of the Centre of the Covenant: “Erelong will it be witnessed that the government of the native land of the Blessed Perfection will become the most honoured government of this world, and Írán will become the most prosperous of all lands. This indeed is a token of God’s bountiful favour, and verily in this is there a lesson unto every beholder.”” — Shoghi Effendi, quoting ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Fire and Light, Section III, letter III (`fire-and-light.md`, line 372)

In its message of 26 November 2003 to the Bahá’ís of Iran, the Universal House of Justice quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (the same message is in the compilation Messages from the Universal House of Justice 2001–2022, and its 12 January 2004 letter to the Iranian believers living outside Iran quotes the second paragraph again, saying the community in Iran “will increasingly be able to turn its attention to the role it is destined to play in the country’s regeneration”):

“The horizon of Persia hath been illumined with the light of the heavenly Orb. Erelong will the Daystar of the supernal realm shine so brightly as to raise that land even unto the ethereal heights and to cause it to shed its radiance over the whole earth. The imperishable glory of bygone generations shall once more be manifest in such wise as to dazzle and bewilder the eyes….

Iran shall become a focal centre of divine splendours. Her darksome soil will become luminous and her land will shine resplendent. Although now wanting in name and fame, she will become renowned throughout the world; although now deprived, she will attain her highest hopes and aspirations; although now destitute and despondent, she will obtain abundant grace, achieve distinction and find abiding honour.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, quoted by the Universal House of Justice, 26 November 2003 ([source], line 94; also [source], lines 6425–6440)

Shoghi Effendi

The Fire and Light compilation draws on Nár va Núr, a compilation prepared by the Universal House of Justice for the Bahá’ís of Iran (`fire-and-light.md`, note 1). Its extracts from the Guardian’s letters are translations from the Persian and are undated in the file. They are the longest body of the Guardian’s writing on Iran’s future that this knowledge base holds. In one letter he writes:

“However, the essential thing is patience, fortitude, courage, and audacity. The day is approaching when that country will have turned into a blissful paradise, when the wronged ones of Persia will have become the pride of the world and the leaders of mankind. And this is a promise that will not be belied.” — Shoghi Effendi, Fire and Light, Section III, letter III (`fire-and-light.md`, line 376)

The sentence just before it reads: “Such God-given promises and sublime glad-tidings that the Pen of the Most High and the divinely aided and inspired fingers of the Centre of the Covenant have inscribed will, according to the following blessed verse, be unquestionably fulfilled. How enthralling is His Word: “Whatsoever hath streamed forth from the Pen of the Most High hath either already occurred or shall assuredly come to pass. Not a single letter thereof will remain unrealized, for verily the fair-minded shall behold it established upon the throne of fulfilment.”” (line 374).

The same letter opens by quoting the Aqdas passage and says of it: “It well beseems you to direct your eager gaze to the glad-tidings that have been revealed by the Pen of the Most High and to let the shining prophecies that are contained in His Most Holy Book and in other Tablets illumine your expectant eyes. Can one imagine a pronouncement sweeter, an utterance and promise more perfect, more appropriate, and more glorious than these conclusive verses that have streamed forth from the repository of the Abhá Pen?” (line 372). The footnotes in the file identify the “land” and “country” in these letters as Írán and Ṭihrán.

Another letter in the same compilation links the community’s release to turmoil, which matters when reading it against the present (§8):

“The release of this innocent and wronged community from the bonds of captivity, and its deliverance from the clutches of the enemy and oppressor, cannot but be accompanied by general commotions and disturbances; likewise, the attainment by the people of Bahá to a position in which they will enjoy true honour, comfort, and tranquillity must inevitably encounter the hostility and resistance, the clamorous opposition and tumultuous protests of all those who harbour enmity and rancour towards them.” — Shoghi Effendi, Fire and Light, Section III, letter I (`fire-and-light.md`, line 348)

The letter goes on: “If, therefore, the troubled waters of the sea of adversity should grow yet more turbulent … then know unhesitatingly, and with unwavering conviction, that the hour of deliverance, the appointed time when the promises of old are to reach their glorious fulfilment, has drawn nigh”. That letter speaks of the situation at the time it was written. It does not mention any event after the Guardian’s ministry, and this page does not apply it to any present event.

In his message of 20 August 1955 on the persecution in Iran (Citadel of Faith), the Guardian called that crisis “a blessing in disguise, not a “calamity” but a “providence” of God”, and wrote that the publicity it brought “cannot but prove to be a prelude, however slow the process involved, to the emancipation of these same valiant sufferers from the galling fetters of an antiquated religious orthodoxy” (`citadel-faith.md`, lines 1619, 1636). He added that such an emancipation “cannot be confined to Bahá’u’lláh’s native land” and “will, in varying measure, have its repercussions in Islamic countries”. He says nothing there about a ruler, a date, or a form of government.

The Universal House of Justice

The Universal House of Justice has returned to Iran’s future in many letters to the Iranian community. Four are quoted here, all from `sources/payamha_iran/` (authorised translations from the Persian).

On 26 November 2003 it wrote, after the ‘Abdu’l-Bahá passage above: “Each time we visit the Holy Shrines you are in the forefront of our hearts and prayers. Your long night will end, and you will have the joy of witnessing with your own eyes the mighty structure your sacrifices have raised.” (`en_0123.md`, line 96.)

On 23 June 2009, when street protests had been violently put down, it wrote: “No matter what the turn of events, we are confident you will adhere firmly to the fundamental principle of our Faith that strictly prohibits any involvement in partisan political activity by individual Bahá’ís or by Bahá’í institutions. Yet you cannot remain aloof and insensitive to the suffering of your people.” And: “Keep alive in your hearts the feeling of confidence that the future of Iran holds bright promise, the certitude that the light of knowledge will inevitably dispel the clouds of ignorance, the conviction that concern for justice will protect the nation from falling prey to calumny, and the belief that love will ultimately conquer hatred and enmity.” (`en_0045.md`, line 22.) The same letter says the proper response to oppression “is neither to succumb in resignation nor to take on the characteristics of the oppressor.”

On 21 March 2018 it quoted the Guardian: “The people of Bahá, whether inside or outside Iran, venerate the birthplace of the Blessed Beauty and sacrifice their own interests and comfort, nay their very lives and substance, for the revival, glory, progress, and furtherance of the true welfare of that land.” It added “with complete confidence in the great and glorious future of Iran” (`en_0266.md`, line 22).

On 24 October 2018 it wrote of “a firm belief in the fulfilment of Divine Purpose regarding the brilliant future of Iran and its people,” and “A feeble candle is extinguished by a mere breeze, while the flame of love and faith that is ablaze in the hearts of His loved ones grows in intensity, light, and heat through the storms of persecution.” (`en_0275.md`.)

Its most recent letter held here, dated 27 February 2026, was addressed “To the steadfast and faithful followers of the Greatest Name in the Cradle of the Faith” on the eve of the Fast. It speaks of “this time of bitter suffering in the history of your land” and says the Bahá’ís of the world are “praying fervently to His Threshold, beseeching serenity and honour for the sacred land of Iran, the birthplace of Bahá’u’lláh, and entreating protection and health, well-being and comfort, for all its peoples.” (`en_0858.md`, line 22.) It was issued one day before the war described in §8 began, and it does not mention the war.

5. The persecution, in brief

The long record of the persecution of the Iranian Bahá’ís is set out in SituationOfBahaisInIran and bahai-iran-persecution; this page does not repeat it. Three points from those pages bear on the Land of Ṭá.

  • The persecution is as old as the Faith. Shoghi Effendi, writing in 1955, called the then crisis “tragically reminiscent of the tribulations experienced by the dawn-breakers of the Heroic Age of the Faith at the hour of its birth in that sorely tried, long-agitated land” (`citadel-faith.md`, line 1573).
  • The Universal House of Justice describes the Iranian Bahá’ís’ conduct under it as non-retaliatory. Its letter of 23 August 2022 says: “The Bahá’ís seek justice and long for fairness and equity, but never pursue retaliation and revenge. Their hearts are filled with pain, but wholly free of hatred and rancour.” Its Naw-Rúz 180 letter (21 March 2023) says of the community: “To poison, it responded with sweetest honey.” ([source], line 36).
  • The Guardian’s 1955 reading, quoted above, treats the persecution as a “providence” that carries the Faith’s name and history into the world, and the House has applied the same pattern in later decades.

So the promise in ¶91–93 was made to a city where Bahá’u’lláh was imprisoned and where Bahá’ís are still imprisoned, and the authoritative texts hold the two facts together.

6. The “just ruler”: what the texts say, and what they leave open

What Shoghi Effendi says

The one authoritative commentary on ¶91 in the books held here is in The Promised Day Is Come, in the section on the kings and rulers Bahá’u’lláh addressed. After quoting Bahá’u’lláh’s wish that a king arise “for the triumph of this wronged, this oppressed people”, and two predictions (in the Lawḥ-i-Ra’ís and the Riḍvánu’l-‘Adl) that God will raise up kings who aid His loved ones and “rule amongst men even as they rule their own selves”, the Guardian writes:

“In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas He visualizes in these words the elevation to the throne of His native city, “the Mother of the World” and “the Dayspring of Light,” of a king who will be adorned with the twin ornaments of justice and of devotion to His Faith: “Let nothing grieve thee, O Land of Tá, for God hath chosen thee to be the source of the joy of all mankind. He shall, if it be His will, bless thy throne with one who will rule with justice, who will gather together the flock of God which the wolves have scattered. …”” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come (`promised-day-come.md`, line 433)

From this passage alone, the following is established.

  1. Shoghi Effendi reads the passage as a prediction about a person who will be raised to a throne in Ṭihrán. He calls that person “a king”.
  2. He reads the “ruler” as marked by two qualities: justice, and “devotion to His Faith”. The second comes from the Aqdas text’s statement that the ruler will “turn his face towards, and extend his favors unto, the people of Bahá”.
  3. He places it with other predictions in which Bahá’u’lláh says God will raise up one or more kings to help His loved ones. In the Lawḥ-i-Ra’ís: “Erelong will God raise up from among the kings one who will aid His loved ones.” (`promised-day-come.md`, line 431).
  4. He keeps Bahá’u’lláh’s own condition (“if it be His will”) in the quotation.

What Bahá’u’lláh’s own text adds

  • The promise is conditional on God’s Will (¶91; repeated in the Epistle).
  • Bahá’u’lláh prayed that the Sháh of His day might be “fair and equitable” (Epistle, line 440). The text does not say that prayer was answered.
  • The passage also says “the reins of power” will “fall into the hands of the people” (¶93). The ruler promise and this statement are both His; neither is explained by the other in any authoritative text found for this page.

What is left open

The following are not stated in any authoritative text reviewed for this page. A reader should not fill them in.

  • Who the ruler is, what his office is called, and whether the word “throne” is literal or a figure for the seat of government. No person is named, and no authoritative text identifies one.
  • When. The texts say “erelong” and “the day is approaching” (¶93), and Shoghi Effendi says “the day is approaching” (Fire and Light). No year or period is given.
  • How the ruler’s arrival relates to the transfer of power to “the people” in ¶93, and to the institutions that exist or may exist in Iran.
  • Whether the promise concerns one individual or is a pattern that may be fulfilled in more than one way. The text says “one who will rule with justice”; the Guardian says “a king”. The Guardian’s other statements about Iran’s future (§4) speak of the country and the community, not of a ruler.
  • Whether any part has already been fulfilled. In the edition of Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era held here, Esslemont wrote after quoting the passage: “So far, Persia has only begun to emerge from the period of confusion foretold by Bahá’u’lláh, but already constitutional government has been started, and signs are not lacking that a brighter era is at hand.” (`bahaullah-new-era.md`, line 2731.) That is an author’s observation, written in a different era, and it is not an authoritative interpretation. No authoritative text found here states that any ruler has fulfilled ¶91.

How Bahá’ís hold the promise

The Writings are definite that what Bahá’u’lláh has promised will come to pass. In the letter quoted in §4, Shoghi Effendi writes that the promises of the Pen of the Most High and of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “will … be unquestionably fulfilled”, that “a promise that will not be belied” stands behind Iran’s future, and, in the verse he quotes, that “Not a single letter thereof will remain unrealized”. Within the text, the means and the timing are not stated. The Writings give the certainty and withhold the schedule.

What Bahá’ís are asked to do meanwhile

¶95, which follows the Land of Ṭá and Land of Khá passages directly, reads: “None must contend with those who wield authority over the people; leave unto them that which is theirs, and direct your attention to men’s hearts.”

The Universal House of Justice’s letter of 23 June 2009 (quoted in §4) applies this to Iran’s political turmoil: partisan political activity is strictly prohibited for individual Bahá’ís and for Bahá’í institutions, whatever the turn of events. The same letter asks them to stay close to the suffering of their compatriots and to hold a confident hope for Iran’s future.

So a Bahá’í reading of ¶91 does not give a program of political action, and does not endorse or oppose any person, party or movement. It gives a promise to hold and a way of conduct while holding it.

7. The Land of Khá, and a note on the surrounding paragraphs

¶94 addresses Khurásán (note 124: “a reference to the Iranian province of Khurásán and neighboring areas, which include the city of ‘Ishqábád (Ashkhabad)”) and says: “We hear from thee the voice of heroes, raised in glorification of thy Lord.” Shoghi Effendi quotes this too, as Bahá’u’lláh’s “prophecy that ‘the voice of the heroes of Khurásán’ will be raised in glorification of their Lord” (`god-passes-by.md`, line 953). The pairing of the two paragraphs means that the Aqdas names two places in Iran and the territories next to it in its promises: one for joy and justice, and one for the voice of heroes.

8. Iran in October 2026: description, not authority

This section describes events and conditions as reported by news and human-rights sources. It is current as of 13 October 2026 and will age quickly. It is not a Bahá’í teaching, and none of it is evidence for or against the prophecy discussed above. The Writings in §1–§7 say nothing about any event after the lifetime of the Guardian. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated. The page takes no position on the conduct or aims of any government, party or movement.

How the sources were chosen and their limits

The facts below were taken from pages that could be opened (Wikipedia articles on the war, the Islamabad Memorandum and the protests; an Al Jazeera report of 11 October 2026; a report of the Center for Human Rights in Iran; a Jurist report of UN experts’ findings) and, where noted, from search-result summaries of pages that could not be opened. Wikipedia is a secondary compilation. Figures for casualties are disputed and are given with their sources. Several outlets that surfaced in searches are partisan (for example, the National Council of Resistance of Iran), and nothing here rests on them alone.

8.1 The winter of 2025–26: protests

According to the Wikipedia article on the 2025–2026 Iranian protests, nationwide protests began on 28 December 2025 with merchants in Ṭihrán’s Grand Bazaar, in the setting of a collapsing currency (a record low of 1.5 million rials to the US dollar on 6 January 2026) and inflation of 42 per cent reported for December 2025. They spread to many cities. On 8–9 January 2026 the authorities cut internet and telephone services, and security forces used live ammunition. The protests were largely suppressed by about 16 January.

Death-toll estimates differ widely, and the sources are:

  • The Iranian government: 3,117 killed (announced by the Supreme National Security Council on 21 January 2026, per search summaries of the UK and Amnesty pages).
  • The UN Special Rapporteur Mai Sato (16 January 2026): more than 5,000 killed by security forces, and possibly as many as 20,000 on medical sources.
  • The rights group HRANA (as of 5 February, per Wikipedia): 7,015 deaths confirmed.
  • Other estimates cited in the press and compiled by Wikipedia range up to about 36,500 or more.

(Sources for these figures: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%932026_Iranian_protests>; the Mai Sato and Council figures from the search summary of Amnesty International’s page <https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/campaigns/2026/01/what-happened-at-the-protests-in-iran/> and a GOV.UK country bulletin, which I could not open.) A UN fact-finding mission reported in September 2026, as summarised by the Center for Human Rights in Iran, that Iranian authorities committed crimes against humanity in suppressing the January protests, and said the true figures for deaths and injuries were significantly higher than the government’s (<https://iranhumanrights.org/2026/09/un-iranian-authorities-committed-crimes-against-humanity-in-january-2026-suppression-of-nationwide-protests/>; the summary was read in a search listing, and the page was not opened). The Wikipedia article reports that President Pezeshkian apologised to the nation for the killings on 11 February 2026.

8.2 The war from 28 February 2026

According to the Wikipedia article on the 2026 Iran war (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war>), the United States and Israel began strikes across Iran on 28 February 2026 (the US operation was authorised late on 27 February US time). Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed in the opening strikes. Iran responded with missile and drone attacks on Israel, US bases and Gulf states, and blocked the Strait of Hormuz (from late March, per the article). Mojtaba Khamenei was named Supreme Leader on 8 March 2026 (per the protests article).

A ceasefire began on 8 April 2026. Talks in Islamabad on 11–12 April ended without agreement, and the US imposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports. On 17 June 2026 the presidents of the United States and Iran signed the Islamabad Memorandum, a 14-point framework that provided for ending operations, reopening the Strait of Hormuz, lifting the blockades, and a 60-day period to negotiate a final agreement (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamabad_Memorandum>). It began to unravel on 7 July 2026 when Iranian forces attacked commercial ships in the Strait, the US replied with airstrikes, and on 8 July President Trump said the deal was “over” while talks could continue. The 60-day deadline passed on 17 August 2026 without a final deal.

Casualty figures, as compiled by Wikipedia from the sources it names: Iranian government, 3,528 killed; HRANA, 3,684 killed; the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, more than 3,400 civilians killed. The US and Israeli governments claim larger numbers of Iranian military dead. The US lists 20–24 service members killed; Israel lists 40 soldiers and 31 civilians killed.

8.3 The state of play on 11 October 2026

Al Jazeera reported on 11 October 2026 (<https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/11/hormuz-attacks-intensify-as-efforts-to-end-war-on-iran-stall>) that mediated efforts to end the war were deadlocked and attacks around the Strait of Hormuz had increased:

  • At least 12 ships were attacked in the week ending 5 October, the highest weekly number since the war began.
  • The Revolutionary Guards’ navy said that on 10 October a supertanker struck a naval mine on an unauthorised route. US Central Command said a US fighter bombed the stern of a Panama-flagged cargo vessel in the Gulf of Oman to enforce the blockade; since it was reimposed in mid-July, CENTCOM said it had disabled four commercial vessels, turned around 135 ships and destroyed 10 tankers linked to Iran.
  • Crude transit through the Strait averaged 8.5 million barrels a day in the week ending 7 October, about 40 per cent below normal, per Kpler.
  • Messages continue through mediators. Iran wants a return to the June memorandum and US backing for a deal with Oman on routes through the Strait. US Vice President JD Vance said Iran must make a “meaningful” reduction in its enrichment capacity. The report says no breakthrough was expected before Israel’s election on 27 October or the US midterm elections on 3 November.

8.4 Inside Iran

Opposition-aligned outlets (for example the National Council of Resistance of Iran’s daily bulletins for 5–12 October 2026) report a falling currency, executions of people arrested in the January protests, and disagreement among officials over negotiations with the United States. I could not confirm these from a mainstream wire service in the time available, and they are not relied on here. Analysts also disagree about how firmly Mojtaba Khamenei controls the state; sources surfaced in searches include a CNN report of 21 April 2026 on his absence from public view and a Middle East Institute analysis, which I could not open. This page does not assess them.

8.5 The Bahá’ís of Iran in 2026

The Center for Human Rights in Iran reported on 19 May 2026 (<https://iranhumanrights.org/2026/05/irans-bahai-community-under-intensified-state-attack-amid-escalating-domestic-repression/>) that persecution of Bahá’ís had sharply increased since the January protests and the February war. It describes dozens of arrests and home raids across cities including Shiraz, Kerman, Mashhad, Yazd, Sari, Karaj, Isfahan, Semnan and Tabriz, with detainees held without charge or access to lawyers, reported torture and forced “confessions”, and seizure of property. It names about 50 individuals; its cases include the arrest of twelve Bahá’ís, five of them minors, at a birthday gathering in Mashhad on 22 January.

On 29 July 2026 (reported on 31 July), UN human-rights experts said about 57 Bahá’ís were detained or imprisoned in Iran and expressed alarm at reports of enforced disappearance, torture, coerced confessions broadcast on state media, and prosecution on national-security charges (<https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/un-experts-raise-concerns-over-irans-intensified-persecution-of-bahais-amid-the-us-iran-conflict/>). The Bahá’í International Community publishes regular bulletins with updated counts; its page on the current situation reports that the authorities have been using Bahá’ís as scapegoats for the protests and the conflict. The BIC’s own pages could not be opened by the research tool, so the exact current figure is not given here; readers should check the BIC’s bulletin archive (<https://www.bic.org/focus-areas/situation-iranian-bahais/current-situation>).

8.6 How this relates to the question “do we dare hope?”

The question has a specific origin: the hope some feel that the present conflict might lead to change, and eventually to the just ruler that Bahá’u’lláh promised. Four plain statements follow from the preceding sections.

  • The authoritative texts give the promise as certain and give no timetable or means (§6).
  • The authoritative texts do not mention the present conflict, and they do not say that any person or event now in view is the promised ruler or a step toward one. This page makes no such claim, and no source cited here does.
  • The Guardian wrote that the release of the Iranian Bahá’ís “cannot but be accompanied by general commotions and disturbances” (§4). That statement is about a past situation in his ministry. Whether it bears on events now is not something the text says.
  • The Universal House of Justice, at the moment when Iran’s political turmoil was before it in 2009, asked the believers to keep “the feeling of confidence that the future of Iran holds bright promise”, to keep out of partisan politics, and to be “a beacon of strength” and a source of hope and compassion to those around them. Its letter of 27 February 2026 prays for “serenity and honour for the sacred land of Iran” and for protection and well-being “for all its peoples”.

The hope the Writings describe rests on God’s promise and on the community’s conduct. It does not rest on any outcome of the war or on any political party, leader or movement.
