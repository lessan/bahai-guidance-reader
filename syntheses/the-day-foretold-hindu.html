The Day Foretold: The Hindu Promise of the Return of Krishna

Last updated 16 October 2026

This page is part of the series on the-day-foretold. It collects what the Bhagavad Gita and the Vishnu Purana say about a divine figure who comes again when righteousness declines, what Bahá’í texts say about how Bahá’u’lláh relates to that promise, and who has spoken of it in India. It is one of several sibling pages: the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-zoroastrian, the-day-foretold-buddhist, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi. See also promises-of-the-future.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each tradition. For the Hindus he writes:

“to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

Three kinds of source appear on this page and are kept apart: the tradition’s own scripture (in public-domain English translations); Bahá’í texts, labelled by who wrote them; and scholarship and history, labelled by author. A fulfilment is stated here only where an authoritative Bahá’í text states it.

1. The promise

Krishna’s words in the Bhagavad Gita, chapter 4

In the Gita, Arjuna asks how Krishna can say he taught an ancient teaching when Arjuna’s own birth is recent. Krishna answers in the passage known as Gita 4:5–8. The translation of Sir Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial (1885), renders it in verse and does not number the verses. Verses 5–6:

Manifold the renewals of my birth Have been, Arjuna! and of thy births, too! But mine I know, and thine thou knowest not, O Slayer of thy Foes! Albeit I be Unborn, undying, indestructible, The Lord of all things living; not the less--By Maya, by my magic which I stamp On floating Nature-forms, the primal vast--I come, and go, and come.

Verses 7–8:

When Righteousness Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take Visible shape, and move a man with men, Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, And setting Virtue on her seat again.

(Bhagavad Gita 4:5–8, Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, 1885)

The translation of Kashinath Trimbak Telang (1882) is in prose and stays closer to the Sanskrit. For verses 7–8 it reads:

“Whensoever, O descendant of Bharata! piety languishes, and impiety is in the ascendant, I create myself. I am born age after age, for the protection of the good, for the destruction of evil-doers, and the establishment of piety.”

(Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8, K. T. Telang, 1882)

Both translations say that Krishna takes birth from age to age when righteousness declines, to protect the good and restore righteousness.

The same lines in a Bahá’í document

One Common Faith is a document prepared under the supervision of the Universal House of Justice. It is not an authoritative interpretation. It quotes Arnold’s rendering as an early and clear expression of the idea that divine teaching returns from age to age:

“One of its earliest and clearest expressions occurs in the Bhagavad-Gita: “I come, and go, and come. When Righteousness declines, O Bharata! When Wickedness is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take visible shape, and move a man with men, succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, and setting Virtue on her seat again“ (One Common Faith, OCF 42)

In that paragraph the document uses the verses as a pattern of succeeding revelations. It does not name Bahá’u’lláh as their fulfilment.

Kalki in the Vishnu Purana

A second Hindu text describes a figure who comes at the end of an age. The Vishnu Purana, in H. H. Wilson’s translation (1840), says of the last part of the Kali age:

”Thus in the Kali age shall decay constantly proceed, until the human race approaches its annihilation.“

(Vishnu Purana IV.24, H. H. Wilson, 1840)

and then describes Kalki:

”When the practices taught by the Vedas and the institutes of law shall nearly have ceased, and the close of the Kali age shall be nigh, a portion of that divine being who exists of his own spiritual nature in the character of Brahma, and who is the beginning and the end, and who comprehends all things, shall descend upon earth: he will be born in the family of Vishnuyasas, an eminent Brahman of Sambhala village, as Kalki, endowed with the eight superhuman faculties.“

(Vishnu Purana IV.24, H. H. Wilson, 1840)

The passage continues: Kalki ”will then reestablish righteousness upon earth“, and the people of that time ”shall give birth to a race who shall follow the laws of the Krita age, or age of purity“. This is a different text from the Gita, with a different figure and a different setting. The two are connected in the sections below only where a named source connects them.

2. The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

What Shoghi Effendi writes

Shoghi Effendi’s two statements in God Passes By are the authoritative texts on this page. The first is the one quoted above, in the passage that names the fulfilment for each tradition in turn (GPB VI.11): ”to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna.“

The second is in the paragraph that follows, which states what earlier scriptures referred to when they spoke of Bahá’u’lláh. For the Hindu scripture Shoghi Effendi writes:

”To Him the Bhagavad-Gita of the Hindus had referred as the “Most Great Spirit,” the “Tenth Avatar,” the “Immaculate Manifestation of Krishna.”

(Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.15)

The three phrases are in quotation marks in the original. Shoghi Effendi attributes them to the Bhagavad-Gita. The text does not say which verses contain them (see section 6).

The Bahá’í International Community, 1992

Bahá’u’lláh (1992) is a statement of the Bahá’í International Community Office of Public Information. It repeats the formula in shorter form, listing what Bahá’u’lláh is for each tradition, including ”to Hinduism, the new incarnation of Krishna“ (BBIC 156). The statement is commentary, not an authoritative interpretation.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on Krishna

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks that mention Krishna are known from notes of the talks. In a talk recorded in Paris Talks, an Indian visitor said that his aim in life was to carry the message of Krishna to the world. From notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá:

”The Message of Krishna is the message of love. All God’s prophets have brought the message of love. None has ever thought that war and hate are good. Everyone agrees in saying that love and kindness are best.“

(Paris Talks, 9.2)

In a talk of 7 October 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Krishna is named among the founders of religions:

”Blessed souls—whether Moses, Jesus, Zoroaster, Krishna, Buddha, Confucius or Muḥammad—were the cause of the illumination of the world of humanity.“

(The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 109.11)

And in a talk of 5 July 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, He says that all the holy books announce one Day:

”In the divine Holy Books there are unmistakable prophecies giving the glad tidings of a certain Day in which the Promised One of all the Books would appear, a radiant dispensation be established, the banner of the Most Great Peace and conciliation be hoisted and the oneness of the world of humanity proclaimed.“

(The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.1)

In that talk He names the Torah, the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta and ”the books of Buddha“. The Hindu scriptures are not named in the sentence.

Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi

Four letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, collected in Compilation of Compilations vol. 1 (Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster), bear on Krishna. They are secretaries’ letters, not Shoghi Effendi’s own writings.

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (13 July 1938) places Krishna in the series of nine Prophets:

”The nine religions to which you have referred include both the Bábí and the Bahá’í Dispensations, Bahá’u’lláh being the ninth Prophet in the series. The other Prophets included are Zoroaster, Krishna, Moses, the Christ, Muhammad, Buddha, the Prophet of the Sabaeans Whose name is unrecorded, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh“

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (22 June 1943) states the general principle:

”In the Bahá’í teachings it states that all the Prophets have foretold a Promised One Who is Bahá’u’lláh.“

The same letter, and the other two quoted in section 6, also speak of the limits on what the Bahá’í teachings say about earlier scriptures.

3. Those who recognised it

This section comes from scholarship and history, not from the Bahá’í Writings.

History, from Shoghi Effendi. In God Passes By Shoghi Effendi records the growth of the Bahá’í community in India and Burma, ”now including among its members representatives of the Zoroastrian, the Islamic, the Hindu and the Buddhist Faiths, as well as members of the Sikh community“ (GPB XX.17). He adds that at Daidanaw Kalazoo, in the Hanthawaddy district of Burma, ”no less than eight hundred Bahá’ís resided“.

Mass teaching in Malwa, from scholarship. Susan Maneck, in Conversion Movements within Hindu Village Culture (1997), describes the mass teaching in Malwa, in what is now Madhya Pradesh, which began around 1960. The account that follows is hers, and William Garlington’s.

  • In 1960 Mrs. Mehrabani spent several days in a Bhil tribal village near Indore; 75 of the 200 villagers declared themselves believers by placing their thumbprints on enrollment cards.
  • At an open-air meeting at Sangimanda, in Shajapur, in January 1961, ”Bahá’í speakers announced that Bahá’u’lláh was the bhagavan kalkin“, which Maneck glosses as ”the tenth avatar of Vishnu whose return in Vaishnavite theology marks the end of the kali-yuga.“ At the end of the conference 200 villagers became Bahá’ís, and the Indian National Spiritual Assembly bought jeeps and sent teachers through rural Malwa.
  • In the first years most enrolments were from scheduled tribes and castes. When mass teaching began in the Gwalior area in 1962, Bahá’ís approached the leading castes first. Of 276 declared believers Garlington studied, 159 were from untouchable or unclean castes; the rest were caste Hindus, ”often Raiputs and Brahmans“. By 1974 there were 113,692 declared Bahá’ís in Malwa in 6,572 localities.
  • Maneck writes that by translating the Bahá’í concept of the Manifestation of God into the Hindu term avatar, Bahá’ís presented Bahá’u’lláh as Kalkin, the Vaishnavite figure expected at the end of the kali-yuga, and that they made frequent reference to the Bhagavad Gita.

Garlington, in Bahá’í Proselytization in Malwa, India, adds that the early Bahá’í settlers chose their city in order to teach among Hindus, and that the early Malwa believers had ”the distinctly Hindu orientation of its members“.

Bahá’í bhajans, from scholarship. Garlington, in Bahá’í Bhajans, records that some bhajans identify Bahá’u’lláh with the kalkin avatar. He gives this verse of a popular bhajan, which he cites in translation:

”Arise O children of India, the kalkin avatar has come; Vishnu’s avatar has come with the name Bahá’u’lláh Nowhere in the entire world can the influence of religion be seen. The wicked have obtained everything; the truthful have lost all. According to the Gita the time of Vishnu’s avatar has come...Arise!“

Garlington explains the choice of this figure by the mission among lower castes: the hope of a coming deliverer ”would make sense“ as a way to reach them. He also notes that Hindu pandits have not generally given Kalkin an important place in the tradition.

A short Bahá’í introduction. An introductory leaflet, Hinduism and the Bahá’í Faith (in the Warwick Leaflets series at bahai-library.com), states: ”Hindu literature looks towards the return of an avatar of God called Kalki. Bahá’ís believe that Bahá’u’lláh is that return“. Christopher Buck’s article A Unique Eschatological Interface lists Kalki among the figures Bahá’u’lláh has been ”heralded by his followers“ as. Both are statements of what Bahá’ís believe, not authoritative texts.

4. The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

The following is a description from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0), from its articles ”Bhajan“ and ”Kirtan“. It is a description of forms, not a source for lyrics.

Bhajan. ”Bhajan is an Indian term for any devotional song with a religious theme or spiritual ideas.“ It has ”no prescribed form, or set rules“ and is normally ”lyrical and based on melodic ragas.“ Bhajans are accompanied by percussion such as the tabla, dholak or a tambourine, and handheld small cymbals (kartals) keep the beat. They may be sung by one person but are more commonly sung together, in a temple, a home, in the open or beside a river. They are especially common in Vaishnavism, the tradition of devotion to avatars of Vishnu such as Krishna and Rama. Some are centuries old and anonymous; others are newly composed in a local language.

Kirtan. A call-and-response or antiphonal style, in which a lead singer sings a chant, a hymn or a name of God and the group repeats or answers it. The description lists the harmonium, veena, sitar and ektara (strings), the tabla, the mridanga or pakhawaj (drums), flute and the cymbals called karatalas. A performance may begin with Sanskrit mantras such as Om. Kirtan is sometimes called harinam, the chanting of the names of God. Wikipedia describes two forms in Vaishnavism: nama-kirtana, which extols the names of God, and lila-kirtana, which narrates the deity’s life and legends.

Garlington’s article shows that Bahá’í bhajans already exist in India.

5. What the sources do not settle

Kalki is not named in any authoritative text gathered. No text by Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi or the Universal House of Justice in the collected sources uses the word Kalki. Shoghi Effendi writes ”Tenth Avatar“ (GPB VI.15). The link between his ”Tenth Avatar“ and Kalki is made by scholars and believers: Maneck, Garlington, the Warwick leaflet and Buck, cited above, treat Kalki as the tenth avatar of Vishnu and as the figure Bahá’ís identify with Bahá’u’lláh. They are not authoritative interpretation. This page does not state the identification as a Bahá’í teaching; it states that these writers report it.

Which verses contain Shoghi Effendi’s three phrases is not identified. The sources gathered do not say which verses of the Bhagavad-Gita Shoghi Effendi means by ”Most Great Spirit,“ ”Tenth Avatar“ and ”Immaculate Manifestation of Krishna.“ The saved Arnold and Telang texts of chapter 4 were searched for each phrase and for ”avatar“, ”tenth“, ”immaculate“ and ”great spirit“; none appears in either. Those files hold chapter 4 only, so this finding does not extend to the rest of the Gita or to other translations. The page draws no conclusion from this beyond reporting it.

The Gita verses are not tied to Bahá’u’lláh by a Bahá’í text in these sources. One Common Faith quotes Gita 4 as a pattern of succeeding revelations without naming a fulfilment. Shoghi Effendi’s statements in GPB VI.11 and VI.15 are the authoritative texts that name one.

Statements on behalf of Shoghi Effendi about Krishna. Two letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi limit what the Teachings say:

”Your question concerning Brahma and Krishna: such matters, as no reference occurs to them in the Teachings, are left for students of history and religion to resolve and clarify.“

(a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 14 April 1941)

”We cannot be sure of the authenticity of the scriptures of Buddha and Krishna, so we certainly cannot draw any conclusions about virgin birth mentioned in them. There is no reference to this subject in our teachings, so the Guardian cannot pronounce an opinion.“

(a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 25 November 1950)

The first letter concerns questions about Brahma and Krishna in general; the second concerns the virgin birth. Neither concerns the promise of return. The letter of 22 June 1943, quoted above, goes further on scriptures in general:

”We cannot be sure of the authenticity, word for word, of any of the past Holy Scriptures except the Qur’án, as they were either not written down during the Prophet’s lifetime or have been changed in the course of time and the originals lost“

(a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 22 June 1943)

The same letter says that when Bahá’u’lláh or ‘Abdu’l-Bahá stated that Zoroaster foretold a Promised One’s coming, ”it is correct“. This page therefore quotes the Gita in public-domain translations as the tradition’s scripture, and quotes Shoghi Effendi as the Bahá’í statement of the fulfilment, without claiming that the two have been matched verse by verse.

Whether Hindu scholars or institutions accept the Bahá’í reading is not addressed by any source on this page. The page makes no claim either way.

6. For songwriting

These are notes, not lyrics.

Shortest exact phrases from the promise (public domain, Arnold 1885):

  • ”I come, and go, and come“ (Gita 4:5–6)
  • ”I rise, from age to age“ (Gita 4:7–8)
  • ”setting Virtue on her seat again“ (Gita 4:7–8)
  • ”move a man with men“ (Gita 4:7–8)

From Telang (1882, public domain): ”I am born age after age“ (Gita 4:7–8).

Shortest exact phrases from the Bahá’í texts (authorised translations from the Bahá’í Reference Library, © Bahá’í International Community; quote with credit and check the bahai.org terms before a commercial release):

  • ”the reincarnation of Krishna“ (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.11)
  • ”the “Most Great Spirit”” (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.15)
  • “the “Immaculate Manifestation of Krishna”” (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.15)
  • “The Message of Krishna is the message of love” (from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks 9.2)

Concrete images the sources supply. The charioteer who speaks to Arjuna (the dialogue setting is in Arnold’s chapter 4); a figure who “move[s] a man with men”; Virtue set back “on her seat”; a village meeting in the open air at Sangimanda in January 1961, with 200 villagers enrolling; thumbprints on enrollment cards for villagers who could not write; jeeps carrying teachers across Malwa; a bhajan with cymbals and a drum.

A possible emotional arc. Declining righteousness; a promise made in the past (“I come, and go, and come”); waiting through the ages; the promised one’s coming in a human form among people; the message of love; villagers gathering in the open to sing.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • That a Gita verse names Bahá’u’lláh, or that Shoghi Effendi named Kalki. He did not use that word in the texts gathered.
  • That Hindu teachers, scholars or institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.
  • That the Kalki of the Vishnu Purana (who arrives on a horse in Hindu tradition, as Garlington describes) and the Bahá’í account of Bahá’u’lláh are the same description. They differ in many details, and a lyric should not blend them.
  • That the Gita’s own words are Bahá’í scripture. They are quoted as the tradition’s promise.
  • Anything that sets Krishna against another Prophet.
