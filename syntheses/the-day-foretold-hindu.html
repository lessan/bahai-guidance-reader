The Day Foretold: The Hindu Promise of the Return of Krishna

Last updated 17 October 2026

This page is part of the series on the-day-foretold. It sets out what the Bhagavad Gita and the Vishnu Purana say about a divine figure who comes again when righteousness declines, what Bahá’í texts say about Bahá’u’lláh and that promise (including what the Universal House of Justice has said about the sentence in which Shoghi Effendi names the Gita), what Bahá’í authors have written, and who has spoken of it in India. The sibling pages are the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-zoroastrian, the-day-foretold-buddhist, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi. See also promises-of-the-future.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each tradition. For the Hindus he writes:

“to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

Three kinds of source appear on this page and are kept apart: the tradition’s own scripture (in public-domain English translations); Bahá’í texts, labelled by who wrote them; and commentary and history, labelled by author. A fulfilment is stated here as a Bahá’í teaching only where an authoritative Bahá’í text states it. Where an author goes further, the page names the author.

1. The promise

Krishna’s words in the Bhagavad Gita, chapter 4

In the Gita, Arjuna asks how Krishna can say he taught an ancient teaching when Arjuna’s own birth is recent. Krishna answers in the passage known as Gita 4:5–8. The translation of Sir Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial (1885), renders it in verse and does not number the verses. Verses 5–6:

Manifold the renewals of my birth Have been, Arjuna! and of thy births, too! But mine I know, and thine thou knowest not, O Slayer of thy Foes! Albeit I be Unborn, undying, indestructible, The Lord of all things living; not the less--By Maya, by my magic which I stamp On floating Nature-forms, the primal vast--I come, and go, and come.

Verses 7–8:

When Righteousness Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take Visible shape, and move a man with men, Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, And setting Virtue on her seat again.

(Bhagavad Gita 4:5–8, Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, 1885)

The translation of Kashinath Trimbak Telang (1882) is in prose and stays closer to the Sanskrit. For verses 7–8 it reads:

“Whensoever, O descendant of Bharata! piety languishes, and impiety is in the ascendant, I create myself. I am born age after age, for the protection of the good, for the destruction of evil-doers, and the establishment of piety.”

(Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8, K. T. Telang, 1882)

Both translations say that Krishna takes birth from age to age when righteousness declines, to protect the good and restore righteousness. The verse says nothing about a named future figure. It states a recurring pattern.

The same lines in a Bahá’í document

One Common Faith is a document prepared under the supervision of the Universal House of Justice. It is not an authoritative interpretation. It quotes Arnold’s rendering as an early and clear expression of the idea that divine teaching returns from age to age:

“One of its earliest and clearest expressions occurs in the Bhagavad-Gita: “I come, and go, and come. When Righteousness declines, O Bharata! When Wickedness is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take visible shape, and move a man with men, succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, and setting Virtue on her seat again“ (One Common Faith, OCF 42)

In that paragraph the document uses the verses as a pattern of succeeding revelations. It does not name Bahá’u’lláh as their fulfilment.

The ten forms of Vishnu and the one still to come

Hindu texts list the descents (avatars) of Vishnu. The count varies. In the lists Terry gives (section 3), from eight to twenty-two forms, Kalki is last. Momen, in Hinduism and the Bahá’í Faith (1990), writes that in many scriptures ten are identified and in others more than twenty, and that ”most agree that the last four are Rama, then Krishna, then Buddha, then the Avatar to come who is named Kalki.“ The Garuda Purana, as quoted by Anupam Premanand (translator not named in the paper), gives the ten in one line: ”The Fish, the Tortoise, the Boar, the Man-lion, the Dwarf, Paraśurâma, Râma, Krishna, Buddha, and also Kalkî.“

The same list is sung in the hymn to Vishnu in Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda. Arnold’s verse translation (The Indian Song of Songs, 1875) gives each form a stanza; the stanza before this one is the Buddha. The tenth form, which Arnold’s version does not name as Kalki, is the sword-bearing one:

We praise thee, Wielder of the sweeping sword, Brilliant as curving comets in the gloom, Whose edge shall smite the fierce barbarian horde; Hail to thee, Keshav! hail, and hear, and come,

(Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Dashavatara hymn, Edwin Arnold, 1875)

The hymn closes by naming the forms in turn and then: ”Under all thy ten disguises / Endless praise to thee arises.“

Kalki in the Vishnu Purana

The Vishnu Purana, in H. H. Wilson’s translation (1840), describes Kalki at the close of the Kali age, the last of the four ages:

”When the practices taught by the Vedas and the institutes of law shall nearly have ceased, and the close of the Kali age shall be nigh, a portion of that divine being who exists of his own spiritual nature in the character of Brahma, and who is the beginning and the end, and who comprehends all things, shall descend upon earth: he will be born in the family of Vishnuyasas, an eminent Brahman of Sambhala village, as Kalki, endowed with the eight superhuman faculties.“

(Vishnu Purana IV.24, H. H. Wilson, 1840)

The same paragraph continues: Kalki ”will then reestablish righteousness upon earth; and the minds of those who live at the end of the Kali age shall be awakened, and shall be as pellucid as crystal“, and those people ”shall give birth to a race who shall follow the laws of the Krita age, or age of purity.“ Earlier in the chapter, the Kali age begins when Krishna leaves the earth: ”The day that Krishna shall have departed from the earth will be the first of the Kali age“.

This is a different text from the Gita. The Gita speaks in Krishna’s voice about his own recurring births; the Purana speaks of Kalki in the third person, as a future event. They are connected below only where a named source connects them.

2. The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

What Shoghi Effendi writes

Shoghi Effendi’s two statements in God Passes By are the authoritative texts on this page. The first is the one quoted above, in the passage that names the fulfilment for each tradition in turn (GPB VI.11): ”to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna.“

The second is in a later paragraph, one of a series (GPB VI.13–VI.17) that states what the scripture of each tradition referred to when it spoke of Bahá’u’lláh. The paragraph begins with the Buddhist prophecy: ”He alone is meant by the prophecy attributed to Gautama Buddha Himself“. The next sentence is about the Hindu scripture:

”To Him the Bhagavad-Gita of the Hindus had referred as the “Most Great Spirit,” the “Tenth Avatar,” the “Immaculate Manifestation of Krishna.”

(Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.15)

The three phrases are in quotation marks in the original. Shoghi Effendi attributes them to the Bhagavad-Gita. The text does not say which verses contain them. The next two sections report what the Universal House of Justice has said about this and what the Gita and the other Hindu texts saved for this page contain.

What the Universal House of Justice has said about GPB VI.15

The compilation Scriptures of Previous Dispensations collects letters on the Hindu and Buddhist scriptures. Three bear on this sentence. They differ in kind, and each is labelled.

17 August 1971, a letter written by the Universal House of Justice to an individual believer. The believer had observed that ”a contradiction appears to exist between the words of the beloved Guardian and the statements of scholars concerning his reference to the Tenth Avatar in the Bhagavad-Gita.“ The House of Justice replies that scholars disagree about the authorship of the Hindu scriptures and the date of the Gita, though they agree it is ”a great religious classic“. It quotes the letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi of 25 November 1950 (”We cannot be sure of the authenticity of the scriptures of Buddha and Krishna...“) and continues:

”Yet, exercising his special wisdom as Guardian, in God Passes By he applies to Bahá’u’lláh the several titles to which you refer in your letter. As Bahá’ís, we obviously accept as authoritative whatever he has enunciated in such matters.“

(a letter dated 17 August 1971, written by the Universal House of Justice to an individual believer)

12 July 1976, a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice to an individual believer. The believer had been unable to find the phrases in the Gita:

”the House of Justice does not know the exact context of the references quoted by Shoghi Effendi (in ‘God Passes By’) from the Bhagavad-Gita, but it you have been unable to locate them in the book of that name it may be because the beloved Guardian was using the name of the book to stand for the entire Hindu Scripture, as it is common to refer to the entire Old Testament as the Torah, to the New Testament as the Gospel, or the Báb’s Revelation as the Bayán.“

(a letter dated 12 July 1976, written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice; ”it you“ is as printed in the compilation)

1 September 1977, a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice to an individual believer. The question was about Hindu prophecies of Bahá’u’lláh:

”Concerning Hindu prophecies of the coming of Bahá’u’lláh and the relationship of the Hindu and Bahá’í Faiths, nothing authentic and specific is available at the World Centre, apart from the Guardian’s statement in God Passes By“

(a letter dated 1 September 1977, written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice)

It cites GPB p. 95 (the three phrases) and p. 94 (”to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna“), and adds that ”Bahá’í teachings on progressive revelation do, of course, bear on the relationship of these Faiths.“ (It prints the last phrase as ”Immaculate Manifestation of the Krishna“; the God Passes By text in the Bahá’í Reference Library has no ”the“.)

What these letters settle. The House of Justice accepts the Guardian’s statement as authoritative (1971). It does not know the verses to which he refers (1976) and has no other authentic and specific material on Hindu prophecies of Bahá’u’lláh (1977). It offers one possible reason the phrases are not found in the book of that name, that the Guardian used the name for Hindu scripture as a whole, and gives it as a possibility (”it may be because“), not a finding. The question has been put to the World Centre, and the answer on record is that the context is not known there either.

Krishna as a Manifestation, 15 August 1983. A letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice (signed ”For Department of the Secretariat“), in reply to a believer’s letter of 26 July 1983, is filed as ”Krishna, historiography of“. It quotes the letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 13 July 1938 that lists Krishna among nine Prophets, and states:

”It can, therefore, be confidently stated that the teachings of the Faith name Krishna as a Manifestation of God. In light, however, of the other statements of the Guardian, in which he stresses the paucity of our information about the beginnings of Hinduism, we should be cautious not to assert the historical accuracy of specific stories related about Krishna.“

(a letter dated 15 August 1983, written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice)

It compares this to Adam: ”A similar case where allegorical statements and legends surround the figure of a known Manifestation of God is that of Adam.“

The three phrases, one at a time

This section reports what the saved texts contain. The Gita chapters checked are Arnold’s chapters 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13 and 15 and Telang’s chapter 4 (`sources/web/the-day-foretold/hindu/`). No verse with any of the three phrases was found in them, and no Bahá’í text found says which verses Shoghi Effendi means.

”Most Great Spirit.“ In God Passes By this is first Bahá’u’lláh’s own term. Shoghi Effendi writes that in the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán the ”Most Great Spirit“, ”as designated by Himself, and symbolized in the Zoroastrian, the Mosaic, the Christian, and Muḥammadan Dispensations by the Sacred Fire, the Burning Bush, the Dove and the Angel Gabriel respectively“, descended upon Him (GPB VI.26). He introduces Bahá’u’lláh’s account in the Súratu’l-Haykal of the Maiden ”symbolizing the “Most Great Spirit”” (GPB VI.28). In a passage quoted from a Tablet, Bahá’u’lláh says: “the Most Great Spirit appeared before My face” (GPB VI.29). No text found says which Sanskrit term lies behind the same words in GPB VI.15. The nearest wordings in Arnold’s English, offered as the closest matches and not as sources, are:

That Ultimate, High Spirit, Uncreate,

(Bhagavad Gita ch. 13, Arnold, 1885)

Am called of men and Vedas, Life Supreme, The PURUSHOTTAMA.

(Bhagavad Gita ch. 15, Arnold, 1885)

“Tenth Avatar.” The ten-form lists end with Kalki. No Gita chapter checked mentions Kalki or counts avatars. The Gita states the avatar principle (4:7–8). The figure counted as tenth belongs to the Puranic and devotional tradition: the Vishnu Purana, the Garuda Purana (as quoted by Premanand) and Jayadeva’s hymn. That may be what the 1976 letter means by “the entire Hindu Scripture”, but this is an inference, not a statement in a Bahá’í text. Shoghi Effendi writes “Tenth Avatar”, not “Kalki”.

“Immaculate Manifestation of Krishna.” “Manifestation” is Bahá’í vocabulary for a Prophet. No saved Hindu text calls Krishna’s descent “immaculate”. Arnold has passages on the Supreme being untouched by action: chapter 13, “The subtle Soul sits everywhere, unstained”; chapter 10, Arjuna addresses Krishna as “The Great Purification!”. These are nearby ideas, not sources. Terry, after Jacobi, notes that the Mahabharata’s word for a divine appearance, pradurbhava, is translated as “manifestation” (section 3).

The Kitáb-i-Íqán on “return”

Shoghi Effendi’s word in GPB VI.11 is “reincarnation”. The Kitáb-i-Íqán does not mention Krishna, but it is the Bahá’í text that explains in what sense one Manifestation is the return of another:

“Strive therefore to comprehend the meaning of “return” which hath been so explicitly revealed in the Qur’án itself, and which none hath as yet understood.”

(Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 160)

“Wherefore, should one of these Manifestations of Holiness proclaim saying: “I am the return of all the Prophets,” He verily speaketh the truth.”

(Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 162)

No source found applies these passages to GPB VI.11. Momen applies the idea of return to Hindu figures (section 3).

The Bahá’í International Community, 1992

Bahá’u’lláh (1992) is a statement of the Bahá’í International Community Office of Public Information. It lists what Bahá’u’lláh is for each tradition, including “to Hinduism, the new incarnation of Krishna” (BBIC 156). It is commentary, not an authoritative interpretation.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on Krishna and on Hindus

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks that mention Krishna are known from notes of the talks. In a talk recorded in Paris Talks, an Indian visitor said that his aim in life was to carry the message of Krishna to the world. From notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá:

“The Message of Krishna is the message of love. All God’s prophets have brought the message of love. None has ever thought that war and hate are good. Everyone agrees in saying that love and kindness are best.”

(Paris Talks, 9.2)

In a talk of 7 October 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Krishna is named among the founders of religions:

“Blessed souls—whether Moses, Jesus, Zoroaster, Krishna, Buddha, Confucius or Muḥammad—were the cause of the illumination of the world of humanity.”

(The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 109.11)

In a talk of 5 July 1912 (PUP 80.1) He says that “unmistakable prophecies” in the holy books announce one Day. He names the Torah, the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta and “the books of Buddha”; the Hindu scriptures are not named in that sentence.

In Some Answered Questions, in the commentary on Isaiah 11, He names Hindus among Bahá’u’lláh’s followers:

“Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, Hindus, Buddhists, and Persians all consort together with perfect love and fellowship, as if for a thousand years they had belonged to the same kindred and family”

(‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 12.6)

Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi

Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, collected in Compilation of Compilations vol. 1 (Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster), bear on Krishna and on Hindu scripture. They are secretaries’ letters, not Shoghi Effendi’s own writings.

A letter of 13 July 1938 places Krishna in a series of nine Prophets: “Bahá’u’lláh being the ninth Prophet in the series. The other Prophets included are Zoroaster, Krishna, Moses, the Christ, Muhammad, Buddha, the Prophet of the Sabaeans Whose name is unrecorded, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh”.

A letter of 22 June 1943 states the general principle and its limit:

“In the Bahá’í teachings it states that all the Prophets have foretold a Promised One Who is Bahá’u’lláh.”

“We cannot be sure of the authenticity, word for word, of any of the past Holy Scriptures except the Qur’án, as they were either not written down during the Prophet’s lifetime or have been changed in the course of time and the originals lost”

The same letter says that when Bahá’u’lláh or ‘Abdu’l-Bahá stated that Zoroaster foretold a Promised One’s coming, “it is correct”. Three letters limit what the Teachings say about Hinduism:

“The Bahá’í writings also do not refer specifically to any of these forms of religion current in India.”

(a letter of 17 April 1936, in reply to a believer studying Hinduism, which also urges that such studies be carried on “with the view of bringing the Message to the Hindus”)

“Your question concerning Brahma and Krishna: such matters, as no reference occurs to them in the Teachings, are left for students of history and religion to resolve and clarify.”

(a letter of 14 April 1941)

“We cannot be sure of the authenticity of the scriptures of Buddha and Krishna, so we certainly cannot draw any conclusions about virgin birth mentioned in them. There is no reference to this subject in our teachings, so the Guardian cannot pronounce an opinion.”

(a letter of 25 November 1950)

The 1941 letter concerns Brahma and Krishna in general and the 1950 letter the virgin birth; neither concerns the promise of return.

3. How Bahá’í authors have read the prophecies

These works are not authoritative. They are the main Bahá’í books and papers in the corpus on the Hindu prophecies, reported as each author’s reading. Where a reading rests on a Bahá’í text, the text is named.

Moojan Momen, Hinduism and the Bahá’í Faith (1990)

Momen’s book is an introduction for Hindu readers. Chapter 4 sets out the Kali Yuga and the Kalki Avatar: “Hindus are awaiting the coming of the Kalki Avatar at the end of this present age, Kali Yuga. Bahá’ís believe that we are already at this time. We are at the end of the Kali Yuga and Bahá’u’lláh is the Kalki Avatar.” Momen quotes the Vishnu Purana on Kalki, then a second passage: “He will then establish righteousness upon the earth and the minds of the people will be awakened and become pure as crystal.” Momen glosses the name Bahá’u’lláh as “the Glory of Bhagwan” or “the Splendour of Ishvara”, and in chapter 3 explains “return” as the return of a “mind-type”, not of the same person.

Momen also reports that some Bahá’í scholars have shown from Hindu texts that the Kali Yuga ends in 1844 (citing H. M. Munje and Mishra). That is a scholars’ calculation; no Bahá’í text found states it, and this page does not repeat it. Momen’s pairing of Gleanings LXI (“The world is in travail”) with the Kali Yuga prophecies is Momen’s own. Momen does not discuss the three phrases of GPB VI.15; Momen quotes the Gita only for the general avatar principle (4:7–8).

Momen and Terry cite different sources for the same passage. Momen’s note 10 gives Vishnu Purana 4:24 for the “pure as crystal” sentence; Momen’s note 8 gives 4:24 for the Kalki-birth sentence and adds a “see also” to Bhagavata Purana XII, 2:16. Terry gives the Kalki-birth sentence as “VP:4:24” and the “pure as crystal” sentence as “BP:XII:2:16”. The wording is the same in both. In Wilson’s translation both sentences stand in one paragraph of Vishnu Purana IV.24, so Momen’s reference agrees with Wilson; Terry’s second reference is to the Bhagavata Purana, which also describes Kalki. No Bhagavata text was saved, so its wording was not checked.

Christopher Buck, “The Mystery of the Sworded Warrior in Hindu Apocalypse: Was Kalki Viṣṇuyaśas Bahá’u’lláh?” (1980)

Buck submitted this paper to World Order on 10 November 1980. It is the item found that examines the Hindu texts most critically. Buck’s conclusion: “In an ideal sense, yes. In a strict sense, no.” In brief:

  • Kalki in the older texts is a warrior on a horse who destroys the wicked and begins the golden age. Kalki’s coming “is for the first time foretold in the Mahabharata (3:190:94)”.
  • Bahá’í books in Hindi and English adduce proof-texts from the Kalki Purana; Buck says they do not agree with the latest critical edition. Proving the identification from chronological prophecies, Buck says, is “doomed to confusion” if one wishes to represent Hindu tradition with intellectual honesty.
  • The Gita does not name Kalki. Its own words for the avatar’s coming are janman (birth), sambhava (coming into being) and srjana (creation). The Mahabharata prefers pradurbhava, “manifestation or appearance”.
  • Bahá’ís in Malwa “have proclaimed in song” that Bahá’u’lláh is Kalki, and Buck suggests Bahá’u’lláh “has redefined as well as revalorized Kalki”.
  • Buck ends on a condition: Bahá’u’lláh is Kalki “if and only if” He and His followers can wage love more effectively than war and restore justice.

A sentence in Star of the West, 1915

Buck says the only place in the Bahá’í sources available to Buck where a central figure approved the identification is Star of the West, vol. 6, no. 15 (12 December 1915). The corpus has that issue:

  • The article is “The Call of God”, by George O. Latimer. A note says: “Compiled in Stuttgart, Germany, during August, 1914. This manuscript was presented to Abdul-Baha personally by Mr. Latimer while in Akka in October of the same year. Abdul-Baha gave consent to its publication.—The Editors.”
  • The sentence reads: “the Hindoos await the appearance of the tenth incarnation of truth, called Kalki”. It stands in a list of the expectations of Jews, Christians, Muslims, Buddhists and Zoroastrians.
  • The article later says the Bahá’í Revelation “brings the fulfillment of all these hopes and conditions to the world.”

This is a report in a periodical, in the words of its author. The editors say ‘Abdu’l-Bahá consented to the publication of the manuscript. They do not say He wrote or confirmed the sentence, and no Tablet of His on Kalki was found in the corpus. Buck’s “In a strict sense, no” treats it as the nearest thing in the sources, not as a statement by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. This page therefore does not say that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá named Kalki.

Peter Terry, Proofs of the Prophets: The Case for Lord Krishna

Terry compiled this volume of the Bahá’í Studies Series. Terry’s preface says only the Bahá’í Faith “regards Lord Krishna as the Founder of a Faith, as a Messenger and Manifestation of God”. Terry’s avatar lists (Harivamsa eight, Mahabharata nine, Matsya Purana ten, Bhagavata Purana twenty-two) all end in Kalki. Terry treats Kalki as a prophecy attributed to Krishna: “We find descriptions of Kalkin-Avatara in Vishnu Purana and in Mahabharata, two works associated with Krishna, and so we will treat the promise of Kalki as a prophecy of Krishna-Avatara.”

On vocabulary, Terry reports, after Hermann Jacobi, that the Harivamsa and Mahabharata call the appearances pradurbhava, that the Harivamsa lists Kalki as the eighth “but then states that Kalki is the tenth”, and that “Pradurbhava” is translated as “manifestation” and does not imply “incarnation.” This is the only discussion found of a Hindu term corresponding to the Bahá’í “Manifestation”.

Gloria Faizi, The Promise of Lord Krishna

Faizi’s booklet (Minh Publications, New Delhi; credited to G. Faizi) is a short presentation for Hindu readers. It opens with Gita 4:7 (“Whenever there is decay of righteousness”) under the heading “The Promise” and asks “Why should the Lord come back?” Faizi says the Lord “did not come as people expected, nor did He appear in India as He had done centuries ago”. Faizi argues from the Gita (the references include IV:7–8, IX:11, XI:8), not from the Puranas, and does not discuss Kalki or the three phrases in the pages searched. Anupam Premanand’s paper in Lights of ‘Irfán, Book Eleven, “Dashavatara and Progressive Revelation”, sets the ten avatars beside progressive revelation and says “As per the common Hindu thought, the tenth Avatara Kalki is yet to manifest Himself”.

David M. Earl, a letter to World Order (Winter 1982–83)

Earl’s letter “About Krishna” answers William Garlington’s article on Bahá’í bhajans. It is a reader’s opinion. Earl argues that in GPB VI.15 Shoghi Effendi “is clearly quoting a specific piece of literature, the Bhagavad Gītā—not Krishna—and he is not making any statement at all about Krishna, but is rather making one about Bahá’u’lláh.” The House of Justice’s letter of 15 August 1983 (section 2) later states that the Faith’s teachings name Krishna as a Manifestation of God, with caution about the historical accuracy of specific stories. Ruth Moffett’s New Keys to the Book of Revelation (1977) repeats GPB VI.15 without comment.

4. Those who recognised it

This section comes from history and scholarship. It is not from the Bahá’í Writings.

History, from Shoghi Effendi. In God Passes By Shoghi Effendi records the growth of the Bahá’í community in India and Burma, “now including among its members representatives of the Zoroastrian, the Islamic, the Hindu and the Buddhist Faiths, as well as members of the Sikh community” (GPB XX.17). He adds that at Daidanaw Kalazoo, in the Hanthawaddy district of Burma, “no less than eight hundred Bahá’ís resided”.

Early speakers. Garlington writes that a Mr. Ayer lectured at the 1921 All-India Bahá’í Convention, “identified Bahá’u’lláh with the Hindu avatar tradition”, and that one of Dorothy Baker’s addresses in Malwa in 1953 was entitled “The New Avatar”.

Malwa, 1941. Garlington describes the move of two Bombay families to Ujjain. In the latter months of 1941 Mrs. Meherabani asked her husband Boman Meherabani to leave the city, in answer to Shoghi Effendi’s call to settle in goal locations, and in December the Meherabani and Munje families left together. Garlington’s two articles tell differently how he came to agree:

  • In Bahá’í Proselytization in Malwa, India: he was “unresponsive”, but “following a dream in which Shoghi Effendi urged him to fulfill his duty”, he consented.
  • In The Development of the Bahá’í Faith in Malwa: “after a vivid dream in which he felt that the Guardian had called on him to make a sacrifice for Bahá’u’lláh”, he consented.

Both say a dream involving the Guardian preceded his agreement. They differ on the call (a duty, or a sacrifice for Bahá’u’lláh), and neither gives a source for the dream.

Daya Ram Malviya. In 1974 Garlington received a letter from Daya Ram Malviya of Harsodan, one of the first Malwa villagers to declare as a Bahá’í and later a leading travelling teacher. Garlington translated it as Biographical letter from a Hindu villager. Malviya says he first heard of the Faith in 1941 when Mr. Boman, Shirin Mummy and Dr. Munje came from Bombay, and that his father accepted at once. His father died in 1944. Troubled by the deceit he saw among followers of every religion, Malviya began to think of ways to become richer. At the house of Kishan Lal Malviya in Shajapur he wrote the names of occupations on separate pieces of paper, some respectable (hotel owner, shop keeper, farmer) and some not, and one that read “Service to the Bahá’í Faith.” Reciting the name of Bhagavan, without looking, he drew one. It was “Service to the Bahá’í Faith.” He became free of anxiety, and a year later “Shirin Mummy and Dr. Muhajir” made him a travelling teacher. The letter gives no year for the draw; it follows 1944 in the letter’s order. It does not mention Kalki.

Mass teaching in Malwa. Susan Maneck, in Conversion Movements within Hindu Village Culture (1997), describes the mass teaching in Malwa, in what is now Madhya Pradesh, which began around 1960. The account that follows is Maneck’s and Garlington’s.

  • In 1960 Mrs. Mehrabani spent several days in a Bhil tribal village near Indore; 75 of the 200 villagers declared themselves believers with thumbprints on enrollment cards.
  • At an open-air meeting at Sangimanda, in Shajapur, in January 1961, “Bahá’í speakers announced that Bahá’u’lláh was the bhagavan kalkin”, which Maneck glosses as “the tenth avatar of Vishnu whose return in Vaishnavite theology marks the end of the kali-yuga.” At the end of the conference 200 villagers became Bahá’ís, and the Indian National Spiritual Assembly bought jeeps and sent teachers through rural Malwa. Garlington’s note says this conference “really initiated the flood of declarations in Malwa over the next decade”.
  • Most early enrolments were from scheduled tribes and castes. In the Gwalior area in 1962 Bahá’ís approached the leading castes first. Of 276 declared believers Garlington studied, 159 were from untouchable or unclean castes; the rest were caste Hindus, “often Raiputs and Brahmans”. By 1974 there were 113,692 declared Bahá’ís in Malwa in 6,572 localities.
  • Maneck writes that by translating the Bahá’í concept of the Manifestation of God into the Hindu term avatar, Bahá’ís presented Bahá’u’lláh as Kalkin, the Vaishnavite figure expected at the end of the kali-yuga, and made frequent reference to the Bhagavad Gita. The passage most often quoted in Bahá’í literature and song, Maneck says, is Gita 4:7–8.

Garlington adds that Bahá’u’lláh was commonly called “Bhagvan [Lord] Bahá’u’lláh” or “Bhagvan Baha”. In 1964 Ruhiyyih Khanum’s first stop in Malwa was the Bhil village that had been “the ignition point of the mass teaching campaign in the region”; she was taken to the Bahá’í bhavan “in an especially painted ox cart to the accompaniment of flutes and drums”.

Bahá’í bhajans. Garlington, in Bahá’í Bhajans, records that some bhajans identify Bahá’u’lláh with the kalkin avatar. Garlington gives this verse of a popular bhajan, in Garlington’s translation:

“Arise O children of India, the kalkin avatar has come; Vishnu’s avatar has come with the name Bahá’u’lláh Nowhere in the entire world can the influence of religion be seen. The wicked have obtained everything; the truthful have lost all. According to the Gita the time of Vishnu’s avatar has come...Arise!”

Garlington explains the choice of this figure by the mission among lower castes: the hope of a coming deliverer “would make sense” as a way to reach them. Garlington notes that Hindu pandits have not generally given Kalkin an important place. Other lines quoted include “Bhagavan has said that he will return in every age to restore righteousness” and “Radha and Arjuna knew that the Lord had taken a new form.” Garlington reads the appeal to “the Gita” as showing that Bahá’ís treated it as the supreme Hindu text, “if not as the book of the avatar Krishna, then at least as the text most significantly related to him”. GPB VI.15 names the same book.

Short introductions. The leaflet Hinduism and the Bahá’í Faith (Warwick Leaflets series) states: “Hindu literature looks towards the return of an avatar of God called Kalki. Bahá’ís believe that Bahá’u’lláh is that return”. Buck’s A Unique Eschatological Interface lists Kalki among the figures Bahá’u’lláh has been “heralded by his followers” as. Neither is an authoritative text.

5. The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

The following is a description from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0), from its articles “Bhajan” and “Kirtan”. It describes forms and is not a source for lyrics.

Bhajan. “Bhajan is an Indian term for any devotional song with a religious theme or spiritual ideas.” It has “no prescribed form, or set rules” and is normally “lyrical and based on melodic ragas.” Small handheld cymbals (kartals) keep the beat, with the tabla, dholak or tambourine. Bhajans are more often sung together than alone, in a temple, a home, in the open or beside a river, and are especially common in Vaishnavism.

Kirtan. A call-and-response style in which a lead singer sings a chant, a hymn or a name of God and the group repeats or answers it, with harmonium, tabla, mridanga, flute and karatalas. In Vaishnavism, nama-kirtana extols the names of God and lila-kirtana narrates the deity’s life and legends.

In Malwa. Garlington describes a group bhajan: one devotee stands and sings the verses while the whole assemblage joins in unison to sing the refrain. Bahá’í bhajans there use this form with Bahá’u’lláh as the subject.

6. What the sources do not settle

Which verses Shoghi Effendi means in GPB VI.15. The House of Justice does not know the context (section 2). The saved Gita chapters were searched for each phrase and for “avatar”, “tenth”, “immaculate” and “great spirit”; none appears. Those files do not cover the whole Gita or other translations.

Whether “Tenth Avatar” is Kalki. Shoghi Effendi writes “Tenth Avatar”. The Hindu lists that count ten end with Kalki, and Momen, Terry, Premanand, Buck, Maneck and Garlington treat the Tenth Avatar as Kalki. No text by Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi or the House of Justice that was found uses the word Kalki. The one earlier Bahá’í use found is the 1915 Star of the West sentence by Latimer, whose manuscript ‘Abdu’l-Bahá consented to have published (section 3). The earliest use found of “the tenth avatar of Vishnu” for Bahá’u’lláh is a 1913 Vassar Miscellany report by Professor Hill, who was not a Bahá’í and wrote that Bahá’u’lláh’s followers believe him to be the one expected by “the Hindus as the tenth avatar of Vishnu”. This page states the identification as a reading, not as a Bahá’í text.

Whether Hindu scholars or institutions accept the Bahá’í reading is not addressed by any source on this page.

What was not read. The Bhagavata, Garuda and Kalki Puranas and the Mahabharata passage Buck cites were not saved; they are known here only through Momen, Terry, Premanand and Buck. Sarwal and Sreenivasan, “Bhagavad-Gita and the Bahá’í Faith”, is listed on bahai-library.com as audio and was not read.

The sword in “Brilliant as curving comets in the gloom” belongs to Jayadeva’s tenth form in Arnold’s translation. It is not a Bahá’í text and should not be put in Bahá’u’lláh’s mouth.

Concrete images

  • The charioteer who speaks to Arjuna (the setting of Arnold’s chapter 4).
  • A figure who will “move a man with men” and set “Virtue” back “on her seat”.
  • A rider with a sword on a white horse, in Hindu tradition as Garlington describes it.
  • Slips of paper with occupations written on them, a name of God said over them, and the one that reads “Service to the Bahá’í Faith” (Malviya, 1974).
  • An open-air meeting at Sangimanda in January 1961, 200 villagers enrolling; thumbprints on enrollment cards; jeeps carrying teachers across Malwa.
  • An ox cart painted for a visitor, with flutes and drums (Garlington, 1964).
  • A bhajan with cymbals and a drum, a lead voice and the gathering joining the refrain.

Possible emotional arcs

  1. Declining righteousness; a promise made long ago (“I come, and go, and come”); waiting through the ages; villagers gathering in the open air to sing.
  2. A village house, a man weighing his own ambitions, a slip of paper that reads “Service to the Bahá’í Faith”.

What a lyric must not claim

  • That a verse of the Gita names Bahá’u’lláh, or that Shoghi Effendi named Kalki. He did not use that word in the texts found, and the House of Justice does not know which verses he meant.
  • That ‘Abdu’l-Bahá named Kalki. The only item found is a sentence by George Latimer in Star of the West (1915).
  • That Hindu teachers, scholars or institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.
  • That the Kalki of the Vishnu Purana and the Bahá’í account of Bahá’u’lláh are the same description. They differ in many details.
  • That the stories told of Krishna are historical in their details (letter of 15 August 1983).
  • Anything that sets Krishna against another Prophet.
