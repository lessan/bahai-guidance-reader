The Hour of Dawn: Rising to Pray While the World Sleeps

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page covers

Bahá’u’lláh names one hour for the praise of God in a House of Worship: “the hour of dawn.” His prayers and Tablets return to the same hour, and to a particular kind of person: those who leave their beds to pray. This page collects what the Writings say about dawn as a time for the soul, in this order:

  1. The law. What Bahá’u’lláh ordains about dawn, and how flexible He makes it.
  2. The people who wake. The prayers about lovers who “flee from sleep,” and the contrast with a world that is asleep.
  3. What the texts say dawn gives the soul. Bahá’u’lláh’s instructions to the seeker, the prayers for waking, and what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá writes about the first act of the morning.
  4. How the Central Figures kept the hour. The Báb, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi.
  5. Dawn as an image of the Revelation. The Mashriqu’l-Adhkár as a “dawning-place,” and the night of the Báb’s Declaration.
  6. What the texts do not settle. Where the sources give a reason for praying at dawn and where they give only the practice.

Quotations are copied from the texts held in this knowledge base. Two kinds of source are labelled where they appear: narrative accounts (Nabíl’s The Dawn-Breakers, The Priceless Pearl, a Bahá’í News reminiscence, a published diary), which tell what happened but are not the Writings; and commentary by Adib Taherzadeh.

1. The law: “at the hour of dawn”

Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶115

The paragraph of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas that ties the House of Worship to an hour of the day is ¶115:

“Blessed is he who, at the hour of dawn, centering his thoughts on God, occupied with His remembrance, and supplicating His forgiveness, directeth his steps to the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár and, entering therein, seateth himself in silence to listen to the verses of God, the Sovereign, the Mighty, the All-Praised.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶115)

The sentence describes a person in motion: thoughts already on God, already remembering, already asking forgiveness, then walking to the House of Worship and sitting in silence to listen. The blessing is given to the one who does this.

How strict “dawn” is

In the Questions and Answers appended to the Aqdas, Bahá’u’lláh was asked what “at the hour of dawn” means:

“Although the words “at the hour of dawn” are used in the Book of God, it is acceptable to God at the earliest dawn of day, between dawn and sunrise, or even up to two hours after sunrise.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Questions and Answers, no. 15)

The Universal House of Justice’s compilation The Institution of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár repeats this in a note on the phrase “at dawn” in a Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, which shows the House of Justice treats it as the working definition. So the text fixes the hour and also allows a margin of about two hours after sunrise. It does not say that a person who cannot rise before the sun has failed.

The other times of day

Dawn is not the only hour named. Bahá’u’lláh also writes:

“Recite ye the verses of God every morn and eventide.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶149)

and, in the paragraph that raises work to the rank of worship:

“Reflect, O people, on the grace and blessings of your Lord, and yield Him thanks at eventide and dawn.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶33)

The same paragraph 149 warns against turning the practice into a burden:

“Read ye the sacred verses in such measure that ye be not overcome by languor and despondency.”

and says that “were a man to read a single verse with joy and radiance it would be better for him than to read with lassitude all the Holy Books of God.” The standard is joy, not quantity. This matters for anyone setting an alarm: the text prefers a short prayer said gladly to a long one said wearily.

The obligatory prayer is also tied to the morning: it is “to be offered at noon and in the morning and the evening unto God” (¶6). Question 83 defines these as sunrise, noon and sunset, with the morning prayer allowed “from morning till noon.” So the obligatory prayer’s morning slot begins at sunrise. The “hour of dawn” in ¶115 is earlier and is a different matter: it is the time for the gathering in the House of Worship.

The name of the building

Bahá’u’lláh’s name for the House of Worship is itself about the east. The House of Justice glosses it in The Institution of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár as “the Dawning-place of the Praise of God.” See MashriqulAdhkar.

2. The people who wake, and the world that sleeps

The cup reserved for each daybreak

The clearest picture of who rises at dawn is in a prayer Bahá’u’lláh revealed for the Fast (Prayers and Meditations, LXXXV; also in the Fast section of the Bahá’í Prayers book):

“For Thine ardent lovers Thou hast, according to Thy decree, reserved, at each daybreak, the cup of Thy remembrance, O Thou Who art the Ruler of rulers! These are they who have been so inebriated with the wine of Thy manifold wisdom that they forsake their couches in their longing to celebrate Thy praise and extol Thy virtues, and flee from sleep in their eagerness to approach Thy presence and partake of Thy bounty. Their eyes have, at all times, been bent upon the Dayspring of Thy loving-kindness, and their faces set towards the Fountainhead of Thine inspiration.”

Three things in this passage are stated directly. The cup of remembrance is reserved “at each daybreak”, so the hour has a gift attached to it. The people who take it leave their beds because of longing (“in their longing to celebrate Thy praise”), not because of a rule. And they “flee from sleep in their eagerness to approach Thy presence”, so sleep is what they leave behind and nearness to God is what they run toward.

The prayer goes on at once to the hour itself:

“Lauded be Thy name, O my God! This is the hour when Thou hast unlocked the doors of Thy bounty before the faces of Thy creatures, and opened wide the portals of Thy tender mercy unto all the dwellers of Thine earth.”

The fire that drove sleep from their eyes

Another prayer in the same book asks to be counted among these people:

“I beseech Thee, O my God, by the fire of Thy love which drove sleep from the eyes of Thy chosen ones and Thy loved ones, and by their remembrance and praise of Thee at the hour of dawn, to number me with such as have attained unto that which Thou hast sent down in Thy Book and manifested through Thy will.” (Prayers and Meditations, CLXXVII)

Here the cause is named: “the fire of Thy love.” The one praying does not claim to have been awake at dawn. He asks to be numbered with those who were.

Another prayer, in Gleanings (CXXXVIII), describes the same people from God’s side, as servants who “arise, at the earliest dawn of day, to make mention of Thy Name, and to celebrate Thy praise, in the hope of obtaining their share of the goodly things that are treasured up within the treasuries of Thy grace and bounty.” The prayer is about those who keep the Fast: they “observe in the daytime the fast prescribed by Thee” and also rise at the earliest dawn. What they hope for is a “share”, and they ask not to be hindered “from taking their portion.”

The lover who cannot sleep

The same image of sleeplessness from love appears in Bahá’u’lláh’s story of the lover in the Seven Valleys:

“How many a day he found no respite from his longing; how many a night the pain of her kept him from sleep.” (The Seven Valleys, ¶25)

In the story the lover’s longing is for a human beloved, and Bahá’u’lláh uses it as a figure for the seeker. It is a different kind of sleeplessness from the one in the Fast prayer, because it comes from pain and not from joy, but the cause is the same: longing for the Beloved.

A prayer for the night puts the question from the other side:

“How can I choose to sleep, O God, my God, when the eyes of them that long for Thee are wakeful because of their separation from Thee; and how can I lie down to rest whilst the souls of Thy lovers are sore vexed in their remoteness from Thy presence?” (Prayers and Meditations, CLXXII)

It does not tell anyone to stay up. The next paragraph of the same prayer has the one praying commit his spirit “into the right hand of Thy might and Thy protection” and lay his head on his pillow “through Thy power.” Sleep is not forbidden. The prayer shows how a person who loves God experiences the choice to sleep.

The world that sleeps through it

Against the lovers, Bahá’u’lláh sets the many who do not wake:

“The peoples of the world are fast asleep. Were they to wake from their slumber, they would hasten with eagerness unto God, the All-Knowing, the All-Wise.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶39)

In the Hidden Words, God Himself comes at dawn to a sleeping person and leaves:

“O BONDSLAVE OF THE WORLD! Many a dawn hath the breeze of My loving-kindness wafted over thee and found thee upon the bed of heedlessness fast asleep. Bewailing then thy plight it returned whence it came.” (Hidden Words, from the Persian, no. 30)

“O ESSENCE OF DESIRE! At many a dawn have I turned from the realms of the Placeless unto thine abode, and found thee on the bed of ease busied with others than Myself. Thereupon, even as the flash of the spirit, I returned to the realms of celestial glory and breathed it not in My retreats above unto the hosts of holiness.” (Hidden Words, from the Persian, no. 28)

These two passages say that dawn is the hour at which God visits, and that the visit can be missed. In both, sleep is a figure for heedlessness (“the bed of heedlessness”, “the bed of ease busied with others than Myself”), so they are about attention, not literally about getting out of bed. They do show why the Writings treat dawn as an hour with something in it: in the Hidden Words the visit happens at dawn whether or not anyone is awake to receive it.

Bahá’u’lláh’s own waking

Bahá’u’lláh describes His own call to speak in the same terms:

“I lay asleep on my couch, O my God, when lo, the gentle winds of Thy grace and Thy loving-kindness passed over me, and wakened me through the power of Thy sovereignty and Thy gifts, and bade me arise before Thy servants, and speak forth Thy praise, and glorify Thy word.” (Prayers and Meditations, CLXXIX)

In another prayer He says: “I lay asleep on the bed of self when lo, Thou didst waken me with the divine accents of Thy voice” (Prayers and Meditations, XVIII). In these prayers waking is something God does to a person, and the person responds by rising.

3. What dawn gives the soul

Bahá’u’lláh to the seeker

In the passage describing how a seeker should conduct himself, Bahá’u’lláh places communion with God at the start of each day:

“At the dawn of every day he should commune with God, and, with all his soul, persevere in the quest of his Beloved. He should consume every wayward thought with the flame of His loving mention, and, with the swiftness of lightning, pass by all else save Him.” (Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, CXXV; the same passage is in the Kitáb-i-Íqán)

The instruction comes with its purpose: to burn away “every wayward thought” and to pass quickly by everything else. The seeker begins the day by turning his attention to God before anything else claims it.

A Tablet to an individual believer, translated from the Persian, says the same in the second person:

“Occupy thyself in remembrance of the Beauty of Him Who is the Unconstrained at early morn, and seek communion with Him at the hour of dawn. O ‘Alí! Remembrance of Me is a healing medicine to the souls and a light to the hearts of men.” (Bahá’u’lláh, from a Tablet, in Prayer and Devotional Life)

The reason given is in the last sentence: remembrance is “a healing medicine to the souls and a light to the hearts of men.”

The prayers for waking

Bahá’u’lláh revealed prayers to be said on waking. Each is a thanksgiving for being woken, and each turns the first moment of the day toward the Revelation. The first is the prayer in the Bahá’í Prayers book’s morning section (Prayers and Meditations, CXLIV):

“I have risen from my couch at this dawntide when the Daystar of Thy oneness hath shone forth from the Dayspring of Thy will, and hath shed its radiance upon the whole world, according to what had been ordained in the Books of Thy Decree.”

It continues: “Praise be unto Thee, O my God, that we have wakened to the splendors of the light of Thy knowledge.” The next prayer in that section asks for light inside as there is light outside:

“I have wakened in Thy shelter, O my God, and it becometh him that seeketh that shelter to abide within the Sanctuary of Thy protection and the Stronghold of Thy defense. Illumine my inner being, O my Lord, with the splendors of the Dayspring of Thy Revelation, even as Thou didst illumine my outer being with the morning light of Thy favor.” (Prayers and Meditations, CLVIII)

And a third, which begins with the waking itself:

“I give praise to Thee, O my God, that Thou hast awakened me out of my sleep, and brought me forth after my disappearance, and raised me up from my slumber. I have wakened this morning with my face set toward the splendors of the Daystar of Thy Revelation, through Which the heavens of Thy power and Thy majesty have been illumined, acknowledging Thy signs, believing in Thy Book, and holding fast unto Thy Cord.” (Prayers and Meditations, CLVI)

In all three the physical sunrise and the spiritual one are named together (“the morning light of Thy favor”, “the Daystar of Thy Revelation”), and the person waking asks that the inner light match the outer.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the first act of the morning

‘Abdu’l-Bahá was asked about the morning prayer. His answer is in a Tablet to an American believer, translated by Mírzá Aḥmad Sohráb on 5 March 1914 at Haifa and printed in Star of the West (vol. 8, no. 4, 17 May 1917), in the chapter on prayer of “The Divine Art of Living,” a compilation by Mrs. Mary M. Rabb:

“Regarding thy question about the morning prayer. Both meanings are included in the word dawn—the natural dawn, and the dawn of the kingdom. When a soul rises in the morning from sleep before everything else he must commemorate the name of God in order that he may obtain spirituality and illumination.”

This is the nearest thing in the sources held here to a stated reason from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá for praying at dawn. It gives two points. “Dawn” has two senses, the sunrise and “the dawn of the kingdom”. And the practice is defined by order, not by the clock: the first thing a person does on rising is to remember God, and the stated result is “spirituality and illumination.” Section 6 says what this passage is and is not.

In a Tablet collected in the compilation The Importance of Obligatory Prayer and Fasting, he links his own morning supplication to what is available to everyone:

“Each morn God’s infinite grace confirmeth the ardent and tearful invocations of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. Accordingly, let every awakened soul obtain, to the extent of its capacity, a portion of this spiritual grace. This can be achieved by fervently offering unto God prayers and supplications at every dawn and observing the law of obligatory prayer. Thus may his nostrils delight in the sweet savors wafting from the garden of the bounty of God, his soul attain new life, and his reality mirror forth the effulgences of the All-Merciful.”

The passage states a mechanism: grace is confirmed each morning, and a person obtains a share of it “to the extent of its capacity.” The measure is the person’s own capacity, not a fixed amount. In the same compilation he writes: “Persevere in the use of the Obligatory Prayer and early morning supplications, so that day by day thine awareness may increase”.

In another Tablet he speaks of the morning as a season of the spirit:

“It is early morning, and the reviving winds of the Abhá Paradise are blowing over all creation, but they can stir only the pure of heart, and only the pure sense can detect their fragrance.” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 8)

and in a prayer, for himself:

“In the darksome night of despair, my eye turneth expectant and full of hope to the morn of Thy boundless favor and at the hour of dawn my drooping soul is refreshed and strengthened in remembrance of Thy beauty and perfection.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Bahá’í Prayers)

That prayer goes from despair at night to refreshment at dawn. It is the plainest statement in the Writings of what dawn does for a tired person.

Gathering at dawn

‘Abdu’l-Bahá also describes what happens when the friends gather at this hour. The House of Justice quoted the following Tablet in its message of 23 May 2021 for the dedication of the House of Worship in Matunda Soy, Kenya, and it appears in The Institution of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár:

“O ye beloved of God! Behold how abundant shall be the freshness, tenderness, spirituality, and radiance that are attained when ye assemble in that gathering place of the spirit, occupy yourselves at dawn with the remembrance of God, and, after reciting prayers, make mention together of the Most Bountiful Lord in sweet melodies. These melodies shall reach unto the Kingdom of Glory, and these songs shall cheer and gladden the Concourse on high.”

The four named results are “freshness, tenderness, spirituality, and radiance.” The sequence is also stated: gather, remember God at dawn, recite prayers, then sing together.

Writing to two believers who taught children the prayers, he approved a pattern of the same kind:

“Every day at first light, ye gather the Bahá’í children together and teach them the communes and prayers. This is a most praiseworthy act, and bringeth joy to the children’s hearts” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 115)

The Guardian and the House of Justice

Shoghi Effendi, in God Passes By, describes the future role of the House of Worship for the administrative institutions:

“From the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár, ordained as a house of worship by Bahá’u’lláh in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, the representatives of Bahá’í communities, both local and national, together with the members of their respective committees, will, as they gather daily within its walls at the hour of dawn, derive the necessary inspiration that will enable them to discharge, in the course of their day-to-day exertions in the Ḥaẓíratu’l-Quds—the scene of their administrative activities—their duties and responsibilities as befits the chosen stewards of His Faith.”

A letter dated 13 December 1928 to the Bahá’ís of ‘Ishqábád, written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi and translated from the Persian, is more direct:

“Among the signs of enkindlement, of grace and edification, of cheer and spirituality, is gathering in the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár at the hour of dawn and offering supplications and prayers in that majestic and luminous Temple. This matter is important and will produce great results.”

The Universal House of Justice has returned to dawn prayers several times. In a letter to all National Spiritual Assemblies of 17 April 1981 it lists among the matters to attend to “the vital necessity of prayer, and the value of holding gatherings for dawn prayers where and when feasible”. In its message of 25 May 1975 it described the effect in places where the practice had taken hold:

“In some rural areas this has become already an established practice of the friends and indeed a source of blessing and benefit to them as they pursue their activities during the day, as well as increasing the consciousness of community solidarity.”

Looking back on ‘Ishqábád, in its message to the Bahá’ís of the world of 1 August 2014, it described the House of Worship there as a place where “souls gathered at daybreak for humble invocation and communion before flowing out of its doors to engage in their daily pursuits.” The House of Justice connects the hour of prayer with the service that follows it.

4. How the Central Figures kept the hour

The following accounts are narrative, not the Writings. They tell what the early believers and family members reported.

The Báb

In the account of the Báb’s life in Shiraz by Ḥabíbu’lláh Afnán, which gives the testimony of the Báb’s wife, the morning begins before sunrise:

“In the morning, He would rise one hour before the dawn and, after washing, would perform His ablutions”

and then goes to the upper room set aside as a guestroom, where “He stood to recite tablets of visitation, prayers, supplications, and to repeat the names of God.” Only after sunrise does the household’s day begin: “When the sun had risen, Fiddih would fire up the samovar and prepare the tea”. Nabíl’s The Dawn-Breakers describes the Báb at Búshihr:

“From early dawn till sunrise, and from midday till late in the afternoon, He dedicated His time to meditation and pious worship.”

and says He “at every break of day, greeted, with a heart overflowing with love and joy” the rising sun, which to Him was “a sign and symbol of that Day-Star of Truth that was soon to dawn upon the world.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá

A reminiscence printed in Bahá’í News (no. 517, April 1974), told by a woman whose uncle was caretaker of the pilgrim house in ‘Akká, records:

“‘Abdu’l-Bahá used to get up at dawn to walk and meditate and pray in the courtyard of the house when everyone else was asleep.”

This is a single secondhand recollection. It matches what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote Himself in what Star of the West and The Passing of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá give as an excerpt from His last Tablet to the Bahá’ís of America:

“Every morning at dawn I supplicate the Kingdom of God and ask that you may be filled with the breath of the Holy Spirit, so that you may become brilliant candles, shine with the light of guidance and dispel the darkness of error.” (Star of the West, vol. 13, no. 1)

In a Tablet that speaks of the believers of Kenosha He writes: “this prisoner implores and supplicates at dawn, seeking strength and confirmation for the beloved of that town.” (Tablets of Abdul-Baha Abbas, vol. 1). In both, dawn is the time when He prays for other people.

Shoghi Effendi’s childhood

Rúḥíyyih Khánum records the household’s morning in her biography of the Guardian, The Priceless Pearl:

“In those days of Shoghi Effendi’s childhood it was the custom to rise about dawn and spend the first hour of the day in the Master’s room, where prayers were said and the family all had breakfast with Him.”

She adds that the children sat on the floor and “would chant for ‘Abdu’l-Bahá”. The Universal House of Justice included this account in an article it sent to National Spiritual Assemblies on 4 March 1975 about the house of ‘Abdu’lláh Páshá.

5. Dawn as an image of the Revelation

The Writings use dawn for the rising of a Manifestation. These are not instructions about prayer, but two events tie the image to the hour itself.

The Birth of Bahá’u’lláh. In Question 2 of the Questions and Answers, asked about the Festival of the Twin Birthdays, Bahá’u’lláh writes:

“The Birth of the Abhá Beauty was at the hour of dawn on the second day of the month of Muḥarram, the first day of which marketh the Birth of His Herald.”

The night of the Báb’s Declaration. Shoghi Effendi’s account in God Passes By of the meeting between the Báb and Mullá Ḥusayn on 22 May 1844 says that the conversation lasted until dawn:

“The interview itself was protracted till the hour of dawn. The Host remained closeted alone with His guest, nor was the sleeping city remotely aware of the import of the conversation they held with each other.”

The Declaration took place in a city that was asleep, and the world did not know. Mullá Ḥusayn’s own words, as Nabíl records them, describe the night from inside, including a reversal of the usual feeling about morning:

“Every time I visited Him, I spent the entire night in His presence. Wakeful until the dawn, I sat at His feet fascinated by the charm of His utterance and oblivious of the world and its cares and pursuits.”

and:

“How eagerly in those days I looked forward to the approach of the evening hour! With what feelings of sadness and regret I beheld the dawning of day!”

Nabíl records two things Mullá Ḥusayn said afterwards. Describing how he felt when he left the Báb’s house, he said he “seemed to be the Voice of Gabriel personified, calling unto all mankind: “Awake, for lo! the morning Light has broken. Arise, for His Cause is made manifest.”” In the weeks that followed, when the Báb sent for him at night, he recalled:

“Every time I visited Him, I spent the entire night in His presence. Wakeful until the dawn, I sat at His feet fascinated by the charm of His utterance and oblivious of the world and its cares and pursuits.”

and:

“How eagerly in those days I looked forward to the approach of the evening hour! With what feelings of sadness and regret I beheld the dawning of day!”

These are Nabíl’s narrative of a disciple’s recollection and should be read that way. They show that for someone in the Báb’s presence the night was the gift and dawn was its end, the reverse of the dawn in Bahá’u’lláh’s prayers, where dawn is the gift and sleep is what is left.

A star at dawn. One more Star of the West item concerns the hour, under the heading “The Brilliant Stars of the Early Dawn” (vol. 9, no. 14). In a diary kept by Mírzá Aḥmad Sohráb, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá points to Venus shining on the horizon and says: “This star shines clearest at the early dawn; so, at this dawn of the Sun of Reality, the stars of our lives must glisten and gleam in the darkness of the world.” This is a diary report of a spoken remark. It is not a Tablet and is not an authenticated text.

6. What the texts do not settle

Why dawn, specifically. The sources held here give four things and no more:

  • A command with a margin. Bahá’u’lláh names “the hour of dawn” and says the time up to two hours after sunrise is acceptable (Aqdas ¶115; Q&A 15). He gives no explanation of why that hour.
  • A statement about order. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s 1914 Tablet says that on rising, remembrance of God should come “before everything else”, and that “dawn” means both the sunrise and “the dawn of the kingdom.”
  • A statement about grace. His Tablet in the obligatory-prayer compilation says “Each morn God’s infinite grace confirmeth” His own invocations, and that anyone can obtain a share by praying “at every dawn.”
  • A description of the hour in prayer. In Bahá’u’lláh’s Fast prayer it is the hour when “the doors of Thy bounty” are “unlocked” and the cup of remembrance is “reserved.”

Adib Taherzadeh writes in commentary that “Bahá’u’lláh has particularly indicated the merits of attending the service before dawn; prayers said at that time have a special potency” (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2, note), and that dawn is “a time which is specially conducive to spiritual upliftment” (vol. 3). I did not find the Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh that Taherzadeh may have in mind, so I treat these as his reading of the Writings and not as a text of the Writings.

The Star of the West passage. If the passage in question is the one in volume 8, number 4, it is short and is quoted above in full. I searched the Star of the West volumes, Bahá’í News, The Bahá’í World, the compilations, Selections, Tablets of Abdul-Baha Abbas and the House of Justice messages in this corpus for a longer explanation by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá of why dawn is the right hour (for example, one about stillness, about the world being asleep, or about the sun). I did not find one. If one exists, it is likely in a source not held here, such as a pilgrim’s diary.

The “Greatest Name at first waking.” A sentence about the Greatest Name being “found upon the lips in the first awakening moment of early dawn” circulates in compilations. In the sources here it is traced to Bahá’í News, October 1964, and is not attributed there to a Tablet, so it is not used.

Where the hour has been kept. The Aqdas and the Guardian speak of dawn prayer in the House of Worship. The House of Justice, in its letter of 18 December 2014 to the Bahá’ís of Iran, adds that in the Writings the term “Mashriqu’l-Adhkár” has been used for “the gathering of the believers for prayers at dawn”, among other meanings, and that “within Iran too the practice of regular dawn prayers took root and inspired service to humankind”. So the hour has been kept in homes and meeting places, in many countries, long before most communities had a Temple.
