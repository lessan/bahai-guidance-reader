The Authenticated Word: Why Pilgrims’ Notes Are Not Scripture

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

A Bahá’í may read a pilgrim’s account of an afternoon with ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, be moved by it, keep it, copy it, and share it — and may not quote it as the Faith’s teaching. That combination is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly: the Bahá’í community neither canonises its treasured recollections nor suppresses them. It labels them. What is withheld from a pilgrim’s note is authority, never access. Shoghi Effendi, who refused to sign any pilgrim’s notes at all, also insisted that friends who wished to circulate them “should, under no circumstances, be prevented from doing so.”

My position on the central question: this discipline is not archival fastidiousness and it is not a defensive crouch. It is a structural provision of the Covenant, and it is load-bearing in a specific, mechanical sense — it removes the raw material from which clergies and sects are made. An unauthenticated oral tradition must be adjudicated by somebody; whoever adjudicates it becomes, functionally, a priesthood. And a body of floating, unverifiable sayings is the standing inventory from which every schism in religious history has drawn its proof-texts. The Bahá’í Faith closes that door not by inventing a rule after the fact but by making the written, signed, sealed, or corrected text the sole basis of belief — and then building the archives, the Research Department, and the translation programme that make the rule enforceable rather than aspirational. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states it with a bluntness that leaves no working room: “Any narrative that is not authenticated by a Text should not be trusted. Narratives, even if true, cause confusion. For the people of Bahá, the Text, and only the Text, is authentic.”

Note the clause a careless reader will skip: even if true. The objection is not to falsehood. It is to unverifiability as a basis for common life. That is the whole argument compressed into five words, and everything below is its unpacking.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest on a reader of 2026: the failure mode of our era is no longer a mimeographed pilgrim’s note passed hand to hand. It is a fluent, plausible, beautifully cadenced sentence with no provenance at all, arriving in a group chat, a slide deck, or the output of a language model — and plausibility is precisely what a bad attribution optimises for. The highest-leverage habit is therefore not scepticism about content but discipline about locators: never let a quotation enter your own repertoire without knowing which Tablet, which letter, which date. For anyone who builds software, the corresponding design rule is severe and simple — a tool that handles Bahá’í text should never generate a quotation, only retrieve one with an exact citation, and should refuse when it cannot.

“The Text, and only the Text”

The rule that governs everything else was given by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in response to a direct question about pilgrims and their notes, and it is remarkable for how little room it leaves. The Tablet — cited by the International Teaching Centre and in Lights of Guidance as a previously untranslated Tablet of the Master — reads in full:

“Thou hast written concerning the pilgrims and pilgrims’ notes. Any narrative that is not authenticated by a Text should not be trusted. Narratives, even if true, cause confusion. For the people of Bahá, the Text, and only the Text, is authentic.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, from a previously untranslated Tablet

Two moves are made here, and only the first is obvious. The first is exclusion: a narrative without a Text behind it is not to be trusted. The second is the reason given, and it is not the reason most people would supply. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not say that narratives are usually false. He says that narratives cause confusion — even if true. The problem being solved is not deception; it is the impossibility of a shared, checkable basis for belief once the community begins reasoning from material that no two people can verify against the same object.

The Guardian named the historical mechanism this rule forecloses. Writing on his behalf to the United States Publishing Committee on 29 December 1931, his secretary set out the principle and then its purpose:

“Bahá’u’lláh has made it clear enough that only those things that have been revealed in the form of Tablets have a binding power over the friends. Hearsays may be matters of interest but can in no way claim authority. This basic teaching of Bahá’u’lláh was to preserve the Faith from being corrupted like Islam which attributes binding authority to all the reported sayings of Muḥammad.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 29 December 1931

The comparison is about a transmission mechanism, not about a rival religion’s worth, and Shoghi Effendi’s own writing settles that beyond argument. In The Advent of Divine Justice, his letter of 25 December 1938 to the Bahá’ís of the West, he directs believers to approach “reverently and with a mind purged from preconceived ideas the study of the Qur’án which, apart from the sacred scriptures of the Bábí and Bahá’í Revelations, constitutes the only Book which can be regarded as an absolutely authenticated Repository of the Word of God.” The Qur’án is honoured on exactly the criterion this article is about. What the 1931 letter warns against is the secondary layer that grew around it — a vast corpus of reported sayings whose grading became, over centuries, a specialist science, and whose custody became a source of authority for the specialists who held it.

That is the structural point. Where a religion carries a large body of unauthenticated report, someone must decide which reports count. That someone acquires a power the founder never granted. The Bahá’í provision does not make Bahá’ís better readers than anyone else; it removes the job.

What makes a text authentic: the seal, the signature, the correction

The positive criterion is physical and boringly concrete, which is its virtue. Writing on the Guardian’s behalf on 18 November 1931, his secretary put it in a sentence:

“The Tablets that bear the seal or signature of Bahá’u’lláh and the Master are the only parts of the literature that have any authority and that constitute the basis of our belief. All other forms of literature may bear points of interest but they cannot be considered as authentic.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 18 November 1931

The founding document of the Covenant itself was handled with exactly this evidentiary care, and Shoghi Effendi records the procedure in God Passes By. Of the Kitáb-i-’Ahd he writes:

“Written entirely in His own hand; unsealed, on the ninth day after His ascension in the presence of nine witnesses chosen from amongst His companions and members of His Family; read subsequently, on the afternoon of that same day, before a large company assembled in His Most Holy Tomb… this unique and epoch-making Document, designated by Bahá’u’lláh as His ‘Most Great Tablet’… can find no parallel in the Scriptures of any previous Dispensation.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By

Autograph, sealed, unsealed before named witnesses, read aloud to an assembly the same afternoon: the succession of the Faith rests on a document whose chain of custody is a matter of record rather than of memory.

The other half of the picture is stranger and more instructive. The written corpus is not everything Bahá’u’lláh uttered — by His own decision. Nabíl, living in Baghdád during the years after Bahá’u’lláh’s return from Sulaymáníyyih, reported that the unrecorded verses streaming from His lips averaged, in a single day and night, the equivalent of the Qur’án. And Mírzá Áqá Ján, His amanuensis, told Nabíl that by Bahá’u’lláh’s express order hundreds of thousands of verses, mostly in His own hand, were obliterated and cast into the river. “Finding me reluctant to execute His orders,” Mírzá Áqá Ján related, “Bahá’u’lláh would reassure me saying: ‘None is to be found at this time worthy to hear these melodies.’ … Not once, or twice, but innumerable times, was I commanded to repeat this act.”

The canon, in other words, is bounded by the Revealer’s own act, not merely by the accidents of preservation. What survived is still enormous — the Universal House of Justice’s introduction to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas speaks of “the more than one hundred volumes comprising the sacred Writings of Bahá’u’lláh.” But a community that knows the Author Himself withheld material has a ready answer to the perennial suspicion that something was suppressed by later hands. Withholding was part of the design, and it was done at the top.

Three grades of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s utterance

The most practically consequential application of the criterion concerns ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks, because that is where beloved and widely quoted books sit. The same 29 December 1931 letter sets out the grading:

“Those talks of the Master that were later reviewed by Him, corrected or in some other form considered authentic by Himself, such as the ‘Some Answered Questions’, these could be considered as Tablets and therefore be given the necessary binding power. All the other talks such as are included in Aḥmad’s diary or the diary of pilgrims, do not fall under this category and could be considered only as interesting material to be taken for what they are worth.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 29 December 1931

So there are three grades, not two: Tablets in His hand or under His seal; talks He read and corrected, which become Tablets in force; and talks recorded by others and never authenticated, which are interesting material. The middle grade is the one worth dwelling on, because it is the mechanism by which what began as a pilgrim’s notes became scripture.

Some Answered Questions is the worked example, and its foreword tells the story precisely. Laura Clifford Barney, introduced to the Faith by May Bolles Maxwell in Paris around 1900, made several extended visits to ‘Akká during 1904–1906 — the most dangerous years of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s ministry, under Ottoman surveillance and the threat of exile or execution. Many of the conversations took place at the lunch table. Arrangements were made for one of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s sons-in-law, or for one of His three secretaries of that period, to take down the replies in Persian. Then came the step that changes everything:

“From the resulting collection of notes a selection was made; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá then corrected these notes twice in His own hand, sometimes substantially revising them in the process as well as carefully reviewing the final wording.” — Foreword, Some Answered Questions

Corrected twice, in His own hand, with substantial revision. That is what authentication looks like as an act rather than a category. Three first editions followed in 1908 — the Persian original from E. J. Brill in Holland, Barney’s English translation from Kegan Paul in London, and Hippolyte Dreyfus’s French translation from Ernest Leroux in Paris. A century later, for the centenary of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s journeys to the West, a committee at the Bahá’í World Centre issued a thoroughly revised English translation, on the reasoning that Barney was “a student of the Persian language and, however able, could not have entirely mastered its intricacies,” and could not have drawn on the illumination later cast on the Texts by Shoghi Effendi’s own translations.

Set beside that, consider ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in London. Asked about it, the Guardian answered through his secretary on 24 October 1947, in a letter to the National Spiritual Assembly of the British Isles:

“Nothing can be considered scripture for which we do not have an original text. A verbatim record in Persian of His talks would of course be more reliable than one in English, because He was not always accurately interpreted. However such a book is of value, and certainly has its place in our Literature.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 24 October 1947 (Unfolding Destiny, p. 208)

Two sentences, two rulings, and they do not cancel each other. Not scripture; genuinely valuable; keep it in the literature. This is the pattern the whole system runs on, and it is worth naming before going further: grade, do not purge.

The talks that are still being sorted

The most widely read of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Western talks — The Promulgation of Universal Peace and Paris Talks — sit in the unresolved zone, and the House of Justice has said so directly. A letter written on its behalf on 23 March 1987 sets out both the situation and the intention:

“The original of ‘Some Answered Questions’ in Persian is preserved in the Holy Land; its text was read in full and corrected by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Himself. Unfortunately, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá did not read and authenticate all transcripts of His other talks… For many of His addresses included in ‘The Promulgation of Universal Peace’ and ‘Paris Talks’, for example, no original authenticated text has yet been found. However, the Guardian allowed such compilations to continue to be used by the friends. In the future each talk will have to be identified and those which are unauthenticated will have to be clearly distinguished from those which form a part of Bahá’í Scripture. This does not mean that the unauthenticated talks will have to cease to be used — merely that the degree of authenticity of every document will have to be known and understood.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 23 March 1987

That last clause is the design philosophy in a line: the degree of authenticity of every document will have to be known and understood. Not restricted. Not withdrawn. Known.

The same 1996 Research Department memorandum that transmits this ruling adds the honest administrative footnote: asked which extracts in Foundations of World Unity and Bahá’í World Faith are authentic, the Department replied that “such a listing has not been compiled” and invited enquiry about specific passages. Thirty years on, the sorting is still in progress. That is a limit worth stating rather than glossing.

The tiers, named plainly

Because the word authentic is often heard as a synonym for authoritative, it helps to separate them before laying out the ladder. Authentic means the communication really is from the source it claims. Authoritative means it binds within its domain. A Secretariat acknowledgement of a donation is authentic and not authoritative; a doctrinal letter from the same Department is both. The full treatment of the authority axis is at bahai-authority-framework and AuthorityAndInfallibility; what follows is the authentication axis, which is the one this page is about.

Three of those rows carry facts a reader is likely to want in hand.

The Guardian’s secretaries. Asked in 1930 to settle whether letters signed by Rúḥí or Suhayl really carried his authority, Shoghi Effendi added a postscript in his own hand to a letter of 7 December 1930: “I wish to add and say that whatever letters are sent in my behalf from Haifa are all read and approved by me before mailing. There is no exception whatever to this rule.” The rule is total — and yet the same corpus refuses to flatten the difference. A letter of 25 February 1951 to the National Spiritual Assembly of the British Isles observes that although “the secretaries of the Guardian convey his thoughts and instructions and these messages are authoritative,” their words “are in no sense the same as his, their style certainly not the same, and their authority less, for they use their own terms and not his exact words in conveying his messages.” Authentic, authoritative, and still a step removed in phrasing. A third letter, of 17 October 1944, draws the boundary from the other side: “The infallibility of the Guardian is confined to matters which are related strictly to the Cause and interpretation of the teachings; he is not an infallible authority on other subjects, such as economics, science, etc.” — and personal advice about a believer’s own future “is not binding; you are free to follow it or not as you please.”

Signed letters versus Secretariat letters. The House of Justice described its own practice with unusual procedural candour in a letter of 22 October 1996: “Drafts of letters which contain newly formulated policies are consulted upon and approved during a meeting of the House of Justice; correspondence dealing with previously enunciated policies, or with matters of a routine nature, are prepared, as delegated by the House of Justice, by its Secretariat and initialed by at least the majority of the members of the House of Justice before being dispatched. All letters written over the signature of the Department of the Secretariat are authorized by the Universal House of Justice.” Initialled by a majority before dispatch — the difference is one of register and formality, not of channel.

The Research Department’s own tier. The same 1996 letter is scrupulous about the body that does the authenticating. Research Department materials, “though prepared at its direction, represent the views of that Department. While such views are very useful as an aid to resolving perplexities or gaining an enhanced understanding of the Bahá’í Teachings, they should never be taken to be in the same category as the elucidations and clarifications provided by the Universal House of Justice.” This is the most easily missed distinction on the page. A Research Department memorandum saying a prayer is unauthenticated carries evidentiary weight — it is the best-informed finding available — but not doctrinal weight. The institution that certifies the Text is not thereby elevated above it.

Read, cherished, and stripped of authority

Everything above could describe a suspicious institution locking things away. The actual record is the opposite, and it is the part of this story most often told wrongly.

Start with the Guardian’s refusal, because the reason he gave is not the one people assume. Writing on his behalf to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States, his secretary explained:

“The Guardian has stated that he is unwilling to sign the notes of any pilgrim, in order that the literature consulted by the believers shall not be unduly extended… This means that the notes of pilgrims do not carry the authority resident in the Guardian’s letters written over his own signature. On the other hand each pilgrim brings back information and suggestions of a most precious character, and it is the privilege of all the friends to share in the spiritual results of these visits.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, Bahá’í News no. 281, July 1954

So that the literature consulted by the believers shall not be unduly extended. The concern is the size of the shelf a serious believer must master — a pastoral concern about attention, not a proprietary one about control. And the second half of the passage grants the sharing outright: the privilege of all the friends.

He went further when a National Assembly appeared minded to restrict circulation. Asked in 1939 about the Haifa notes collected by May Maxwell, his secretary replied:

“These have exactly the same status as all other pilgrims’ notes, and as such there should be no objection to their circulation among the believers. While these notes taken down by the pilgrims do not constitute as official pronouncements made by the Guardian, and therefore should not be imposed on the friends, those who wish to share them with the members of the Community should, under no circumstances, be prevented from doing so. Though not strictly official, and in some instances inaccurate and misleading, these notes, as experience has shown, can be of tremendous help, guidance and inspiration to many individual believers, and their value as such should therefore be readily admitted.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 28 April 1939

Read that as a policy and it is an oddly balanced one. Not official. Sometimes inaccurate and misleading. Of tremendous help. Not to be imposed. Not to be prevented. Readily admitted. Four verbs, pulling in different directions, held together by the single distinction between authority and access. The same balance appears in the ruling on stories about the Master, from a letter of 2 October 1935 to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States and Canada: such stories “should be regarded in the same light as the notes and impressions of visiting pilgrims. They need not be suppressed, but they should not also be given prominence or official recognition.”

What the Guardian did insist on was that nobody mistake his conversation for his guidance. From a letter of 26 February 1933: “Shoghi Effendi has often said that the notes of the pilgrims should be for their own personal use and bear absolutely no authority. What he desires to convey to the friends at large he will always say in his general letters.” And when a believer produced notes he acknowledged to be accurate, the answer was still no — a letter of 17 February 1938 told her that although her notes were “substantially correct, … he does not advise that you circulate them as constituting his own words.”

The House of Justice later supplied the sentence that does the most work in practice, in a letter of 23 January 1980:

“The instructions of the Master and the Guardian make it very clear that Pilgrims’ notes are hearsay and cannot claim the authority and binding power of the Sacred Text…. Moreover, the fact that the pilgrim writing of his experience is a reliable or well-known believer, or that the reported statement seems to be repeated in the notes of several pilgrims, does not in itself confer authority upon the pilgrim’s note in question.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 23 January 1980

That second sentence is an anti-heuristic, and it is aimed squarely at how human beings actually decide what to believe. We trust the source’s reputation; we trust convergence across independent witnesses. Both instincts are excellent in ordinary life and both are explicitly disallowed here. A Hand of the Cause’s recollection is a pilgrim’s note. Six pilgrims recording the same sentence is six pilgrims’ notes. Neither social proof nor corroboration can manufacture the one thing that would settle the matter — a text.

1984: what an ungoverned oral tradition actually costs

Principles argued in the abstract are easy to nod at. The clearest demonstration of the cost is a documented episode, and the International Teaching Centre wrote it up on 1 July 1984 in a letter to the Continental Board of Counsellors in the Americas. (The Teaching Centre operates under the House of Justice’s direction; its guidance carries institutional weight, not the House’s own — the authorities it quotes do the binding work.)

Counsellor Peter Khan had just completed a visit to North America. He reported that he had met believers “who appeared preoccupied with a fear of impending calamity, based largely on views derived from pilgrim notes, and that this fear was diverting these believers from the teaching work and the pursuit of the goals of the Plan.”

Here is what makes the case instructive: the underlying notes were not false. Shoghi Effendi had in fact spoken to pilgrims about the danger to great cities in a future war. Thirty years earlier he had had to say so himself, through his secretary, in a letter of 20 June 1954 to the American National Spiritual Assembly:

“He does not feel that the Bahá’ís should waste time dwelling on the dark side of things. Any intelligent person can understand from the experiences of the last world war, and keeping abreast of what modern science has developed in the way of weapons for any future war, that big cities all over the world are going to be in tremendous danger. This is what the Guardian has said to the pilgrims. … It is remarks such as these that the pilgrims have carried back in their notes. He sees no cause for alarm, but he certainly believes that the Bahá’ís should weigh these thoughts, and take action for the sake of spreading the Faith of Bahá’u’lláh, and for their own ultimate happiness as well.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 20 June 1954

A sober observation that any intelligent person could make, offered in the context of an appeal to disperse from the cities and teach, arrived back in America as a prophecy of doom. Nothing was fabricated. What was lost was proportion, occasion, and emphasis — precisely the things a note cannot carry and a letter can. Three decades later the residue was still circulating, and it was costing the community its attention.

The Teaching Centre’s framing of the general lesson is the sharpest single formulation in the whole corpus:

“The history of religion clearly illustrates the misunderstanding, division and error that have resulted from reliance on verbal statements as a source of authoritative knowledge of religious teachings. One of the precious bounties of this Dispensation is that the door has been closed, clearly and unambiguously, to such a source of problems.” — International Teaching Centre, 1 July 1984

A bounty. Not a restriction to be endured — a gift, and a comparative one, offered against the background of what happened to every previous Dispensation that lacked it.

2013: why the internet changed the calculus without changing the rule

The most substantial modern statement on pilgrims’ notes is a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice on 12 August 2013, to a believer asking whether he might post pilgrimage accounts online. Its reasoning proceeds in four steps, and each one adds something the older letters left implicit.

First, the epistemic limit is intrinsic, not incidental. “As personal impressions, such notes are always filtered through the individual’s perception and can never be fully accurate representations of the words of the Guardian.” No amount of care by the note-taker closes this gap; it is a property of the medium.

Second, even accurate notes are the wrong shape. Where notes do faithfully represent the Guardian’s sentiments, “they are excerpts, taken out of context, from informal and sometimes private conversations that were never meant to be public statements.” This is the 1954 calamity case generalised into a principle.

Third — and this is the step I find most striking — silence is treated as intentional. Since whatever the Guardian wished to communicate publicly he wrote in his letters, “if information is contained in pilgrims’ notes which is not also found in his written messages, it is not unreasonable to infer that it was not information that he wished to communicate to the community as a whole — much less to the general public.” Read that carefully. It inverts the instinct that makes pilgrims’ notes exciting in the first place. Friends prize them for the very thing the letter identifies as the disqualifier: material found nowhere in the written guidance. On this reading, an unpublished remark is not a leak of something withheld from us; it is evidence of a judgement about timeliness and audience that we are being asked to respect.

The letter grounds that judgement in Bahá’u’lláh’s own practice. From the Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd: “Every word is endowed with a spirit, therefore the speaker or expounder should carefully deliver his words at the appropriate time and place, for the impression which each word maketh is clearly evident and perceptible.” And from Gleanings, on His own restraint in disclosure:

“How great the multitude of truths which the garment of words can never contain! How vast the number of such verities as no expression can adequately describe, whose significance can never be unfolded, and to which not even the remotest allusions can be made! How manifold are the truths which must remain unuttered until the appointed time is come!” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, section LXXXIX

If the Manifestation of God exercises discretion about what to disclose and when, a believer with a notebook is not the appropriate authority to overrule it.

Fourth, the medium matters even when the rule does not change. The letter is explicit that the concern “is even more pronounced when moving from an era when the dissemination of such material was necessarily limited and normally involved the passing of typed copies from one individual to another, to one in which material can be distributed widely and instantly and compiled in a manner that may create significant misconceptions.” Note the specific hazard named: not merely wide distribution but compilation. Individually harmless fragments, gathered and arranged by someone with a thesis, become an argument that no single note could have made. Anyone who has watched a curated thread of decontextualised quotations do its work online will recognise the mechanism exactly.

What the House did not do is instructive. Asked in 1997 whether there were rules for publishing pilgrims’ notes on the World Wide Web, the reply of 14 December was that “while wisdom should continue to be used in publishing and disseminating pilgrims’ notes, the House of Justice has not formulated any guidelines or policies specifically addressed to the content of World Wide Web sites,” with blatantly inappropriate content referable to the relevant National Spiritual Assembly. Sixteen years later, the 2013 letter still declines to legislate a rule and instead commends the believer for having consulted his National Assembly first. The discipline is deliberately placed in the individual’s judgement, exercised in consultation with the institutions — not in a code of practice. That is a design decision about where responsibility lives, and it is consistent with a Faith that has no clergy to enforce a code.

The 2013 letter closes on two considerations easily forgotten in arguments about openness: the responsibility owed “towards those Bahá’í communities where the friends are in a precarious situation,” and “the privacy of his informal conversations with the pilgrims.” Not every argument for restraint is an argument about doctrine. Some are about people who can be arrested for what appears on a website, and about a man’s right not to have his dinner-table conversation published.

The archives: what a religion does when it decides to keep the receipts

A rule that only says no is cheap. What makes the Bahá’í discipline more than a slogan is that the community built the apparatus to make authentication possible — and started early, at cost, under pressure.

Shoghi Effendi made it an administrative task of the emerging Assemblies. God Passes By records, among the achievements of the Faith’s first century, the establishment of National Archives “for the authentication, the collection, the translation, the cataloguing and the preservation of the Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh and of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá,” alongside “the verification and transcription of the original Tablets of the Báb, of Bahá’u’lláh and of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in the possession of Oriental believers.” Authentication heads the list — before collection, before translation, before cataloguing.

Two international Archives were founded on Mount Carmel, one adjoining the Shrine of the Báb, the other near the resting-place of the Greatest Holy Leaf, gathering treasures “hitherto scattered and often hidden for safekeeping.” Among them: part of the Hidden Words in Bahá’u’lláh’s own hand, the Persian Bayán in the handwriting of Siyyid Ḥusayn — the Báb’s amanuensis — the original Tablets to the Letters of the Living penned by the Báb, and the manuscript of Some Answered Questions. And, in a detail that says a great deal about how the work was actually done: “no less than twenty volumes of prayers and Tablets revealed by the Founders of the Faith, authenticated and transcribed by Bahá’í Assemblies throughout the Orient.” Ordinary Assemblies in Persian towns, verifying and copying originals, volume by volume.

The purpose-built institutions followed on a timeline worth laying out, because it shows a community spending its scarce resources on this problem for seventy years without interruption.

The 1970 entry is the one that reveals priorities. The House of Justice’s letter of 29 December 1970 announced a grave crisis in the Bahá’í International Fund and a drastic reduction in expenditure. Gardens at Bahjí: postponed. Terraces below the Shrine of the Báb: postponed. Additional World Centre offices: postponed. Assistance to National Assemblies: cut by ten percent for two quarters. And the work on the International Archives Building — “necessary to protect the precious Tablets and relics from the high humidity and increasingly polluted atmosphere of Haifa city” — was completed. When a community with no money chooses what to finish, it tells you what it thinks it is for.

The translation programme is the visible harvest. The House forwarded Gems of Divine Mysteries to National Assemblies as the first volume in the new schedule under the Five Year Plan; The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, The Tabernacle of Unity, Days of Remembrance, The Call of the Divine Beloved, Light of the World and Twelve Table Talks Given by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in ‘Akká have followed. And in Bahá’í Sacred Writings the pattern of this article is visible in the volume’s own accounting of itself: 257 selections from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, 241 passages from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá — “including a few selections from His utterances.” Even a devotional anthology marks the seam.

The unresolved part: access

Honesty requires the other side. In December 1998 the scholar Susan Maneck wrote to the House of Justice asking how tight control over access to primary sources at the World Centre could be reconciled with the principle of the unfettered search after truth. The reply of 4 May 1999 is the fullest statement available on the question, and it neither dismisses the tension nor pretends to dissolve it.

The custodial framing comes first. Source documents at the World Centre “are held by the Universal House of Justice in trust for the entire Bahá’í world and ultimately for the whole of humankind, of both present and future generations.” Then the practical obstacle: “There is tremendous work to be accomplished in sorting, identifying and cataloguing such documents so that they can be effectively studied without either damaging them or losing vital information by disturbing their inter-relationships” — and, given finite resources, “the primary work in this respect must be devoted to the Sacred Texts rather than to documents of historical interest.” Hence the ruling: “It would be irresponsible for the House of Justice, without itself first being fully informed of what is in the Archives, to consider opening them to individual scholars for the pursuit of purely personal interests.”

Two further points from that letter belong on the record. The archives are not closed in principle: the House states its intention that “in due course, it will be possible to publish editions of historical documents in facsimile accompanied, in the case of each document, by a printed transcription, and supplemented by necessary commentaries and notes.” And documents held elsewhere are entirely open — “access to source documents relating to the Bahá’í Faith which are held in libraries in different parts of the world, or are in the hands of individuals, is open to anyone who wishes to consult them,” with the House noting that tracing such deposits and obtaining archival-quality photocopies for the World Centre has been “a major service which a number of Bahá’í scholars have rendered to the Faith.”

Then the sentence that does not soften itself: “You refer to the principle of the unfettered search after truth. This is certainly upheld, but it cannot imply that the institutions of the Faith have a duty to make available to each enquirer every piece of information he or she requests.”

A reader may find that satisfying or may not. What should not be in dispute is that the position is stated, with reasons, in a letter the House chose to publish. The tension between custodial integrity and open access is real, it is acknowledged at the highest level, and the timetable for resolving it is open. It belongs in this article’s honest limits, not in its argument.

What the discipline protects

Pulling the threads together, the authentication rule does at least five distinct jobs. They are worth separating, because they are usually collapsed into a vague sense of “the Faith is careful about texts.”

It removes the raw material of clergy. This is the deepest function and the one argued at the top. A body of unauthenticated report requires graders; graders acquire standing; standing becomes office. The Bahá’í Faith has no clergy by explicit provision — and one of the ways it stays without clergy is by never generating the corpus that would need one.

It removes the raw material of schism. The first assault on the Covenant after Bahá’u’lláh’s ascension was, quite literally, an attack on the text. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names it in His Will and Testament among the gravest of Mírzá Muḥammad-‘Alí’s deeds: he “hath falsified the Holy Text.” The Will asks the question rhetorically — “What deviation can be greater than interpolating and falsifying the words and verses of the Sacred Text, even as testified and declared by Mírzá Badí‘u’lláh!” — and the forensic detail in God Passes By is chilling in its ordinariness. He had, when sent on a mission to India, “tampered with the text of the holy writings entrusted to his care for publication.” While Bahá’u’lláh’s body still awaited interment he had “carried off, by a ruse, the two satchels containing his Father’s most precious documents.” And “by an exceedingly adroit and simple forgery of a word recurring in some of the denunciatory passages addressed by the Supreme Pen to Mírzá Yaḥyá, and by other devices such as mutilation and interpolation,” he had made those passages appear to condemn ‘Abdu’l-Bahá.

A forgery of a word. One word, recurring, altered. That is the scale at which a Covenant can be attacked, and it is why the archives are a covenantal institution rather than a librarian’s hobby. Authentication is the immune system. Related material on how the community handles Covenant-breaking content circulating today is at covenant-breaking-material-online.

It protects the individual from unfalsifiable claims. The 1980 anti-heuristic — reputation and repetition confer nothing — is a defence of the ordinary believer against being told what the Faith teaches by whoever has the most compelling anecdote or the most impressive circle of acquaintance. In a community without clergy, the alternative to textual authority is not freedom; it is charisma.

It protects the guidance from inflation. A community that treats every recollection as guidance ends up with more obligations than the Lawgiver imposed, and eventually with a devotional life built on a hearsay chain. The House of Justice’s warning in another connection applies exactly — that believers should take care “lest they go to extremes, by either diminishing its station or assigning to it exaggerated attributes.” The same caution governs texts. Inflating a note into scripture damages the community as surely as dismissing a genuine Tablet.

It keeps the community’s attention on the work. The 1984 episode is the demonstration. The measurable harm reported to the Teaching Centre was not heresy; it was diversion — believers whose energies were absorbed by dread instead of by the goals of the Plan. Guidance that is actually binding is comparatively sober, and it tends to point outward, toward service (bahai-plans).

There is one further consideration, and honesty requires making it against my own convenience. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s most quotable statements about investigating reality rather than relying on hearsay — “man must forget all hearsay and examine truth himself” — occur in The Promulgation of Universal Peace, which is itself in the unauthenticated class. I have not built any argument on those passages, and a reader should notice that I could have, easily, and that it would have been the wrong move. The discipline costs something. Some of the most rhetorically useful sentences in the Bahá’í literature are ones a careful writer must decline to lean on. Being willing to pay that price on a page whose subject is exactly this is the minimum test of whether one believes it.

What this asks of you

The rule is easy to state and moderately demanding to keep. Here is what keeping it looks like in the settings a believer actually occupies.

Read the pilgrims’ notes. They are a genuine bounty, and the Guardian defended your right to them against Assemblies who wished to restrict their circulation. Nothing in this article argues for reading less; the whole architecture exists so that the community can read widely without being confused about what binds.

Say the provenance in the same breath. When you quote at a Feast, in a study circle, in a talk or a post, the words “this is a pilgrim’s note” cost four words and settle everything. The pastoral instinct that fears sounding pedantic has it backwards: the label is what makes it safe to share the beautiful thing.

Refuse the three false signals. Not from a well-known believer. Not from repetition across several sources. Not from a passage sounding exactly like the Guardian — that last is the weakest signal of all, because a plausible cadence is precisely what a misattribution has been selected for. The only thing that settles it is a locator.

Carry locators, not just quotations. Which Tablet. Which letter. Which date. Which paragraph. The habit takes a week to form and permanently changes what you are able to say responsibly.

Ask when you cannot source something. The friends who did this produced a public good. A notice in The American Bahá’í in September 1992 reported the Research Department’s findings on prayers and “tablets” then circulating in typed form, and the list is a useful education in how attribution actually fails. Two well-loved prayers turned out to have been written by Ibrahim Kheiralla, a Covenant-breaker, and attributed by him to Bahá’u’lláh. The much-used “Marriage Tablet” beginning “The bond that unites hearts most perfectly is loyalty” is “not a tablet at all, but is an unauthenticated record by Ahmad Sohrab of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá” — usable, the notice said, provided it is made clear that it is not scripture. And “Fill Thou, O God, our home with harmony and happiness”, attributed for decades to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, is in fact three short supplications from pages 147–148 of George Townshend’s The Mission of Bahá’u’lláh. A separate Secretariat letter of 28 February 2000 noted that a prayer beginning “O my Glorious Lord! Help me to refrain from every irregular inclination” — printed in early prayer books and in World Order magazine in May 1940 — has no located original and is therefore not included in new publications.

None of those errors was malicious. Every one of them came from someone who loved the words.

If you build software, build for provenance. This is where a technically capable Bahá’í has leverage that nobody else has, and where the field is genuinely unserved.

The contemporary hazard is measurable, and it should be read strictly as description of the field rather than as authority for anything. A study published in JMIR Mental Health on 12 November 2025 by Jake Linardon and colleagues at Deakin University examined citations generated by GPT-4o across six simulated literature reviews: 19.9% of all citations were entirely fabricated, and among the citations that referred to real papers, 45.4% contained bibliographic errors. Fabrication varied sharply with the model’s familiarity with the topic — 6% for major depressive disorder, 29% for body dysmorphic disorder. Extrapolate the pattern to a corpus far smaller and less represented in training data than the psychiatric literature, and the expected behaviour of a general-purpose model asked for “a quotation from Bahá’u’lláh on X” is obvious. It will produce something beautiful, plausible, correctly cadenced, and unlocatable. That is a machine for manufacturing pilgrims’ notes at industrial scale, with none of the human sincerity that made the originals worth keeping.

The design response follows directly from the 1987 ruling — the degree of authenticity of every document will have to be known and understood — and it is unusually specific:

  • Never generate a quotation. Retrieve one. The system should be physically incapable of emitting quoted text that did not come out of an indexed corpus with an exact locator attached. When it cannot find one, the correct output is “I cannot locate this,” not a paraphrase in quotation marks.
  • Make provenance a first-class field, not a footnote. Work, paragraph, date, recipient, translation status, authentication status. A record without those fields is incomplete data, not merely under-documented data.
  • Grade, do not purge. Mirror the institutional pattern exactly: surface unauthenticated talks and pilgrims’ notes with a visible standing label rather than excluding them. Excluding them recreates the problem elsewhere, in less careful hands.
  • Make the label travel with the excerpt. The 2013 letter’s warning about compilation is a warning about copy-paste. If a quotation can be lifted out of your interface without its grade, your interface is a distribution channel for the failure mode.

Precedent exists, built by individuals rather than institutions, and it is worth studying before starting from scratch. Bahá’í Library Online stamps every pilgrim’s-note page with a standing notice — “This is a ‘Pilgrim’s note,’ an individual’s recollection of statements and actions of the Central figures. They are subjective and not authoritative” — and its Pilgrim Accounts Collection assembles 253 such documents in one indexed table, sorted for duplicates and variant manuscripts. Steven Phelps’s Partial Inventory of the Works of the Central Figures catalogues over 29,000 writings and recorded utterances of the Báb, Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá; the notebook interface built on it instructs users, before beginning, to deselect the three source files of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s recorded utterances “which may not be authenticated.” That instruction is a provenance filter implemented as a checkbox — crude, and exactly the right idea.

And if you build nothing at all, the highest-leverage contribution is still available and costs nothing: be the person in the study circle who asks, warmly and without embarrassment, “where is that from?” — and who then goes and finds out. In a community that has deliberately declined to appoint anyone to police its texts, that question is how the discipline actually gets kept.

Open questions / honest limits

  • The 1987 undertaking is not complete. The commitment that “each talk will have to be identified” and unauthenticated talks “clearly distinguished” from scripture has not, so far as this knowledge base can establish, produced a published comprehensive list. The Research Department stated in 1996 that no such listing existed for Foundations of World Unity and Bahá’í World Faith and invited enquiry about individual passages. The current state of that work is not known to this page.
  • The keystone quotation is itself imperfectly located. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablet — “the Text, and only the Text, is authentic” — is cited by both the International Teaching Centre and Lights of Guidance as “a previously untranslated Tablet,” with no recipient or date given. By its own standard it is under-specified. Naming that is more useful than pretending otherwise; the ruling is transmitted through authoritative institutional channels, but a reader entitled to a locator does not have one.
  • Several letters here are known through compilations, not originals. Lights of Guidance is a personal compilation by Helen Hornby (1988, revised 1994); a number of the letters written on the Guardian’s behalf cited above are known to this corpus through it or through Bahá’í Library Online rather than through an official publication of the originals. Dates and recipients are given as recorded there.
  • The relation between a letter written on the Guardian’s behalf and one over his own signature is not fully settled in the sources consulted here. The 1951 letter says the secretaries’ authority is “less”; the 1930 postscript says every such letter was read and approved without exception. This page does not attempt to derive a general conflict-resolution rule from those two facts, because the sources here do not supply one.
  • The archives-access question is open, and the House says so. The 4 May 1999 letter states the custodial reasoning and the intention to publish facsimile editions “in due course,” without a timetable. Readers with scholarly interests will find the reasoning either persuasive or not; this page reports it rather than adjudicating it.
  • The external research will age, and grounds nothing. The GPT-4o citation-fabrication figures come from a single 2025 study in one clinical domain. They describe the contemporary field and support no Bahá’í principle whatever. Model behaviour changes quickly; the underlying design lesson — retrieve, do not generate — does not depend on the numbers holding.
  • Adjacent matters this page does not cover: the review of Bahá’í literature prior to publication; translation policy and the standing of Shoghi Effendi’s English renderings as themselves interpretive; the status of the Persian transcripts of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Western talks, which stand on a different footing from their English records; and the authority hierarchy proper, which is treated at bahai-authority-framework and AuthorityAndInfallibility.
