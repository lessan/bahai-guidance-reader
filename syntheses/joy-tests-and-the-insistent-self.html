Joy, Tests and the Insistent Self: What the Writings Say About Losing and Regaining Momentum

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page is for

This page is for committed believers who know the pattern from the inside: wholly devoted to Bahá’u’lláh, yet at times struggling to bring the self under control and drifting into a routine of distraction and habitual reward-seeking. What brings many back is the Word of God, read and shared, and the image of shaking the mud off one’s wings to take flight again. The pattern is the ordinary human cycle of crisis and victory, and of contending with the insistent self. The question is what motivates, according to the Writings themselves.

The page takes the question in five parts:

  1. Joy. What the texts say joy is, where it comes from, and what it does.
  2. Tests and the cycle. Shoghi Effendi’s pattern of crisis and victory, Bahá’u’lláh’s and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s statements on tests, and how far the pattern applies to one person’s life.
  3. The insistent self. What the texts call the lower self, how they describe it, how long the struggle lasts, and what they say against discouragement.
  4. What the texts prescribe. The Word morning and evening, prayer, meditation, the Fast, teaching and service, and the daily account.
  5. Present-day research on habit, attention and reward, kept separate, as description only.

It ends with the practice the texts describe for a low period, gathered in one place.

On sources. Quotations are copied from the texts held in this knowledge base. Two kinds of source need a word. Paris Talks and The Promulgation of Universal Peace are notes of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks; a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice (23 March 1987) says that for many of these talks “no original authenticated text has yet been found”, while the Guardian allowed them to be used (see authenticated-word). They are quoted here, and marked as talks. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi or the House of Justice are named as such, since they carry less weight than letters the Guardian or the House of Justice wrote themselves.

The mud and the wings

The image of mud on the wings is in the Writings. In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, Bahá’u’lláh writes to the Shaykh:

“We fain would hope that through thine exertions the wings of men may be sanctified from the mire of self and desire, and be made worthy to soar in the atmosphere of God’s love. Wings that are besmirched with mire can never soar.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf

Two things in the passage matter for what follows. The mire is named: it is “self and desire”. And the wings are not the problem. They are given, and they are meant to be used. Bahá’u’lláh says the same in the Hidden Words:

“O SON OF DESIRE! How long wilt thou soar in the realms of desire? Wings have I bestowed upon thee, that thou mayest fly to the realms of mystic holiness and not the regions of satanic fancy.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Persian 79

The verse does not say the person addressed has stopped flying. It says he is flying in the wrong direction. Desire also lifts and moves a person, towards “satanic fancy” instead of holiness. Nothing has to be added to someone caught in this loop. The faculty that is busy elsewhere has to be turned.

In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas the wings appear again, this time in the law on daily reading of the Word. Bahá’u’lláh asks that the verses be read in a measure that will “lighten and uplift” the soul, “so that they may soar on the wings of the Divine verses towards the Dawning-place of His manifest signs” (¶149). The Universal House of Justice, writing to the Bahá’ís in Iran on 18 December 2014, uses the same image for prayer: “On its wings does the soul soar in the heavens of God and draw closer to the divine reality.” So the texts name two means of flight, the Word and prayer, which are the two usual remedies.

Joy: what it is, where it comes from, and what it does

What joy does

The passage this image recalls is from a talk ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave in Paris on 22 November 1911:

“IN this world we are influenced by two sentiments, Joy and Pain.

Joy gives us wings! In times of joy our strength is more vital, our intellect keener, and our understanding less clouded. We seem better able to cope with the world and to find our sphere of usefulness. But when sadness visits us we become weak, our strength leaves us, our comprehension is dim and our intelligence veiled. The actualities of life seem to elude our grasp, the eyes of our spirits fail to discover the sacred mysteries, and we become even as dead beings.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, no. 35, “Pain and Sorrow” (talk)

This is a claim about what joy does, and it is practical. Joy is not presented as a reward that comes after the work is done. It is the condition in which strength, intellect and understanding work best and in which a person finds “our sphere of usefulness”. Sadness has the opposite effect, and the talk describes that effect as a loss of capacity, not as a moral failure. One consequence follows for anyone in a slump: getting back to joy is not self-indulgence. It is how the capacity to serve comes back.

The same talk says where the two sentiments come from:

“There is no human being untouched by these two influences; but all the sorrow and the grief that exist come from the world of matter—the spiritual world bestows only the joy!”

and describes a person whose attention has moved:

“A man living with his thoughts in this Kingdom knows perpetual joy. The ills all flesh is heir to do not pass him by, but they only touch the surface of his life; the depths are calm and serene.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá then gives His own life as the evidence:

“I myself was in prison forty years—one year alone would have been impossible to bear—nobody survived that imprisonment more than a year! But, thank God, during all those forty years I was supremely happy! Every day, on waking, it was like hearing good tidings, and every night infinite joy was mine. Spirituality was my comfort, and turning to God was my greatest joy.”

The phrase to keep is “turning to God was my greatest joy”. In this account joy is produced by the direction a person faces, not by circumstances.

Where joy comes from: nearness

Bahá’u’lláh puts the same rule in two sentences:

“O SON OF MAN! Sorrow not save that thou art far from Us. Rejoice not save that thou art drawing near and returning unto Us.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Arabic 35

The verse takes away the usual reasons for joy and sorrow and gives one measure: distance from God. “Returning” is in the verse. The person addressed is expected to have gone away and to be coming back, and the coming back is itself a reason to rejoice. That bears directly on the cycle of drifting and returning. The texts do not ask a person to wait until the return is finished before feeling glad. The turn is the occasion.

The next verse makes joy a duty:

“O SON OF MAN! Rejoice in the gladness of thine heart, that thou mayest be worthy to meet Me and to mirror forth My beauty.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Arabic 36

And the verse before both announces it:

“O SON OF SPIRIT! With the joyful tidings of light I hail thee: rejoice! To the court of holiness I summon thee; abide therein that thou mayest live in peace forevermore.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Arabic 33

Spiritual joy and material pleasure

The texts do not condemn ordinary pleasure. They distinguish it from joy and rank the two. In a talk in New York on 2 June 1912, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá observed that a bird has more physical comfort than most people, and drew the conclusion:

“The physical happiness of material conditions was allotted to the animal. … But the honor of the human kingdom is the attainment of spiritual happiness in the human world, the acquisition of the knowledge and love of God. … But if material happiness and spiritual felicity be conjoined, it will be “delight upon delight,” as the Arabs say.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 2 June 1912 (talk)

Material happiness is real. It is the animal’s portion, and it adds to spiritual happiness without replacing it. That is the dividing line between the texts and asceticism, which Bahá’u’lláh abolished.

The texts become severe when pleasure becomes the occupation. A letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice on 8 May 1979 names “one of the signs of a decadent society”:

“…an almost frenetic devotion to pleasure and diversion, an insatiable thirst for amusement, a fanatical devotion to games and sport, a reluctance to treat any matter seriously, and a scornful, derisory attitude towards virtue and solid worth. Abandonment of “a frivolous conduct” does not imply that a Bahá’í must be sour-faced or perpetually solemn. Humour, happiness, joy are characteristics of a true Bahá’í life. Frivolity palls and eventually leads to boredom and emptiness, but true happiness and joy and humour that are parts of a balanced life that includes serious thought, compassion and humble servitude to God, are characteristics that enrich life and add to its radiance.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 8 May 1979, in A Chaste and Holy Life

The same letter notes that Shoghi Effendi “does not forbid “trivial” pleasures, but he does warn against “excessive attachment” to them”, and recalls “‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s caution that we should not let a pastime become a waste of time.” “Frivolity palls and eventually leads to boredom and emptiness” is close to an exact description of a long evening of scrolling. The letter predicts that result and does not treat the pastime itself as the offence.

A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 19 April 2013, answering three Bahá’ís who had asked about moral standards, defines the joy that is being sought:

“The inner joy that every individual seeks, unlike a passing emotion, is not contingent on outside influences; it is a condition, born of certitude and conscious knowledge, fostered by a pure heart, which is able to distinguish between that which has permanence and that which is superficial.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 19 April 2013, “Adhering to Bahá’í Standards of Morality” (muhj-2001-2022)

The letter then quotes the Hidden Words: “Wert thou to speed through the immensity of space and traverse the expanse of heaven, yet thou wouldst find no rest save in submission to Our command and humbleness before Our Face” (Arabic 40). Joy here is a condition that lasts, with named causes: certitude, knowledge and a pure heart. It is not a feeling that has to be chased again each evening.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá goes further in Some Answered Questions. He places paradise and hell partly in this life, and the hell he describes in this life looks like the loop:

“For such souls, there is no greater torment than to be veiled from God, and no worse punishment than selfish qualities, evil attributes, baseness of character, and engrossment in carnal desires. When these souls are delivered from the darkness of these vices through the light of faith, when they are illumined by the rays of the Sun of Truth and endowed with every human virtue, they reckon this as the greatest reward and regard it as the true paradise.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 60, ¶3

On this account, being caught in a cycle of wanting is not a pleasure paid for later. It is already the punishment, at the time it happens. Getting out is already the reward.

Joy from the Word

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas makes the reading of the Word a daily law and, in the same paragraph, makes joy the measure of it:

“Recite ye the verses of God every morn and eventide. Whoso faileth to recite them hath not been faithful to the Covenant of God and His Testament, and whoso turneth away from these holy verses in this Day is of those who throughout eternity have turned away from God. Fear ye God, O My servants, one and all. Pride not yourselves on much reading of the verses or on a multitude of pious acts by night and day; for were a man to read a single verse with joy and radiance it would be better for him than to read with lassitude all the Holy Books of God, the Help in Peril, the Self-Subsisting. Read ye the sacred verses in such measure that ye be not overcome by languor and despondency. Lay not upon your souls that which will weary them and weigh them down, but rather what will lighten and uplift them, so that they may soar on the wings of the Divine verses towards the Dawning-place of His manifest signs; this will draw you nearer to God, did ye but comprehend.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶149

Asked about this verse, Bahá’u’lláh reduced the minimum further: “The prime requisite is the eagerness and love of sanctified souls to read the Word of God. To read one verse, or even one word, in a spirit of joy and radiance, is preferable to the perusal of many Books” (Questions and Answers, no. 68). For someone coming out of a slump, the law asks for very little in quantity and something definite in quality: one verse, read gladly, morning and evening.

Bahá’u’lláh also says what the Word does to desire, which is the mechanism behind the experience of being drawn back:

“Were any man to ponder in his heart that which the Pen of the Most High hath revealed and to taste of its sweetness, he would, of a certainty, find himself emptied and delivered from his own desires, and utterly subservient to the Will of the Almighty. Happy is the man that hath attained so high a station, and hath not deprived himself of so bountiful a grace.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CLXIV

The sequence in this passage is ponder, then taste the sweetness, then be emptied of desire. It does not ask a person to suppress desire first and read afterwards. A stronger sweetness displaces the weaker one. That is why immersion can work when resolve alone does not. “Immerse yourselves in the ocean of My words” is itself a command of the Aqdas (¶182).

A further passage bears on singing or chanting the verses:

“Intone, O My servant, the verses of God that have been received by thee, as intoned by them who have drawn nigh unto Him, that the sweetness of thy melody may kindle thine own soul, and attract the hearts of all men. Whoso reciteth, in the privacy of his chamber, the verses revealed by God, the scattering angels of the Almighty shall scatter abroad the fragrance of the words uttered by his mouth, and shall cause the heart of every righteous man to throb. Though he may, at first, remain unaware of its effect, yet the virtue of the grace vouchsafed unto him must needs sooner or later exercise its influence upon his soul.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXXXVI

The melody kindles “thine own soul” first. The last sentence is an assurance for low periods in particular: the effect may not be felt at first, and it comes “sooner or later” all the same. A reading that seems to do nothing has not failed.

Joy from teaching and service

The second remedy, sharing the Word, is described by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá with an image that fits it closely:

“WHEN the friends do not endeavor to spread the message, they fail to remember God befittingly, and will not witness the tokens of assistance and confirmation from the Abhá Kingdom nor comprehend the divine mysteries. However, when the tongue of the teacher is engaged in teaching, he will naturally himself be stimulated, will become a magnet attracting the divine aid and bounty of the Kingdom, and will be like unto the bird at the hour of dawn, which itself becometh exhilarated by its own singing, its warbling and its melody.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, §211

The passage works in both directions. Not teaching is linked to failing to “remember God befittingly” and to seeing no confirmations. Teaching stimulates the teacher. The bird is not singing because it is already exhilarated. It becomes exhilarated by singing. The order is the same as in Gleanings CXXXVI: the action comes first, and the joy follows from it. Bahá’u’lláh gives the same promise in stronger terms: “Whoso openeth his lips in this Day and maketh mention of the name of his Lord, the hosts of Divine inspiration shall descend upon him” (Gleanings, CXXIX).

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 18 February 1954 states the general rule:

“The more we search for ourselves, the less likely we are to find ourselves; and the more we search for God, and to serve our fellow-men, the more profoundly will we become acquainted with ourselves, and the more inwardly assured. This is one of the great spiritual laws of life.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 18 February 1954, Lights of Guidance, no. 391

“Inwardly assured” is the state that a slump removes. The letter places its recovery in turning outward, to God and to other people, and warns that searching inward for it makes it harder to find.

Tests, and the cycle of crisis and victory

The pattern in the life of the Cause

Shoghi Effendi set out the pattern at the start of God Passes By (1944), his history of the Faith’s first century:

“Indeed, the history of the first hundred years of its evolution resolves itself into a series of internal and external crises, of varying severity, devastating in their immediate effects, but each mysteriously releasing a corresponding measure of divine power, lending thereby a fresh impulse to its unfoldment, this further unfoldment engendering in its turn a still graver calamity, followed by a still more liberal effusion of celestial grace enabling its upholders to accelerate still further its march and win in its service still more compelling victories.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, Foreword

Near the end of the book he calls it “a series of pulsations, of alternating crises and triumphs, leading it ever nearer to its divinely appointed destiny.” Three features of the pattern matter here. Each crisis releases “a corresponding measure” of power, so the size of the difficulty and the size of the help are linked. The crises do not get smaller as the Faith advances; “a still graver calamity” follows growth. And the alternation has a direction: it leads the Faith “ever nearer to its divinely appointed destiny”. In 2023 the Universal House of Justice summarised this part of the Guardian’s teaching as “the dialectic of crisis and victory”, which he described to prepare the believers “for the tortuous path they must traverse” (28 November 2023, 20231128_001).

Does it apply to one person?

Shoghi Effendi wrote this passage about the Cause, not about individual souls, and it should not be presented as a teaching about personal psychology. But the Writings state a parallel rule for individuals, and Bahá’u’lláh gives it in so many words in the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat:

“Be generous in your days of plenty, and be patient in the hour of loss. Adversity is followed by success and rejoicings follow woe. Guard against idleness and sloth, and cling unto that which profiteth mankind, whether young or old, whether high or low.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat

“Adversity is followed by success and rejoicings follow woe” is the individual form of the cycle. In the same paragraph Bahá’u’lláh warns against “idleness and sloth” and, a few sentences earlier, gives the rule of daily progress: “Let each morn be better than its eve and each morrow richer than its yesterday.” The cycle is not a reason to wait for the next upswing. It comes with instructions for what to do during the low part.

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 23 December 1948 applies the same reasoning to an individual:

“The Guardian urges you not to be discouraged by any setbacks you may have. Life is a process of trials and testings, and these are--contrary to what we are prone to thinking--good for us, and give us stamina, and teach us to rely on God. Knowing He will help us, we can help ourselves more.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 23 December 1948, The Unfolding Destiny of the British Bahá’í Community, p. 453

The last sentence describes how the cycle turns at the level of one person. Being confident of help is what makes a person able to help himself.

What a test is for

‘Abdu’l-Bahá connects tests directly with the self. Answering a believer who had written of “afflictive tests”, He wrote:

“To the loyal soul, a test is but God’s grace and favor; for the valiant doth joyously press forward to furious battle on the field of anguish, when the coward, whimpering with fright, will tremble and shake. So too, the proficient student, who hath with great competence mastered his subjects and committed them to memory, will happily exhibit his skills before his examiners on the day of his tests. So too will solid gold wondrously gleam and shine out in the assayer’s fire.

It is clear, then, that tests and trials are, for sanctified souls, but God’s bounty and grace, while to the weak, they are a calamity, unexpected and sudden.

These tests, even as thou didst write, do but cleanse the spotting of self from off the mirror of the heart, till the Sun of Truth can cast its rays thereon; for there is no veil more obstructive than the self, and however tenuous that veil may be, at the last it will completely shut a person out, and deprive him of his portion of eternal grace.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections, §155

The last paragraph links the two halves of the cycle. Tests and the struggle with the self are one process: a test does its work by removing “the spotting of self”. The same event can be grace or calamity depending on how the person meets it. Bahá’u’lláh puts it as a question in the Hidden Words: “If adversity befall thee not in My path, how canst thou walk in the ways of them that are content with My pleasure?” (Arabic 50), and as a paradox in the verse after: “My calamity is My providence, outwardly it is fire and vengeance, but inwardly it is light and mercy” (Arabic 51).

Not every low point is a test sent from outside

One distinction in ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks needs to be kept in view, because it bears on habits. In Paris on 27 October 1911 He divided trials into two kinds:

“The trials of man are of two kinds. (a) The consequences of his own actions. If a man eats too much, he ruins his digestion; if he takes poison he becomes ill or dies. If a person gambles he will lose his money; if he drinks too much he will lose his equilibrium. All these sufferings are caused by the man himself; it is quite clear therefore that certain sorrows are the result of our own deeds.

(b) Other sufferings there are, which come upon the Faithful of God.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, no. 14, “The Benefits of God to Man” (talk)

The same talk goes on to say that “Tests are benefits from God, for which we should thank Him. Grief and sorrow do not come to us by chance, they are sent to us by the Divine Mercy for our own perfecting.” Both kinds can serve growth. But they call for different responses. A low period that comes from one’s own routine (late nights, the phone, a habit of reaching for stimulation) belongs mostly to the first kind. Its remedy is not only patience. It is a change in the deeds that produce it, which is what the daily account in the next section is for. Calling it a test sent from above would skip that step.

What the danger actually is

The dip is not what the texts warn against most. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 8 January 1949 says what is:

“Life is a constant struggle, not only against forces around us, but above all against our own ego. We can never afford to rest on our own oars, for if we do, we soon see ourselves carried down stream again. Many of those who drift away from the Cause do so for the reason that they had ceased to go on developing. They became complacent or indifferent, and consequently ceased to draw the spiritual strength and vitality from the Cause which they should have.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 8 January 1949, The Unfolding Destiny of the British Bahá’í Community, p. 454 (also Lights of Guidance, no. 388)

The danger named is drift: ceasing to develop, and with it ceasing to draw strength from the Cause. On this reading, a believer who keeps falling into a habit and keeps coming back to the Word is inside the struggle the letter describes. The one who has stopped returning has drifted.

The insistent self

The name and what it refers to

“Insistent self” is the term used in the authorised English translations of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. It stands behind the Persian nafs-i-ammárih, “the self that commands” (to evil). The phrase comes from the Qur’án, where Joseph says that the soul is ammāra bi’l-sū’, prone to command evil (12:53), and Sufi writers later made it the first of the soul’s stages. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá uses it in a statement about the Cause itself:

“Its only army is the love of God, its only joy the clear wine of His knowledge, its only battle the expounding of the Truth; its one crusade is against the insistent self, the evil promptings of the human heart. Its victory is to submit and yield, and to be selfless is its everlasting glory.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections, §206

The sentence ranks the struggle very high. The insistent self is the Cause’s “one crusade”, and “its only joy” is in the same sentence. One Common Faith (2005), prepared under the supervision of the Universal House of Justice, glosses the term for a present-day reader: “The ego, termed by Him the “insistent self”, resists instinctively constraints imposed on what it conceives to be its freedom.” It quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Secret of Divine Civilization: “self-love is kneaded into the very clay of man.”

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (10 December 1947) prevents a common confusion, the idea that the self as such is the enemy:

“Self has really two meanings, or is used in two senses, in the Bahá’í writings: one is self, the identity of the individual created by God. This is the self mentioned in such passages as ‘he hath known God who hath known himself etc.’. The other self is the ego, the dark, animalistic heritage each one of us has, the lower nature that can develop into a monster of selfishness, brutality, lust and so on. It is this self we must struggle against, or this side of our natures, in order to strengthen and free the spirit within us and help it to attain perfection.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 10 December 1947, Lights of Guidance, no. 386

The struggle is fought for the true self, not against it. Bahá’u’lláh’s call to “Turn thy sight unto thyself, that thou mayest find Me standing within thee” (Hidden Words, Arabic 13) concerns the first meaning. The insistent self is the second.

A Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in Ottoman Turkish, available only in a provisional translation by Necati Alkan (not authorised), describes the two as states of one spirit: if its spiritual side dominates, the human spirit “becometh lofty, luminous, merciful, tranquil (mutma’innih)”, and if it is “contaminated with contingent, worldly concerns” it becomes “reproachful (lawwamih), commanding to evil (ammárih)”. This is supporting evidence only. It fits the authorised texts: the insistent self is not a second creature living inside a person. It is what the one soul becomes when it is turned towards the world of nature.

The two natures

‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes the ground of the struggle in Some Answered Questions:

“Know that there are two natures in man: the material and the spiritual. The material nature is inherited from Adam, while the spiritual nature is inherited from the reality of the Word of God, which is the spirituality of Christ. The material nature is born of Adam, but the spiritual nature is born of the grace of the Holy Spirit. The material nature is the source of every imperfection, and the spiritual nature is the source of all perfection.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 29, ¶2

In the same chapter: “In this material world, man is subject to the force of instinctual desires, of which sin is the inevitable consequence, for these desires are not bound by the laws of justice and righteousness” (¶5). And the remedy he names is the Word: through “the instruction and guidance of that Essence of detachment”, “all the imperfections imposed by the material life of man are … transmuted into human perfections” (¶7). The spiritual nature is “inherited from the reality of the Word of God”. Read in that light, going back to the Word in a low period is more than a mood-lifting technique. It reconnects a person with the source of the nature that is meant to lead.

In a Paris talk on 1 November 1911 (Paris Talks, no. 18), ‘Abdu’l-Bahá adds a detail that applies to habits: “Every good habit, every noble quality belongs to man’s spiritual nature, whereas all his imperfections and sinful actions are born of his material nature.” Good habits belong to the higher nature. On this account, a routine is not spiritually neutral. It can be built in either direction.

The struggle lasts for life

The texts do not promise that the struggle will end. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 8 January 1949 says:

“The only people who are truly free of the ”dross of self“ are the Prophets, for to be free of one’s ego is a hall-mark of perfection. We humans are never going to become perfect, for perfection belongs to a realm we are not destined to enter. However, we must constantly mount higher, seek to be more perfect.

The ego is the animal in us, the heritage of the flesh which is full of selfish desires. By obeying the laws of God, seeking to live the life laid down in our teachings, and prayer and struggle, we can subdue our egos. We call people ”saints“ who have achieved the highest degree of mastery over their egos.

There is no contradiction between Gleanings p. 66 and p. 262. In one place He says the mirror will never be free from dross, in the other place He says it will be ”so cleared as to be able“ etc. It is a relative thing; perfection will never be reached, but great and ever greater, progress can be made.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 8 January 1949, The Unfolding Destiny of the British Bahá’í Community, pp. 453–54

This takes away two false expectations together. A person should not expect to finish, and should not conclude that, since finishing is impossible, progress is too. The means are listed: obedience to the laws, living the life the teachings describe, prayer and struggle. Shoghi Effendi himself, writing in The Advent of Divine Justice (1938) about the American believers’ “twofold crusade”, lists “their own instincts” first among the forces that “must be reckoned with, resolutely resisted, and completely overcome”, ahead of opposition from outside.

Against discouragement and self-condemnation

The guidance on this point is extensive, and it treats self-condemnation as an obstacle in its own right, not as a sign of seriousness.

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 13 October 1947:

“He strongly urges you not to dwell on yourself. Each one of us, if we look into our failures, is sure to feel unworthy and despondent, and this feeling only frustrates our constructive efforts and wastes time. The thing for us to focus on is the glory of the Cause and the Power of Bahá’u’lláh which can make of a mere drop a surging sea!” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 13 October 1947, Lights of Guidance, no. 396

The letter does not deny the failures. It says that dwelling on them produces despondency, and despondency “frustrates our constructive efforts”. It names something else to attend to.

On 14 July 1945, a letter on his behalf advised a believer to turn to God “realising that He is forgiving, that in one moment He can, through His blessed mercy, take away our sense of failure and help us to do better in the future–if we sincerely wish to” (Lights of Guidance, no. 395). On 30 January 1939, to a believer held back by “every sense of dissatisfaction, and of unworthiness”: “You should not look at your limitations, but derive full confidence at the thought that, however limited your resources and capacities may be, your efforts will be reinforced by Divine confirmations, provided you do your share and discharge your obligations as a believer, fully and conscientiously” (no. 407).

The 2013 letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice brings these together, and it is the closest text in the corpus to this common experience:

“Yet what is expected in this connection is effort sustained by earnest desire, not instantaneous perfection. The qualities and habits of thought and action that characterize Bahá’í life are developed through daily exertion. “Bring thyself to account each day”, writes Bahá’u’lláh. “Let each morn be better than its eve”, He advises, “and each morrow richer than its yesterday.” The friends should not lose heart in their personal struggles to attain to the Divine standard, nor be seduced by the argument that, since mistakes will inevitably be made and perfection is impossible, it is futile to exert an effort. They are to steer clear of the pitfalls of hypocrisy, on the one hand—that is, saying one thing yet doing another—and heedlessness, on the other—that is, disregard for the laws, ignoring or explaining away the need to follow them. So too is paralysis engendered by guilt to be avoided; indeed, preoccupation with a particular moral failing can, at times, make it more challenging for it to be overcome.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 19 April 2013 (muhj-2001-2022)

The passage names three ways to go wrong (hypocrisy, heedlessness and paralysis by guilt) and one way forward: daily effort, sustained by desire. The last sentence makes a practical claim: fixing one’s attention on a particular failing can make that failing harder to overcome. The same letter describes the community a struggling believer belongs to as one “in which mistakes are treated with tolerance and fear of failure is diminished”.

Bahá’u’lláh’s own words set the time frame. At the start of The Seven Valleys:

“The steed of this valley is patience; without patience the wayfarer on this journey will reach nowhere and attain no goal. Nor should he ever become downhearted: If he strive for a hundred thousand years and yet fail to behold the beauty of the Friend, he should not falter.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Seven Valleys, in The Call of the Divine Beloved

And in the Hidden Words, addressing a soul that has given its heart to “another than Me”, He ends: “Notwithstanding I have concealed thy secret and desired not thy shame” (Persian 27). The relationship continues after the failure. God hides the failure, and He does not want the person shamed by it.

What the texts prescribe

The texts give a short list of practices, and they repeat it often. Each item below is the text’s own prescription, with the reason the text gives for it.

The Word, morning and evening

The law is Aqdas ¶149, quoted above: “Recite ye the verses of God every morn and eventide”, in a measure that leaves the soul lightened, not wearied. The House of Justice, in its Riḍván 2010 message, names daily reading as a mark of identity, “the habit of daily reading will remain an integral part of Bahá’í identity”, and in the same message names what it competes with:

“Passivity is bred by the forces of society today. A desire to be entertained is nurtured from childhood, with increasing efficiency, cultivating generations willing to be led by whoever proves skilful at appealing to superficial emotions.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

That is a plain description of a habitual routine of distraction from the institution charged with guiding the community. The House of Justice treats it as a feature of the age that every believer meets, not as a private weakness.

Bahá’u’lláh also tells the reader to be in company: “The company of the ungodly increaseth sorrow, whilst fellowship with the righteous cleanseth the rust from off the heart. He that seeketh to commune with God, let him betake himself to the companionship of His loved ones” (Hidden Words, Persian 56). Reading the Word together with a spouse is one form of that companionship.

Prayer, and remembrance at dawn

The House of Justice’s letter of 18 December 2014 gives the fullest account in the corpus of what prayer does to the self:

“Like the morning’s dew, it brings freshness to the heart and cleanses it, purifying it from attachments of the insistent self. It is a fire that burns away the veils and a light that leads to the ocean of reunion with the Almighty. On its wings does the soul soar in the heavens of God and draw closer to the divine reality. Upon its quality depends the development of the limitless capacities of the soul and the attraction of the bounties of God, but the prolongation of prayer is not desirable.” — The Universal House of Justice, 18 December 2014, to the Bahá’ís in Iran (muhj-2001-2022)

Prayer is named as the specific remedy for “attachments of the insistent self”, and quality counts for more than length. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 27 January 1945 says the same about character: “not everyone achieves easily and rapidly the victory over self. What every believer, new or old, should realize is that the Cause has the spiritual power to re-create us if we make the effort to let that power influence us, and the greatest help in this respect is prayer. We must supplicate Bahá’u’lláh to assist us to overcome the failings in our own characters, and also exert our own will power in mastering ourselves” (Lights of Guidance, no. 394). There are two agents here, prayer and will, and the letter names both.

Bahá’u’lláh names the morning, and gives an instruction about attention that applies to anyone whose attention is pulled by a device:

“At the dawn of every day he should commune with God, and, with all his soul, persevere in the quest of his Beloved. He should consume every wayward thought with the flame of His loving mention, and, with the swiftness of lightning, pass by all else save Him.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXXV

“Consume every wayward thought with the flame of His loving mention” describes a practice: when attention drifts, replace the thought with remembrance. Do not fight it directly. In the Hidden Words: “The healer of all thine ills is remembrance of Me, forget it not” (Persian 32).

Meditation

Bahá’u’lláh, in the Kitáb-i-Íqán, says the meditation of the tradition “One hour’s reflection is preferable to seventy years of pious worship” “must needs be observed”. Gleanings CLXIV, quoted above, says how meditation on the Word acts: by pondering it and tasting its sweetness, a person is “emptied and delivered from his own desires”. The texts are restrained about method. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 23 November 1947, to a believer who had been ill, adds a limit: “You should certainly safeguard your nerves, and force yourself to take time, and not only for prayer and meditation, but for real rest and relaxation. We don’t have to pray and meditate for hours in order to be spiritual” (in Prayer and Devotional Life).

The Fast

Many believers already use the Fast to re-orient themselves. Shoghi Effendi’s description, quoted in the notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, uses nearly the same terms:

“…essentially a period of meditation and prayer, of spiritual recuperation, during which the believer must strive to make the necessary readjustments in his inner life, and to refresh and reinvigorate the spiritual forces latent in his soul. Its significance and purpose are, therefore, fundamentally spiritual in character. Fasting is symbolic, and a reminder of abstinence from selfish and carnal desires.” — Shoghi Effendi, quoted in The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Notes, no. 25

“Readjustments in his inner life” is the Guardian’s description of the Fast’s purpose. The last sentence ties it to the insistent self: the abstinence from food stands for abstinence from “selfish and carnal desires”. The Fast is a yearly, legislated period for the work this page is about.

Teaching and service

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s bird at dawn (Selections, §211) is the central text. Others add to it. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas: “Arise ye, under all conditions, to render service to the Cause, for God will assuredly assist you through the power of His sovereignty which overshadoweth the worlds” (¶74). “Under all conditions” includes low ones. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi to the Hands of the Cause in Asia (30 May 1956) sets the order: “The friends … should turn to God for guidance in prayer and meditation, should study the Teachings of the Faith and then arise and act. Prayer and meditation, and study, without action, are of no value, and action must be reinforced by these other things, which strengthen the soul and nourish the mind.”

The 2013 letter on behalf of the House of Justice states plainly why service bears on the self: the institute courses set the individual on a path in which qualities “are gradually acquired through service—service intended to quell the insistent self, helping to lift the individual out of its confines and placing him or her in a dynamic process of community building.” It also tells the believer that the struggle is not fought alone: “they are not isolated individuals, withstanding alone the onslaught of the forces of moral decay operating in society. They are members of a purposeful community”.

The daily account

“O SON OF BEING! Bring thyself to account each day ere thou art summoned to a reckoning; for death, unheralded, shall come upon thee and thou shalt be called to give account for thy deeds” (Hidden Words, Arabic 31). Read with the letters above, the account is a daily review of deeds. It is not a daily verdict on one’s worth. The 13 October 1947 letter warns against looking into one’s failures in a way that produces despondency. The 2013 letter warns that “preoccupation with a particular moral failing” can make it harder to overcome. The account answers one question, what was done today, and the rule of the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat gives the standard: that tomorrow be a little better than today.

Present-day research on habit, attention and reward

This section describes current research. It is not authority for any Bahá’í teaching, and it may date. Where it agrees with the texts, that is an observation of mine. The texts do not depend on it.

“Dopamine” is mostly about wanting. The popular phrase “dopamine seeking” is partly accurate. Kent Berridge and Morten Kringelbach’s review of the pleasure systems of the brain (Neuron, 2015) separates “wanting” a reward from “liking” it. They write that “Wanting for rewards is generated by a large and distributed brain system”, while “Liking, or pleasure itself, is generated by a smaller set of hedonic hotspots within limbic circuitry”, and that the mesolimbic dopamine system “may not generate pleasure after all”. Manipulations of dopamine, they report, increased “incentive salience, producing ‘wanting’, without causing ‘liking’ or true hedonic impact.” In addiction, cue-triggered wanting can grow “even if the drug became less pleasant.”

Where this meets the texts: the research describes a pull that can strengthen while the satisfaction it brings weakens. The 1979 letter’s “Frivolity palls and eventually leads to boredom and emptiness” makes the same observation about experience. The texts also go further than the research. The joy they describe (“a condition, born of certitude and conscious knowledge”) does not appear in the research’s model at all, which deals with reward systems and not with nearness to God.

Habits are triggered by context, and they run whatever the person intends. Wendy Wood and Dennis Rünger’s review (Annual Review of Psychology, 2016) gives two defining features of habit: “(a) activation by recurring context cues and (b) insensitivity to short-term changes in goals”. In one study they cite, “about 43% of actions were performed almost daily and usually in the same context.” Two findings bear on this page. On days when people’s intentions to exercise “were weaker than usual, they fell back on their exercise habits”. And “Life transitions that alter habit cues can provide a window of opportunity to act on new intentions without competition from old habits.”

Where this meets the texts: a fixed time for the Word, “morn and eventide”, works as a habit that carries a person on days when intention is weak, which is when a low period is most likely to deepen. The Fast changes the cues of daily life (meals, mornings, evenings) for nineteen days each year. On this research that is the kind of change that gives new intentions room to take hold. That is my observation. The Guardian’s stated purpose for the Fast is spiritual.

Missing a day does little harm; missing many does. In Phillippa Lally and colleagues’ study of 96 people forming a daily behaviour (European Journal of Social Psychology, 2010), the average time to reach maximum automaticity was 66 days, with a range of 18 to a predicted 254. As summarised by the British Psychological Society, “a single missed day had little impact on later automaticity gains”, but “too many missed repeats of the behaviour, even if spread out over time, had a cumulative effect.”

Where this meets the texts: this is close to the guidance against both discouragement and heedlessness. One lapse is not a reason to lose heart; the 2013 letter warns against “paralysis engendered by guilt”. A pattern of lapses is drift, which the 1949 letter warns against.

“Dopamine fasting” is a misnomer. The fashion for “dopamine fasting” has been criticised by Peter Grinspoon of Harvard Health Publishing (2020): dopamine is not a substance one can fast from, and its originator, the psychiatrist Cameron Sepah, has said the name is “not to be taken literally.” The Bahá’í Fast should not be described in these terms. Its stated purpose is “meditation and prayer, … spiritual recuperation”. It may change habits as a side effect, but that is not its definition.

Where the texts and the research differ. The research describes mechanisms and can recommend techniques. It is silent on three things the texts treat as central. The first is purpose: what the freed attention is for, which the texts answer with the knowledge and love of God and service to others. The second is a second agent: the texts promise that effort is “reinforced by Divine confirmations”, and the research makes no such claim. The third is the order of things: the texts put sweetness first (“taste of its sweetness” and be emptied of desire), while much habit advice puts restriction first.

The practice the texts describe for a low period

This list gathers the guidance quoted above. Each line names its source, and nothing in it is added beyond the texts.

  1. Do not read the low period as a verdict. It is part of a pattern the texts expect: “Adversity is followed by success and rejoicings follow woe” (Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat). “Life is a process of trials and testings” (on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 1948).
  2. Ask honestly which kind of trial it is. If it is “the consequences of his own actions” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris), the deeds have to change, and the daily account is the means: “Bring thyself to account each day.”
  3. Do not dwell on the failure. “He strongly urges you not to dwell on yourself” (1947). Guilt that paralyses is to be avoided (2013). God “in one moment” can “take away our sense of failure” (1945).
  4. Return to the Word, in a small amount and gladly. “A single verse with joy and radiance” (Aqdas ¶149); “one verse, or even one word” (Q&A 68). The effect may come later: “sooner or later” (Gleanings CXXXVI).
  5. Pray, especially at dawn, and use remembrance against wandering attention. Prayer purifies the heart “from attachments of the insistent self” (UHJ, 2014). “Consume every wayward thought with the flame of His loving mention” (Gleanings CXXV).
  6. Share the Word, and serve. The teacher “will naturally himself be stimulated … like unto the bird at the hour of dawn” (Selections, §211). “The more we search for God, and to serve our fellow-men, the more … inwardly assured” (1954). “Arise ye, under all conditions” (Aqdas ¶74).
  7. Keep company with those who love God, for “fellowship with the righteous cleanseth the rust from off the heart” (Hidden Words, Persian 56).
  8. Use the Fast for its purpose: “to make the necessary readjustments in his inner life” (Shoghi Effendi).
  9. Expect to keep struggling, and expect to keep progressing. “Perfection will never be reached, but great and ever greater, progress can be made” (1949). “If he strive for a hundred thousand years … he should not falter” (The Seven Valleys).

Notes on sources and limits

  • Talks. The “Joy gives us wings” passage, the two kinds of trials, the two natures and the material/spiritual happiness passage come from Paris Talks and The Promulgation of Universal Peace. The House of Justice has said that many of these talks have no authenticated original. Each point taken from them is also supported here by an authenticated text (the Hidden Words, the Aqdas, Some Answered Questions, Selections).
  • Crisis and victory. Shoghi Effendi’s formulation in God Passes By concerns the history of the Faith. This page does not claim that he applied it to individual lives. The individual form rests on Bahá’u’lláh’s Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat and on letters written on his behalf, which use the same reasoning.
  • “Insistent self” and nafs-i-ammárih. The English term is from the authorised translation of Selections, §206. The link to the Persian term and its Qur’ánic origin (12:53) is general background, not a statement from an authoritative text. The Tablet on the soul’s states (ammárih, lawwámih, muṭma’innih) is cited from a provisional, unauthorised translation by Necati Alkan and is supporting evidence only.
  • Gleanings numbering. The passage “Were any man to ponder in his heart…” stands in section CLXIV in the corpus edition. The compilation The Importance of Prayer, Meditation and the Devotional Attitude cites it as sec. 163, p. 343, from an earlier printing.
  • Wording of the 2014 letter. The corpus holds two printings. The Messages volume reads “Upon its quality depends”; the compilation The Institution of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár reads “depend”. This page follows the Messages volume.
  • Not verified. I did not find, in this corpus, a letter of Shoghi Effendi containing the exact words “we must not be discouraged by our failings”. The letters quoted above (1939, 1945, 1947, 1948, 1949) say the same thing in their own words.
  • Research findings are summarised from the sources listed below and reflect the field as of October 2026. The 66-day figure in Lally et al. comes from the participants whose data fitted the model; secondary reports note that this was a subset.
