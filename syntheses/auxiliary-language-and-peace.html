A Language for All and the Peace of the World: The Seventh Issue in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

In October 1985 the Universal House of Justice addressed a statement “To the Peoples of the World.” Part II of The Promise of World Peace reviews the declarations and conventions of the United Nations and then singles out a few issues that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven. The international auxiliary language comes last, and its paragraph is the shortest of the seven — two sentences:

“A fundamental lack of communication between peoples seriously undermines efforts towards world peace. Adopting an international auxiliary language would go far to resolve this problem and necessitates the most urgent attention.” — The Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, October 1985, Part II

(The text on bahai.org and in Messages from the Universal House of Justice 1963–1986, ¶438.36, reads “go far to resolve.” The bahai-library.com transcription of the statement, and a 1994 Research Department memorandum quoting it, read “go far to resolving.” The meaning is the same.)

This page reads the two sentences closely, places them in the argument of the whole statement, and follows the theme back to its source in the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, through ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s talks and Tablets, the letters of Shoghi Effendi, and the guidance of the Universal House of Justice. It ends with a section prepared for a songwriter.

No earlier article in this knowledge base treats the subject. The unity in diversity article explains why Bahá’í teaching protects cultural and linguistic difference; this page relies on that principle and does not repeat it. The concept pages on the oneness of humanity and the Most Great and Lesser Peace mention a universal language only in passing.

The paragraph, sentence by sentence

“A fundamental lack of communication between peoples.” The problem is named first, and it is not named as a shortage of languages or a surplus of them. It is a lack of communication, and it lies between peoples — not between governments or diplomats, who have interpreters. The adjective “fundamental” places it at the foundation: it is a defect in the ground on which other efforts are built.

“Seriously undermines efforts towards world peace.” The verb describes a condition that weakens efforts already under way. The statement does not say that the lack of communication causes war, as it says that racism is “a major barrier to peace” or that the disparity between rich and poor keeps the world “virtually on the brink of war.” It says that whatever peoples attempt together is weakened when they cannot understand each other.

“Adopting an international auxiliary language.” Three words carry the remedy. Adopting means a deliberate act by those with the authority to adopt, and the Writings name who that is (see below). International means a language for use between peoples. Auxiliary means a helper, added to the languages people already speak; it does not replace them.

“Would go far to resolve this problem.” The claim is measured. The language would go far; it would not by itself complete the work. Understanding each other’s words is necessary for peace and is not sufficient for it, and the other six issues and the closing call to spiritual principle cover what a common language cannot.

“And necessitates the most urgent attention.” The sentence ends on urgency. The wealth-and-poverty paragraph speaks of decisions “that must urgently be made”; this is the only one of the seven that asks for “the most urgent attention.” The paragraph is the shortest of the seven, and its request is the most urgently worded.

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The paralysis of will

Part II opens by arguing that banning particular weapons or settling particular disputes will not remove “the root causes of war.” National leaders do not lack information. “There is, however, a paralysis of will.” The statement traces that paralysis to three roots: a “deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind”; an “unwillingness to face courageously the far-reaching implications of establishing a united world authority”; and “the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order in which they can live in peace, harmony and prosperity with all humanity.”

The third root is a failure of expression. Peoples who want peace cannot say so to one another, or cannot say it in a way that reaches beyond their own borders. The two paragraphs that close the list of seven — universal education and an auxiliary language — answer this third root most directly. Education addresses the “largely ignorant”; a shared second language gives what has been learned a way to cross from one people to another.

The introduction had already listed, among the favourable signs of the age, “the spontaneous spawning of widening networks of ordinary people seeking understanding through personal communication,” and had described the barriers that remain: “Doubts, misconceptions, prejudices, suspicions and narrow self-interest beset nations and peoples in their relations one to another.” Misconception and suspicion feed on misunderstanding. The language paragraph names one practical means of reducing it.

The paragraph just before: universal education

The sixth issue asks governments to give universal education “the utmost support,” and to consider “teaching the concept of world citizenship as part of the standard education of every child.” The Writings place the auxiliary language in the same setting: it is to be “taught to children in schools throughout the world” (Bahá’u’lláh, Third Glad-Tidings). The two paragraphs describe one programme — a school in every land teaching every child the mother tongue, a common second language, and the idea that the child belongs to one human family.

Some pages in this knowledge base (for example PeaceStatement) count “the lack of universal education and a universal auxiliary language” as one obstacle and so speak of six. The statement gives them separate paragraphs, and this series follows the statement in counting seven.

The two points that follow the seven

After the language paragraph the statement adds: “Two points bear emphasizing in all these issues.” The first is that “the abolition of war is not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols; it is a complex task requiring a new level of commitment to resolving issues not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.” Of all seven issues, a school language is perhaps the one least associated with peace by most people; the first point applies to it fully.

The second point is that “the primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism.” An auxiliary language can be argued on purely practical grounds — cost, efficiency, trade — and the Bahá’í International Community has used such arguments (see below). The statement asks for more: to see the language as an expression of the oneness of humankind. Bahá’u’lláh’s own phrase for it is “the cause of unity” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶189).

Parts III and IV

  • Part III quotes Shoghi Effendi’s 1931 description of a world “organically unified in all the essential aspects of its life, its political machinery, its spiritual aspiration, its trade and finance, its script and language, and yet infinite in the diversity of the national characteristics of its federated units.” A paragraph later it quotes him again: the principle of oneness “does not ignore, nor does it attempt to suppress, the diversity of ethnical origins, of climate, of history, of language and tradition, of thought and habit, that differentiate the peoples and nations of the world.” Language appears twice, once as something to be unified and once as something to be protected. The word auxiliary holds both: one shared language for what all peoples must do together, and many languages for the life of each people.
  • Part III also calls for a world gathering of leaders and for “candid, dispassionate and cordial consultation.” Consultation between peoples needs a medium in which they can understand each other. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá made the link explicitly in 1912: with a universal language “consultation would become general” (see below).
  • Part IV offers the Bahá’í community as “a single social organism, representative of the diversity of the human family, conducting its affairs through a system of commonly accepted consultative principles.” Such a community already lives with the problem the paragraph names: its members speak hundreds of languages and depend on translation to consult at all.

The House of Justice had prepared for the statement with a letter of 23 January 1985 about the International Year of Peace that the United Nations had proclaimed for 1986. It invited Bahá’í communities to sponsor activities on topics relevant to peace, including “the adoption of a world auxiliary language,” and proposed a publicity campaign using themes such as “world peace through world religion,” “world peace through world education,” “world peace through world language,” “world peace through world law.”

Bahá’u’lláh: the law, and the people it is addressed to

The paragraph in the Promise is a short restatement of a law of Bahá’u’lláh. He revealed it in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas and repeated it in at least five later Tablets, each time addressed to a different body. Read together, these passages show what the language is for, who is to choose it, and how it relates to the mother tongue.

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas: a sign of humanity’s coming of age

“O members of parliaments throughout the world! Select ye a single language for the use of all on earth, and adopt ye likewise a common script. God, verily, maketh plain for you that which shall profit you and enable you to be independent of others. He, of a truth, is the Most Bountiful, the All-Knowing, the All-Informed. This will be the cause of unity, could ye but comprehend it, and the greatest instrument for promoting harmony and civilization, would that ye might understand! We have appointed two signs for the coming of age of the human race: the first, which is the most firm foundation, We have set down in other of Our Tablets, while the second hath been revealed in this wondrous Book.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶189

The command is addressed to legislators. It joins a common script to the common language. It names three purposes: unity, harmony, and civilization. And it names the adoption of the language as one of two signs that humanity has come of age. Shoghi Effendi confirmed that this is the “second” sign the verse refers to: Bahá’u’lláh “has enjoined the selection of a single language and the adoption of a common script for all on earth to use, an injunction which, when carried out, would, as He Himself affirms in that Book, be one of the signs of the ‘coming of age of the human race’” (quoted in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, note 194). The Promise describes humanity as approaching “its long-awaited coming of age.” The auxiliary language is one of the marks by which that coming of age will be known.

The notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas set out the two stages the Writings envisage:

“Bahá’u’lláh enjoins the adoption of a universal language and script. His Writings envisage two stages in this process. The first stage is to consist of the selection of an existing language or an invented one which would then be taught in all the schools of the world as an auxiliary to the mother tongues. The governments of the world through their parliaments are called upon to effect this momentous enactment. The second stage, in the distant future, would be the eventual adoption of one single language and common script for all on earth.” — Kitáb-i-Aqdas, note 193

The Third Glad-Tidings: one country

“The third Glad-Tidings concerneth the study of divers languages. This decree hath formerly streamed forth from the Pen of the Most High: It behooveth the sovereigns of the world—may God assist them—or the ministers of the earth to take counsel together and to adopt one of the existing languages or a new one to be taught to children in schools throughout the world, and likewise one script. Thus the whole earth will come to be regarded as one country.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Bi<u>sh</u>árát (Glad-Tidings)

Here the addressees are sovereigns or ministers, the method is that they “take counsel together,” and the place of the language is the classroom. The result is stated as a change in how people see the earth: “one country.” This is the same phrase as the Promise’s quotation in the nationalism paragraph — “The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.” The fifth issue asks for a wider loyalty; the seventh supplies one of the practical means by which people come to feel it.

The Sixth Ishráq: to understand one another’s writing and speech

“The sixth I<u>sh</u>ráq is union and concord amongst the children of men. From the beginning of time the light of unity hath shed its divine radiance upon the world, and the greatest means for the promotion of that unity is for the peoples of the world to understand one another’s writing and speech. In former Epistles We have enjoined upon the Trustees of the House of Justice either to choose one language from among those now existing or to adopt a new one, and in like manner to select a common script, both of which should be taught in all the schools of the world. Thus will the earth be regarded as one country and one home. The most glorious fruit of the tree of knowledge is this exalted word: Of one tree are all ye the fruit, and of one bough the leaves.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, I<u>sh</u>ráqát (Splendours)

This passage is the closest scriptural source for the Promise’s paragraph. Where the Promise names “a fundamental lack of communication between peoples,” Bahá’u’lláh names “the greatest means for the promotion of that unity”: that peoples “understand one another’s writing and speech.” It also gives the task to a different body — “the Trustees of the House of Justice” — which is why the House of Justice later spoke of “two different provisions in the Sacred Texts” (see below). And it sets the language beside the image of one tree, one bough.

The Tablet of Maqṣúd: as if entering one’s own home

“Among the things which are conducive to unity and concord and will cause the whole earth to be regarded as one country is that the divers languages be reduced to one language and in like manner the scripts used in the world be confined to a single script. It is incumbent upon all nations to appoint some men of understanding and erudition to convene a gathering and through joint consultation choose one language from among the varied existing languages, or create a new one, to be taught to the children in all the schools of the world.

The day is approaching when all the peoples of the world will have adopted one universal language and one common script. When this is achieved, to whatsoever city a man may journey, it shall be as if he were entering his own home. These things are obligatory and absolutely essential. It is incumbent upon every man of insight and understanding to strive to translate that which hath been written into reality and action.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd; also Gleanings, CXVII

In this Tablet the language passage stands next to the call for a world assembly of rulers — the same passage the Promise quotes in Part III — and next to “The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.” The world gathering, the shared language and world citizenship appear together in Bahá’u’lláh’s text before they appear together in the House of Justice’s statement. The Tablet also gives the most personal picture of the result: a traveller in a strange city who feels at home. And it calls the matter “obligatory and absolutely essential,” asking every person of insight to help make it real.

The Words of Paradise: two languages, then one

“We have formerly ordained that people should converse in two languages, yet efforts must be made to reduce them to one, likewise the scripts of the world, that men’s lives may not be dissipated and wasted in learning divers languages. Thus the whole earth would come to be regarded as one city and one land.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Kalimát-i-Firdawsíyyih (Words of Paradise), eighth leaf

The first clause describes the auxiliary stage: every person speaks two languages. The second looks to the far stage. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 16 March 1946, which the House of Justice quoted in a letter written on its behalf on 29 December 1974, explains the relation:

“What Bahá’u’lláh is referring to in the Eighth Leaf of the Exalted Paradise is a far distant time, when the world is really one country, and one language would be a sensible possibility. It does not contradict His instructions as to the need immediately for an auxiliary language.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 16 March 1946

Two Tablets on purism and on the Houses of Justice

Bahá’u’lláh also addressed the opposite impulse: the attempt to purify a national language of foreign words. He wrote:

“Some of the peoples of the world have exerted the utmost effort to converse in their native tongues alone, considering this to be conducive to their exaltation and advancement, even as the people of Persia, who are adorned with the ornaments of talent and capacity, have sought to speak their ancient language and to purge it of all that is foreign. However, that which is pleasing in the sight of this Wronged One is that they should regard the whole earth as one country and strive, as hath been previously stated, to promote a single language or two so that the light of unity may envelop the whole earth and its inhabitants may receive their portion from the Sun of Knowledge and the Ocean of Wisdom.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Additional Tablets and Extracts from Tablets Revealed by Bahá’u’lláh

The passage does not criticise love of the mother tongue. It criticises the belief that a people rises by speaking only its own language. Here language and nationalism meet: the fifth issue in the Promise, “unbridled nationalism,” has a linguistic form, and Bahá’u’lláh’s answer to it is to “regard the whole earth as one country.”

In another Tablet He calls the Aqdas command a “categorical ordinance” revealed “for the people of the world in general and the members of parliaments in particular, inasmuch as the application of the laws and ordinances revealed in the Book hath been left to the men of divine Houses of Justice. This command is the greatest means of unity and the most powerful cause of fellowship and solidarity among the people of the world” (Additional Tablets).

A conversation in Constantinople

Bahá’u’lláh recorded a conversation from His time in Constantinople in 1863, in the last major work of His ministry:

“One day, while in Constantinople, Kamál Pá<u>sh</u>á visited this Wronged One. Our conversation turned upon topics profitable unto man. He said that he had learned several languages. In reply We observed: ‘You have wasted your life. It beseemeth you and the other officials of the Government to convene a gathering and choose one of the divers languages, and likewise one of the existing scripts, or else to create a new language and a new script to be taught children in schools throughout the world. They would, in this way, be acquiring only two languages, one their own native tongue, the other the language in which all the peoples of the world would converse.’ … When in Our presence, he acquiesced, and even evinced great joy and complete satisfaction. We then told him to lay this matter before the officials and ministers of the Government, in order that it might be put into effect throughout the different countries. However, although he often returned to see Us after this, he never again referred to this subject, although that which had been suggested is conducive to the concord and the unity of the peoples of the world.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf

The story shows the “paralysis of will” in one person. The minister agreed, was pleased, and did nothing. “You have wasted your life” is not contempt for learning languages — the Kitáb-i-Aqdas grants leave to anyone “that he be instructed in the divers tongues of the world” in order to teach the Faith (¶118). It is a judgement on a world that makes each person spend years on languages that a single agreed medium would make unnecessary.

Shoghi Effendi summarised Bahá’u’lláh’s teaching on the subject in God Passes By: “The necessity of adopting a universal tongue and script He repeatedly stresses; deplores the waste of time involved in the study of divers languages; affirms that with the adoption of such a language and script the whole earth will be considered as ‘one city and one land’; and claims to be possessed of the knowledge of both, and ready to impart it to any one who might seek it from Him.” The passage of Bahá’u’lláh behind that last clause was not located in the English texts searched for this page.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: the key to the box

The seventh candle

In a Tablet on the unity of mankind, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá describes seven candles of unity whose light “is now dawning upon the world’s darkened horizon.” The list ends with language:

“The first candle is unity in the political realm, the early glimmerings of which can now be discerned. The second candle is unity of thought in world undertakings, the consummation of which will erelong be witnessed. The third candle is unity in freedom which will surely come to pass. The fourth candle is unity in religion which is the cornerstone of the foundation itself, and which, by the power of God, will be revealed in all its splendor. The fifth candle is the unity of nations—a unity which in this century will be securely established, causing all the peoples of the world to regard themselves as citizens of one common fatherland. The sixth candle is unity of races, making of all that dwell on earth peoples and kindreds of one race. The seventh candle is unity of language, i.e., the choice of a universal tongue in which all peoples will be instructed and converse. Each and every one of these will inevitably come to pass, inasmuch as the power of the Kingdom of God will aid and assist in their realization.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, no. 15

The same Tablet explains why such unity is possible now and was not before: “In this day, however, means of communication have multiplied, and the five continents of the earth have virtually merged into one.” Unity of language, in this list, comes last, after unity of nations and of races — the same position the language paragraph holds in the Promise.

In His Tablet to the Central Organization for a Durable Peace at The Hague (17 December 1919), He lists the language among Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings for peace: “And among the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh is the origination of one language that may be spread universally among the people. This teaching was revealed from the pen of Bahá’u’lláh in order that this universal language may eliminate misunderstandings from among mankind” (First Tablet to The Hague).

Paris, November 1911

In the series of talks on Bahá’u’lláh’s principles that He gave in Paris beginning on 10 November 1911, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá placed the universal language under the eighth principle, universal peace:

“One of the great steps towards universal peace would be the establishment of a universal language. Bahá’u’lláh commands that the servants of humanity should meet together, and either choose a language which now exists, or form a new one. This was revealed in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas forty years ago. It is there pointed out that the question of diversity of tongues is a very difficult one. There are more than eight hundred languages in the world, and no person could acquire them all.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks, “The Eighth Principle—Universal Peace”

He continues: “A universal language would make intercourse possible with every nation. Thus it would be needful to know two languages only, the mother tongue and the universal speech.” And: “To be able to talk with a member of any race and country without requiring an interpreter, how helpful and restful to all!” He also names what the lack does: “Difference of speech is one of the most fruitful causes of dislike and distrust that exists between nations, which are kept apart by their inability to understand each other’s language more than by any other reason.” That sentence is close to a gloss on the Promise’s “fundamental lack of communication.”

North America, 1912

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá spoke of the universal language in at least six of His American talks published in The Promulgation of Universal Peace. The fullest is His message to Esperantists in Washington on 25 April 1912:

“Today the greatest need of the world of humanity is discontinuance of the existing misunderstandings among nations. This can be accomplished through the unity of language. Unless the unity of languages is realized, the Most Great Peace and the oneness of the human world cannot be effectively organized and established because the function of language is to portray the mysteries and secrets of human hearts. The heart is like a box, and language is the key. Only by using the key can we open the box and observe the gems it contains. Therefore, the question of an auxiliary international tongue has the utmost importance.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 25 April 1912

He goes on: “Therefore, the very first service to the world of man is to establish this auxiliary international means of communication. It will become the cause of the tranquillity of the human commonwealth.”

The image of the box and the key explains why a lack of communication undermines peace. People do not distrust each other only because their interests conflict; they also distrust what they cannot see. Language opens the heart of one person to another. Without a shared language, each people’s hopes stay closed to the others — the condition the Promise describes when it speaks of masses unable “to articulate their desire for a new order.”

At All Souls Unitarian Church in New York on 14 July 1912, He said:

“Diversity of languages has been a fruitful cause of discord. The function of language is to convey the thought and purpose of one to another. Therefore, it matters not what language man speaks or employs. Sixty years ago Bahá’u’lláh advocated one language as the greatest means of unity and the basis of international conference. He wrote to the kings and rulers of the various nations, recommending that one language should be sanctioned and adopted by all governments. According to this each nation should acquire the universal language in addition to its native tongue. The world would then be in close communication, consultation would become general, and dissensions due to diversity of speech would be removed.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 14 July 1912

“It matters not what language man speaks” removes any claim that one language is worthier than another. The language is a tool for conveying thought; the choice among candidates is practical. And “consultation would become general” connects the language to the consultation that Part III of the Promise places at the centre of peacemaking.

In Montreal on 1 September 1912 He added the effect on hearts: “Each person will require training in two languages: his native tongue and the universal auxiliary form of speech. This will facilitate intercommunication and dispel the misunderstandings which the barriers of language have occasioned in the world. All people worship the same God and are alike His servants. When they are able to communicate freely, they will associate in friendship and concord, entertain the greatest love and fellowship for each other, and in reality the Orient and Occident will embrace in unity and agreement.”

In other talks He listed the language among the numbered principles of Bahá’u’lláh: “Ninth, a universal language shall be adopted and be taught by all the schools and institutions of the world. A committee appointed by national bodies of learning shall select a suitable language to be used as a medium of international communication. All must acquire it. This is one of the great factors in the unification of man” (9 June 1912); “Eleventh, one language must be selected as an international medium of speech and communication. Through this means misunderstandings will be lessened, fellowship established and unity assured” (5 September 1912). Shoghi Effendi, summarising these journeys in God Passes By, included “the adoption of a universal auxiliary language” among “the essential elements of that Divine polity” that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá proclaimed in the West.

Esperanto: praise and a limit

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá spoke warmly of Esperanto, the constructed language created by Dr. L. L. Zamenhof. In Paris in 1911 He said: “Esperanto has been drawn up with this end in view; it is a fine invention and a splendid piece of work, but it needs perfecting. Esperanto as it stands is very difficult for some people.” And: “Therefore appreciate ‘Esperanto,’ for it is the beginning of the carrying out of one of the most important of the Laws of Bahá’u’lláh, and it must continue to be improved and perfected” (Paris Talks).

To a believer working among Esperantists after the First World War He wrote: “As to the Esperantists, associate with them. Whenever you find one with capacity, convey to him the fragrances of Life.” And, after a disappointing meeting at The Hague: “Grieve not over the apathy and coldness of the Hague meeting. Put thy trust in God. Our hope is that among the people the Esperanto language may hereafter have a powerful effect. Thou hast now sown the seed. Assuredly it will grow. Its growth dependeth upon God” (Selections, no. 228).

In a Tablet quoted in a compilation prepared at the Bahá’í World Centre He set a limit on that praise: “This language will be spread and universalized to a certain degree, but later on a language more complete than this, or the same language will undergo some changes and alterations and will be adopted and become universal. I hope that Dr. Zamenhof may become assisted by the invisible confirmation and do a great service to the world of humanity” (Tablets of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, vol. 3, p. 692, as quoted in the World Centre compilation “The Principle of an International Auxiliary Language”).

Edinburgh and Paris, 1913

Two further talks, both delivered to Esperanto societies, were printed in Star of the West (vol. 4, no. 2, 9 April 1913). The first was given in Edinburgh on 7 January 1913 under the auspices of the Edinburgh Esperanto Society, and the magazine reprinted it from The British Esperantist. The second was given at a banquet of the Paris Esperanto group on 12 February, from Persian notes translated by Mírzá Aḥmad Sohrab. These are reports of spoken words, not authenticated texts, and should be quoted as such. They contain the most concrete images in the whole literature on this subject.

In Edinburgh ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said: “Oneness of language creates oneness of heart.” He described a student from India, Persia, Turkestan or Arabia who comes to Britain to study medicine and “must first struggle with the English language for four years, to the exclusion of all else, before he can even begin the study of medicine.” He spoke of Himself: “I have studied oriental languages profoundly and know the Arabic better than the Arabians themselves. I have studied Turkish and Persian in my native land, besides other languages of the East, nevertheless, when I visited the West I had to take an interpreter with me quite as if I knew no language.” And He told a story:

“I recall an incident which occurred in Bagdad. There were two friends who knew not each other’s language. One fell ill, the other visited him, but not being able to express his sympathy in words, resorted to gesture, as if to say, ‘How do you feel?’ with another sign the sick replied, ‘I shall soon be dead;’ and his visitor, believing the gesture to indicate that he was getting better, said, ‘God be praised!’” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Edinburgh, 7 January 1913, as reported in Star of the West, vol. 4, no. 2

The report of the Paris banquet describes the occasion itself. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá spoke in Persian; Hippolyte Dreyfus-Barney interpreted into French; and “here and there one noted that the French translation was undergoing still further interpretation by Esperantists who had neighbors that did not understand French but knew Esperanto: the occasion itself offering a noteworthy argument for the great necessity of a universal tongue.” In the talk He said: “I am an Oriental and on this account I am shut out from your thoughts and you likewise from mine,” and “Their thoughts are closed in a casket—the universal language will be the master key to open it.” He also gave a historical example: after the Arab conquests, Copts, Syrians, Assyrians and others who had been hostile to one another came to speak Arabic “and they became as one nation.”

The classroom

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá also described how children should be taught languages. In a Tablet on education He tells the teacher to write a word on the board — “samá (heaven) in Arabic” — and ask for the Persian, then: “Later, let the teacher ask: ‘How do they say this in Russian, or French, or Turkish?’” By this method, “over a short period—that is, three years—the children will, as a result of writing the words down, become fully proficient in a number of languages, and will be able to translate a passage from one language to another” (Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks). The auxiliary language does not make the learning of languages unnecessary for everyone. It makes it unnecessary as a condition for understanding.

Shoghi Effendi: a world language among the institutions of a world society

In The Unfoldment of World Civilization (11 March 1936), Shoghi Effendi set the language among the institutions of the future world commonwealth:

“A mechanism of world inter-communication will be devised, embracing the whole planet, freed from national hindrances and restrictions, and functioning with marvellous swiftness and perfect regularity. A world metropolis will act as the nerve center of a world civilization, the focus towards which the unifying forces of life will converge and from which its energizing influences will radiate. A world language will either be invented or chosen from among the existing languages and will be taught in the schools of all the federated nations as an auxiliary to their mother tongue. A world script, a world literature, a uniform and universal system of currency, of weights and measures, will simplify and facilitate intercourse and understanding among the nations and races of mankind.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, “The Unfoldment of World Civilization”

The language appears here beside a world legislature, executive and tribunal — the institutions the Promise describes in Part III. It is one of the institutions of a united world. The phrase “as an auxiliary to their mother tongue” is repeated from the Aqdas note’s description of the first stage.

Letters written on his behalf: the principle, not a particular language

Letters written on behalf of the Guardian, collected in a compilation prepared at the World Centre, return again and again to one distinction: Bahá’ís support the principle of an auxiliary language and do not claim to know which language it will be. The clearest statement is from 17 October 1944:

“Regarding the whole question of an international language and its relation to the Faith: We, as Bahá’ís, are very anxious to see a universal auxiliary tongue adopted as soon as possible; we are not the protagonists of any one language to fill this post. If the Governments of the world should agree on an existing language, or a constructed, new tongue, to be used internationally, we would heartily support it because we desire to see this step in the unification of the human race take place as soon as possible.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 17 October 1944

Bahá’ís were encouraged to learn Esperanto and to co-operate with Esperantists, and many did. Martha Root represented the Faith at Esperanto congresses (letter of 5 April 1947), and Shoghi Effendi listed “the Universal Esperanto Congresses held annually in various capitals of Europe” among the gatherings in which Bahá’í communities took part (God Passes By). But a letter of 24 April 1939 warned against giving Esperantists “the impression that they consider Esperanto as necessarily constituting that international auxiliary language of the future referred to by Bahá’u’lláh and stressed by Him as an indispensable element in the upbuilding of the coming New World Order.” To do so “would not only be an act of dishonesty and disloyalty towards the Cause, but would lead to serious misunderstandings.” The same letter states the real ground of the friendship: “It is not so much that language as the central idea it embodies and inculcates which the Bahá’ís endorse.”

A letter of 18 May 1928, replying to a believer who was promoting English as the world’s auxiliary language, gives what the compilation calls a general principle for the choice: “the mere existence of prejudice is no argument against the possibility of making an existing language universal. The world must try to overcome its many defects and not reinforce it. Perhaps the main consideration in future will be the specific qualities of a language as being exact, rich and easy to learn for both East and West.”

And a letter of 12 December 1942, to a believer about to teach among the Arctic people the letter calls “Eskimo,” shows the principle’s limit in daily service. The Guardian would not advise teaching them Esperanto: “the most direct and quickest way of communicating with them in a common tongue should be chosen; in other words either you should learn their language or they yours, whichever will yield the quickest results.”

The Universal House of Justice: who will choose, and when

Two provisions in the Sacred Texts

Bahá’u’lláh addressed the command to parliaments, to sovereigns and ministers, to the nations, and to “the Trustees of the House of Justice.” A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 8 June 1980 explains how these fit together:

“You are quite correct in stating that there are two different provisions in the Sacred Texts for the selection of an International Auxiliary Language. On the one hand, this task is given to the governments of the world, on the other it is given to the House of Justice. It is not possible now to foresee exactly how this will come about, but it would seem reasonable to suppose that, long before the Bahá’í community is large enough or can exercise the authority to produce such a world-embracing change, events will compel the governments, either progressively or all in concert, to select an International Auxiliary Language to be taught as a second language in all schools and to be used in all international commerce. At a much later stage, possibly at the time of the Bahá’í World Commonwealth, the Universal House of Justice may well decide to review the situation and either confirm the decision that the governments had made, or change the choice to a more suitable language.” — Letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 8 June 1980

The letter adds: “One of the characteristics of Bahá’í Administration is its flexibility which enables it to deal with unforeseen developments and continually changing conditions. The one certain thing about the choice of an International Auxiliary Language is that the Universal House of Justice does not judge the present time propitious for it to take any action in this regard.”

Promote the principle

Other letters written on its behalf say the same from different directions:

  • 8 June 1971: “As to the most propitious time for the choosing of an international auxiliary language, we feel that it is not feasible for the House of Justice to make the choice at this time.”
  • 10 May 1974, to the Bahá’í Esperanto League: “whereas it is clear that the Bahá’í Faith upholds the principle of an international auxiliary language no decision as to which language this shall be has yet been made.”
  • 2 March 1976: “There is no doubt of the vital importance of the establishment of a universal language and it will inevitably come about but the believers have more urgent matters to attend to at the present and are asked to concentrate on teaching the Cause and winning the goals of the Five Year Plan.”
  • 6 October 1976: “it is the concept of an international language that the Bahá’ís are enthusiastic in supporting rather than any particular solution to the problem.”
  • 2 June 1982: “It is not yet timely for the House of Justice to make a pronouncement in favour of any particular language—the important thing now, in this particular field, is for Bahá’ís to promote the principle.”

In 1973 the House of Justice agreed to the formation of a League of Bahá’í Esperantists, after seeking the advice of the Hand of the Cause Adelbert Mühlschlegel “because of his long interest in Esperanto as an approach to overcoming the language obstacles which confront the world.” It hoped that Esperantists would “take the step from universality in language to the greater universalities of one religion and one mankind” (19 March 1973).

Three years after the last of these letters, the Promise carried the principle to the peoples of the world, asking that it receive “the most urgent attention.” The House of Justice’s own role in choosing remains a matter for a later stage; the Promise addresses the governments and peoples whom Bahá’u’lláh first addressed.

Is the language a prerequisite of the Lesser Peace?

A memorandum of the Research Department at the World Centre, dated 7 July 1994, considered whether adopting the language is a precondition of the Lesser Peace or a result of it. It reports that the Department “has not been able to locate any clear statement in the Bahá’í Writings that relates specifically to this question,” and points to the Promise’s paragraph as the most relevant statement. A memorandum of the Research Department is a research finding, not a ruling of the House of Justice. It is useful here because it marks the limit of what the texts say: the language would “go far” towards resolving a problem that “seriously undermines” peace, and the texts do not fix its order in the sequence of events.

The Babel of identities

The House of Justice used the image of Babel in its April 2002 letter to the world’s religious leaders, though about identities rather than languages. Having said that prejudices once thought inherent in human nature are giving way, it continues: “Down with them come barriers that long divided the family of man into a Babel of incoherent identities of cultural, ethnic or national origin.” Bahá’u’lláh Himself, in a Tablet answering a question about the records of ancient Prophets, mentions that diversities of language “arose in a later age, in a land known as Babel,” “the place where the confusion of tongues arose” (Gleanings, LXXXVII). That passage concerns the history of languages and the loss of ancient records; it does not present the auxiliary language as a reversal of Babel.

Other institutional voices

These documents carry the authority of the bodies that issued them, which is less than that of the House of Justice. They show how the principle has been applied.

  • The Bahá’í International Community, Turning Point for All Nations (1995). In a statement for the fiftieth anniversary of the United Nations, the Bahá’í International Community wrote that the United Nations, “which currently uses six official languages, would derive substantial benefit from either choosing a single existing language or creating a new one to be used as an auxiliary language in all its fora.” It proposed that a “high-level Commission” drawn from “linguistics, economics, the social sciences, education and the media” begin “careful study on the matter of an international auxiliary language and the adoption of a common script.” It added: “This proposal should be read narrowly. It does not in any way envision the decline of any living language or culture.” A note explains: “The Bahá’í teachings value and promote cultural diversity, not uniformity. At this point in history, then, we do not envision imposing a single language worldwide.”
  • The Bahá’í International Community, Rethinking Prosperity (3 May 2010), listing the organic changes in the structure of society that sustainable consumption will require, includes “the adoption of an international auxiliary language to facilitate mutual understanding.”
  • One Common Faith (2005), prepared under the supervision of the House of Justice, names “even so practical a principle as an international auxiliary language that will advance integration of the earth’s peoples” among the precepts that, for those who accept Bahá’u’lláh, “carry the same compelling authority as do the injunctions of scripture against idolatry, theft and false witness.”
  • Century of Light (2001) records of Iran: “As early as 1925, communities in a number of cities had instituted classes in Esperanto, in response to their awareness of the Bahá’í teaching that some form of auxiliary international language must be adopted.”

Auxiliary, not a replacement

Every authoritative statement of the principle keeps the mother tongue. Bahá’u’lláh: “only two languages, one their own native tongue, the other the language in which all the peoples of the world would converse.” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: “the mother tongue and the universal speech.” Shoghi Effendi: “as an auxiliary to their mother tongue.” The Aqdas note: “as an auxiliary to the mother tongues.” The Promise: an “auxiliary” language.

Bahá’u’lláh’s Writings also look to a far stage of “one single language and common script for all on earth,” and the Guardian placed it in “a far distant time, when the world is really one country, and one language would be a sensible possibility.” The present guidance does not ask anyone to work toward the disappearance of any language. The Promise quotes Shoghi Effendi’s assurance that the principle of oneness “disclaims all attempts at uniformity” and protects the diversity “of language and tradition.” The auxiliary language is how the peoples of the world can keep their many languages and still understand each other.

Why communication matters for peace: a reading

Taken together, the texts give four reasons why a lack of communication undermines peace and a shared language would go far to remedy it.

  1. Misunderstanding breeds distrust. “Difference of speech is one of the most fruitful causes of dislike and distrust that exists between nations” (‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris, 1911). The Hague Tablet says the language is meant to “eliminate misunderstandings from among mankind.” The Baghdad story shows how a kind visit can end in a cry of thanks for a friend’s death.
  2. Peoples must be able to speak for themselves. The Promise traces part of the paralysis of will to masses unable “to articulate their desire for a new order.” A shared second language lets ordinary people, not only governments and interpreters, reach each other.
  3. Consultation needs a common medium. With a universal language “consultation would become general” (‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, 1912). Part III of the Promise rests the path to peace on consultation among the nations.
  4. The language changes how people see the earth. “Thus the whole earth will come to be regarded as one country” (Bahá’u’lláh, Third Glad-Tidings); “as if he were entering his own home” (Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd). The language is a practical means to the wider loyalty the nationalism paragraph asks for, and a sign of the “coming of age” the whole statement describes.

The texts also give the limit of the remedy. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá called the language the key to the box; it does not fill the box. A letter of 25 January 1943 written on behalf of the Guardian made the point plainly: “With good will and determination an auxiliary language—especially one of the nature of Esperanto—can easily, and relatively quickly, be learned; whereas the Cause requires that people change not only certain ideas but their very characters and habits, and this is much harder to do and often takes a long time!” The Promise’s two points make the same point at the scale of nations: peace “stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude.”

The present field (external sources — description only, current as of October 2026)

The following describes the present state of the world’s languages and of international language policy. It is not authority for any Bahá’í principle, and the figures will change.

  • How many languages. Ethnologue’s “How many languages are there in the world?” page gives 7,164 languages in use and states that roughly 40% are endangered, often with fewer than 1,000 users. (The page could not be fetched directly for this article; these figures come from search results quoting it. Third-party sites attribute higher figures — 7,170 languages, about 44% endangered — to a 2026 edition, which could not be confirmed.) ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s “more than eight hundred languages” in 1911 reflects the knowledge of His time; the point — that no person could learn them all — holds more strongly now.
  • The United Nations. The United Nations still works in six official languages: Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish. Its General Assembly adopted a resolution on multilingualism (A/RES/78/330) on 6 September 2024, which stresses the equality of the six official languages and the use of non-official languages where useful. No United Nations body has taken up the Bahá’í International Community’s 1995 proposal for an auxiliary language for its fora.
  • Machine translation. Consumer earbuds and phones sold in 2026 advertise real-time speech translation, typically for about 100 languages, often depending on a cloud connection. These are vendor claims, not independent measurements, and they cover a small fraction of the world’s languages.

What this research found does not settle how machine translation relates to the principle. The texts consulted for this page do not address it. They describe a language taught to children in schools, chosen by deliberate agreement among nations, and spoken by people directly — the conditions under which “consultation would become general” and a traveller feels at home in any city. Whether translation technology will hasten such an agreement, delay it, or serve alongside it is not something the sources decide, and this article does not decide it either.

Open questions and limits

  • “Resolve” or “resolving.” The bahai.org text and Messages 1963–1986 read “go far to resolve this problem”; the bahai-library.com transcription and the 1994 Research Department memorandum read “go far to resolving.” This page quotes the bahai.org text. Which reading appeared in the first 1985 printing was not checked.
  • Provenance of the letters on the Guardian’s and the House of Justice’s behalf. Most of the letters quoted in the sections on Shoghi Effendi and the House of Justice are taken from “Materials Provided by the Bahá’í World Centre on Universal Auxiliary Language,” a set of documents the Department of the Secretariat sent to an individual on 15 August 2002, as transcribed on bahai-library.com. The 17 October 1944 letter is also printed in Principles of Bahá’í Administration and quoted in Turning Point for All Nations; the 4 June 1937 and 16 March 1946 letters also appear in Lights of Guidance (nos. 1140–1141). The others were found only in the bahai-library.com transcription.
  • The Zamenhof Tablet. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s Tablet on Esperanto (Tablets of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, vol. 3, p. 692) is not in this corpus in its own volume; it is quoted here from the World Centre compilation and Lights of Guidance. The early Tablets of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá translations are not authenticated to the standard of the bahai.org library.
  • The 1913 talks. The Edinburgh and Paris talks are reports in Star of the West, from a magazine reprint and from Persian notes. They are not authenticated texts, and their wording should not be presented as ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s exact words. A World Centre bibliography dates the Paris address 12 February 1912; Star of the West prints it in its issue of 9 April 1913, and ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá was in Paris in February 1913, not 1912. This page uses 1913.
  • The Paris Talks date. The Paris “Eighth Principle” talk is undated in Paris Talks; it belongs to the series that began on 10 November 1911.
  • “A single language or two.” Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablet on Persian purism speaks of promoting “a single language or two.” This page did not find an authoritative explanation of the phrase. It may refer to the two-language stage (mother tongue and auxiliary), but that reading is the author’s.
  • The Constantinople conversation. The year (1863) is from the length of Bahá’u’lláh’s stay in Constantinople, not from the passage itself.
  • “Claims to be possessed of the knowledge of both.” The Tablet in which Bahá’u’lláh says this, summarised by Shoghi Effendi in God Passes By, was not located in the English texts searched.
  • Prerequisite or consequence. Whether the auxiliary language must precede the Lesser Peace or will follow from it is not settled by any text found (see the 1994 memorandum above).
  • Lidia Zamenhof. The account of her life given in “For the song” comes from a secondary source (Bahá’í Chronicles, summarising Wendy Heller’s 1985 biography Lidia), not from the authoritative texts.

For the song

The heart of it, in one sentence. Peoples who cannot understand one another cannot build peace together, and Bahá’u’lláh’s remedy — one language, chosen by the nations and taught to every child beside the mother tongue — would let every people keep its own speech and still open its heart to every other, so that, as the House of Justice says, the matter “necessitates the most urgent attention.”

Concrete images, stories, people, places and moments

  • The box and the key — the heart holds its gems shut; language opens it (Washington, 1912). In Paris (1913) the same image as a casket and a master key.
  • The traveller at home — the stranger who walks into any city of the world and feels he has entered his own house.
  • Two signs of coming of age — Bahá’u’lláh names the choice of one language as one of two signs that humanity has grown up.
  • Seven candles — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s seven unities, the seventh being unity of language; this is the seventh issue in the Promise.
  • Constantinople, a minister and a wasted life — Kamál Páshá boasts of his many languages; Bahá’u’lláh tells him he has wasted his life and asks him to bring the plan to the government; he agrees with joy, visits often, and never mentions it again.
  • Two friends in Baghdad — one sick, one visiting; the gesture for “I shall soon be dead” is read as “I am getting better,” and the visitor says “God be praised!”
  • The Master and the interpreter — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, who knew Arabic, Persian and Turkish deeply, needed an interpreter in the West “quite as if I knew no language.”
  • The Paris banquet, February 1913 — Persian spoken, French interpreted, Esperanto whispered on to neighbours down the tables: one room, three languages, one meaning passed along.
  • The medical student — a young man from India or Persia spends four years on English before he can study medicine.
  • Eight hundred languages — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s 1911 figure; today counted at over seven thousand.
  • A blackboard — samá written in chalk, the children asked for the Persian, then the Russian, French or Turkish.
  • The seed at The Hague — a cold meeting, a discouraged believer, and ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s reply: “Thou hast now sown the seed.”
  • Lidia Zamenhof (1904–1942) — daughter of the creator of Esperanto, born in Warsaw; she met Martha Root, became a Bahá’í, made her pilgrimage in 1930, taught in the United States in 1937–38, returned to Poland, refused chances to leave her family in the Warsaw ghetto, and was killed at Treblinka in 1942 (secondary source; see limits).
  • Martha Root at the Esperanto congresses — the Guardian’s letters name her as the Faith’s representative there.
  • Esperanto classes in Iranian towns, 1925.
  • Six languages at the United Nations — the Bahá’í International Community’s 1995 proposal for one auxiliary language in all its fora.
  • A parliament of the world, a school in every land — the Aqdas addresses the legislators; the Glad-Tidings asks for the language in every schoolroom.

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • Not one language replacing all the others. The present principle is an auxiliary language, learned beside the mother tongue. The Promise itself quotes Shoghi Effendi protecting the diversity “of language and tradition” and disclaiming “all attempts at uniformity.” A song that celebrates the end of the world’s languages says the opposite of the texts. Bahá’u’lláh’s far-future “one language” belongs, in the Guardian’s words, to “a far distant time.”
  • Not a Bahá’í endorsement of Esperanto, or of English. “We are not the protagonists of any one language to fill this post.” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá praised Esperanto and also said a language “more complete” or a changed Esperanto would be adopted. The Guardian called it dishonest to let Esperantists think Bahá’ís considered their language the one foretold.
  • Not something Bahá’ís will impose. The governments of the world are to choose it, “either progressively or all in concert”; the House of Justice may review the choice in a much later age. A song should not picture believers enforcing a language on anyone.
  • Not a cure for war by itself. The Promise says the language would “go far”; it does not say it would suffice. Peace, the statement says, “stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude.” People who speak one language have fought each other often enough.
  • Not contempt for learning languages. “You have wasted your life” answers a minister’s pride in his many languages with a plan for everyone’s benefit. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas grants leave to learn many languages in order to teach, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá taught children several, and the Guardian told a teacher in the Arctic to learn the people’s language.
  • Not a claim about machine translation. The texts do not address it. A lyric may set the human picture — a child learning a second language, two strangers speaking without an interpreter — but should not say the Writings reject or welcome translating devices.
  • Not “Babel reversed” as doctrine. Bahá’u’lláh mentions Babel as history, and the House of Justice used “Babel” for divided identities. The image is available, but a lyric should not present the auxiliary language as the scriptural undoing of the Tower.
  • Attribute accurately. “Oneness of language creates oneness of heart” and the Baghdad story come from press reports of talks, not authenticated texts. The box-and-key image is from The Promulgation of Universal Peace. The Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd passage is Bahá’u’lláh’s, reproduced in Gleanings CXVII. The Lidia Zamenhof story is from a biography, not from the Writings.
