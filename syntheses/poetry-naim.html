Mírzá Muḥammad Ná’ím of Sídih as a poet

Last updated 17 October 2026

Mírzá Muḥammad, who wrote under the pen-name Ná’ím (“Blissful”), was a Persian Bahá’í poet who lived from 1856 to 1916. He was a farmer’s son from the village of Furúshán in the district of Sídih near Iṣfahán. He became a Bahá’í in 1880 or 1881, was beaten and driven from his village, and spent the rest of his life in Tihrán as a scribe, teacher and poet. Almost all of his verse explains or praises the Bahá’í Faith. In the corpus, one English article gives a small selection of his poems. This page summarises what the sources say of his life and work, quotes short extracts, and says what is missing. It is part of the series introduced in Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

His poems are not Bahá’í scripture and carry no authority in matters of belief (tier C). Neither ‘Abdu’l-Bahá nor Shoghi Effendi is found in the corpus saying anything about him (see “What is not settled”). Everything below about his life and verse comes from scholars and biographers, who are cited by name.

The last section of the page, “Other poets of the early Faith”, gives short notes on four other poets for whom the corpus has little or no English verse.

The poet

Life

The two fullest accounts in the corpus are by Hasan Balyuzi, in the chapter “Na’im of Sidih, a Poet Superb” in Eminent Baha’is in the Time of Bahá’u’lláh (Oxford: George Ronald, 1985), and by Roy P. Mottahedeh, “Ná’ím: A Bahá’í Poet”, World Order (Winter 1967), pp. 47–53. Mottahedeh says his account of the life “comes entirely from” the introduction to the edition of Ná’ím’s collected poems prepared by Muḥsin Ná’ímí and ‘Abdu’l-Ḥusayn Ná’ímí (New Delhi: Bahá’í Publishing Trust of India, 1961). Balyuzi also names Muḥsin-i-Ná’ímí as the poet’s biographer and son-in-law. The two accounts are therefore not independent. A third summary, in the Bahaipedia article, cites Balyuzi and Moojan Momen.

  • Ná’ím was born in the spring of 1856 in Furúshán, one of the three villages that made up Sídih. He was the only son of his father, who wanted him educated. He married young (at fifteen according to Mottahedeh, sixteen according to Balyuzi), could not afford to continue his studies, and worked on the land. A cousin, a merchant in Iṣfahán, later made him his agent in Sídih. (Mottahedeh, World Order, p. 48; Balyuzi ch. 11.)
  • He formed a circle of poets in Sídih with two brothers whose pen-names were Síná and Nayyir. Momen names the three as “well-known poets” of Sídih who became Bahá’ís in about 1879–80. (Moojan Momen, “Iran: Province of Isfahan”, `sources/bahai_library/momen_encyclopedia_isfahan.md:103`.)
  • In October 1880 Síná met Mírzá ‘Ináyatu’lláh ‘Alíábádí in Tabríz, who read to the travellers from the Tablet of the Bell (the Lawḥ-i-Náqús) and left it with them. Balyuzi writes that “All that narrative Na’im has put into a gripping and translucent poem.” (Balyuzi ch. 11; Mottahedeh, p. 49.) The Tablet itself is treated on the page on the Tablet of the Bell.
  • Balyuzi says that Ná’ím, studying the Bayán with an Azalí neighbour, concluded before he had read any writing of Bahá’u’lláh that the Bayán pointed to a coming Manifestation. He dates his own declaration to the year 1298 (4 December 1880 to 22 November 1881). Mottahedeh quotes Ná’ím: “In the year 1881,” writes Ná’ím, “I declared myself a Bahá’í.”
  • Ná’ím and four companions were tied together, beaten for many hours and imprisoned in Iṣfahán. Mottahedeh and Balyuzi both give Ná’ím’s own account at length. After release he was told to leave. A cleric declared his marriage void and his wife was married to someone else. He walked to Tihrán with three companions.
  • In Tihrán he lived in the Bábí quarter garden, copied Tablets, and taught the Bahá’ís’ children for a small wage. Later he taught Persian at the British Legation and prospered. In 1907, on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s instruction, he took over classes that trained teachers of the Faith (Bahaipedia, citing Momen). Adib Taherzadeh writes that he “conducted for several years” a class that taught a small number of Bahá’í youth, “most of whom became very prominent teachers of the Cause.”
  • He died in 1916, during the First World War (Mottahedeh, p. 53; Bahaipedia gives 14 March 1916).

A Tablet to Ná’ím

Adib Taherzadeh (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 3) tells a story from Ná’ím’s spoken chronicle. In Tihrán Ná’ím read a prayer of Bahá’u’lláh quoted in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf and wished in his heart that it had been revealed for him. Some time later a Tablet in his name arrived from the Holy Land. It contained the same prayer. Taherzadeh adds: “In His Tablet to Na’im, Bahá’u’lláh addresses him in words of loving-kindness, states that He has re-revealed the prayer as a token of His favours.” Taherzadeh’s notes give the sources as Ná’ím’s chronicle in Gulzár-i-Ná’ím and the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, p. 95. The text of the Tablet to Ná’ím is not in the corpus. Taherzadeh introduces Ná’ím as “a believer of remarkable faith and devotion and a poet of outstanding calibre.”

His work

Mottahedeh writes that Ná’ím “composed long poems in all metres” from his youth, and that his greatest pleasure was in the ghazal, the short, often amatory lyric. After he became a Bahá’í, Mottahedeh says, he “wrote almost all his poetry to explain the Bahá’í Faith”. He wrote on the Prophets, quoted prophecies from the Bible, the Qur’án and the Avesta, and treated events such as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s journey to America in 1912.

The collected poems were published by his son and son-in-law as Aḥsanu’t-Taqvím yá Gulzár-i Ná’ím (New Delhi, 1961; the bahai-library copy of Mottahedeh’s article prints the title as “Ahsam”, a scanning error: the World Order copy in `sources/world_order/S2_V02_I02.md:3281` reads “Ahsan”). Mottahedeh says the title is taken from Qur’án 95:4 and that Ná’ím seems to have chosen it himself.

Other titles that appear in the corpus:

  • Istidlálíyyih I and II (Tabríz, 1911 and 1912), a book of proofs in verse. Taherzadeh calls it “an enchanting book of proofs in verse” used in study circles in Persia.
  • A volume printed in Delhi in 1915 and listed as poems by Ná’ím, whose title the scan renders as “Magdliy-i-Bahd’i”.
  • Natíjatu’l-Bayán, listed as compiled by Mírzá Ná’ím.

These three are listed in bibliographies in The Bahá’í World volumes 8, 10 and 12 (for example `sources/bahairesearch/english/Baha’i/Baha’i_Studies/Baha’i_World_Volumes/Volume__8.md:18998–18999`), where the scan is poor. The corpus has no Persian text of any of these.

A Bahá’í News article of 1976 (`sources/bahai_news_magazine/540.md:157`) records an observation attributed to Ná’ím: the Dawn Prayer (Du‘á-yi-Saḥar) begins with the name Bahá’ and ends with ‘Alá’, the names of the first and nineteenth months of the Badí‘ calendar. This is a commentary by a poet on a text, not a poem.

Balyuzi gives his judgement of Ná’ím’s rank: “a poet of the first rank”. Of his character he writes: “Na’im, an undoubted master of verse, of a poetic ability rarely matched in his days, was a very unassuming man.” A Bahá’í World article of 2019 lists him among “distinguished poets”: Ṭáhirih, Varqá, Ná’ím and ‘Andalíb (`sources/news/bahaiworld/2019-11-26_tales-of-magnificent-heroism.md:88`).

The text in English

All English text of Ná’ím’s verse in the corpus is in Mottahedeh’s 1967 article ([source]). The article names no separate translator, so the renderings are Mottahedeh’s own. They are labelled below as provisional translation by Roy P. Mottahedeh (1967). They are not a Bahá’í-authorised translation and do not carry the authority of the Writings. The bahai-library copy is marked “fair use”. Ná’ím died in 1916, so his Persian is out of copyright in most countries, but Mottahedeh’s renderings are not, so a published lyric that quotes them needs permission (`permission-needed`). Short extracts only are given here. The poem numbers are those of Aḥsanu’t-Taqvím as Mottahedeh cites them.

The ghazal and the Beloved

Ná’ím wrote of the ghazal (lines 93–95 of the corpus file; the poem is not numbered):

The ghazal is the most pleasant of the poetic arts, For in the ghazal, the writer’s nature is turned to the Beloved.

Poem no. 122: loss and gain

Mottahedeh gives the poem’s nine couplets in full after the account of Ná’ím’s exile. They list what the poet gave up (body and life, power, money and property, a house, family and kin) and end with a couplet on trade (lines 157–191). The editor of the Persian text, Mottahedeh says, notes Qur’ánic verses recalled in nearly every line, including 9:112, “God has bought from the believers their selves and their possessions”, and 35:26(29), on “a merchandise that shall not perish”. The last couplet reads:

There is no commerce better than this; This is a commerce in which there is no loss.

Balyuzi tells a story about another line of this poem. Years later, when Ná’ím was prosperous and was having a house built, a visitor reminded him of the couplet about “the mud and brick of this abode so fleeting”. Ná’ím answered that the house itself would serve the “home everlasting”, because believers would gather in it and remember their Lord. (Balyuzi, ch. 11.)

A traditional spring poem

This is poem no. 185. Mottahedeh calls this “One of Ná’ím’s best poems in the traditional style” (lines 241–299). It describes spring as an army and a court, with breezes sent “As prophets and messengers” to wake the dead branches. Two of its lines:

The nightingale like a court preacher sings congratulations. Lightning has struck with its sword and thunder pounded its drum.

The poem ends by quoting the Qur’án (36:78) on how God “gives life to bones when they are decayed”. Mottahedeh’s point is that Persian poets had a shared stock of images, so that one image could turn into another and a reader could take the poem as about spring, a king or God.

The Bahariyyih

Mottahedeh calls the Bahariyyih (a spring poem, on p. 163 of the collected poems) “probably Ná’ím’s most successful long poem”. It is a musammaṭ, a poem in stanzas whose last lines share one rhyme. It has forty-one stanzas, and he says Ná’ím modelled it on the eleventh-century poet Manúchihrí. Mottahedeh adds that parts of it are riddles known to Persians who do not know a Bahá’í wrote them. He gives three stanzas (lines 305–337). One line:

Once again nature, the ruby cutter, has cut its gems,

This stanza is the riddle of the pomegranate, which the stanza’s last line names.

On ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s visit to America

Mottahedeh gives a poem on the visit of 1912 (poem no. 190, lines 343–403). Its themes are the unity of East and West and of all peoples. It includes:

The sun of the East has come up in the West, But the East has not therefore become dark,

and, near the end:

”This is the time for the promised love of mankind; The time for the unity of men has come.“

Modern things and war

Mottahedeh says Ná’ím was one of the first Persian poets to mention steamships and the telegraph. Six lines are given (poem no. 248):

Cities have become close to each other; Why have hearts remained far apart?

The last poems, written during the war, name weapons in a long list (“regiments, cannons, and planes”, rifles, bombs, “blimps”) and then conclude (poem no. 193, lines 425–459):

Half of us spill the blood of the other half For the desires of three or four bloodthirsty men.

The poem ends with a call for “A peace-seeking parliament of man”.

Themes and images

  • Everything given up, and what was gained. Poem 122 uses the language of trade to describe persecution, and the Qur’ánic verses recalled in it already use the same language. The persecution it follows is described in plain prose by Ná’ím himself, in the passages Mottahedeh and Balyuzi quote: five men tied together, beaten for fourteen hours, walked barefoot through snow. Ná’ím’s companion, he writes, said during the march: “God has tied our hands and has brought us in the midst of this crowd as a proof for all people.” In this poem the sacrifice is stated as a deal that “has no loss”, not as a complaint.
  • The Beloved. He says the ghazal turns “the writer’s nature” to the Beloved. Mottahedeh stresses that classical Persian images served “interchangeably for human or divine love”.
  • Spring as the time of the Promised One. In Persian poetry spring is the season of renewal. Ná’ím’s spring poems use this tradition, and the breezes sent “as prophets and messengers” make the link to religion explicit.
  • The unity of peoples, and the modern world. The 1912 poem and the steamship lines treat nearness in space as a reason for nearness of hearts, which is a theme of the Bahá’í teaching on the oneness of mankind (see Promises of the future).
  • War and the need for a peace-making authority. The late poem says “For sixty years this Revelation has said” that a parliament of man is needed, and asks for a king above the kings of the world. Here the poet presents his own call as the message of the Revelation.

What is not settled

  • No translator’s name and no Persian text. The corpus has no Persian text of any poem by Ná’ím, and Mottahedeh does not print the original. The renderings cannot be checked against the Persian here, and the lines of verse have no stated metre or rhyme. The saved web originals in `sources/web/poetry/originals/` do not include any poem by Ná’ím.
  • Date of the persecution. Mottahedeh gives 1881 (“at the age of twenty-five”), consistent with Ná’ím’s own statement that he declared in 1881. Balyuzi dates the declaration between December 1880 and November 1881. Bahaipedia, citing Momen’s The Bahá’í Communities of Iran vol. 2, p. 52, says the beating and exile happened in 1883, that he was arrested again in March 1887, and that he moved to Tihrán in 1892. These cannot all be right and the sources do not reconcile them.
  • Number of persecutions. Mottahedeh and Balyuzi describe one episode with four companions, and Bahaipedia says Ná’ím was paraded with “two other Bahá’ís” and a ring through the nose, citing Momen. This may be a second incident or a different account of the same one. Taherzadeh gives “four other believers”.
  • Date of death. Mottahedeh and Bahaipedia say 1916. A dispatch from Haifa dated 11 February 1919, printed in Star of the West X:11, speaks of “the reported death of Nayyirih Lina, Bamandar and Mírzá Na’ím of Ṭihrán” as one of the losses of the war years (`sources/bahairesearch/english/Baha’i/Authoritative_Baha’i/Abdu’l-Baha/SOW_-_Star_of_the_West/Star_of_the_West.md:19883`). The dispatch says “reported”, and it may refer to him or may not.
  • Nothing from the Central Figures or the Guardian about him as a poet. A search of Memorials of the Faithful, God Passes By and the other authorised works in the corpus finds no passage on Ná’ím the poet. The only authoritative text that concerns him is the Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh to him, known only through Taherzadeh. Two other men called Mírzá Na’ím appear in the corpus and are not this poet: the officer who helped Ḥusayn Khán, the governor of Shíráz, in God Passes By (GPB V.32: “His accomplice, Mírzá Na‘ím, fell into disgrace”), the former governor of Nayríz in a story retold by Taherzadeh (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2) and in Salmání’s memoirs, and the persecutor Muḥammad-Ṣádiq Ná’ím-Ábádí of Manshád. The name Ná’ím also occurs in the surname Ná’ímí, the family of his son and son-in-law.
  • Whether a machine-drafted or other translation is warranted. No machine-drafted translation of Ná’ím’s verse has been made, since the Persian is not in the corpus.

For songwriting

Ná’ím gives little exact English text that may be sung, because the available lines are Mottahedeh’s and need permission. These notes are for planning a lyric.

Short exact phrases (Mottahedeh, 1967, provisional translation, `permission-needed`; the Persian poem is not copyrighted but this English is):

  • “This is a commerce in which there is no loss.” (poem 122)
  • “The sun of the East has come up in the West” (poem 190, on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s visit)
  • “The time for the unity of men has come.” (poem 190)
  • “Why have hearts remained far apart?” (poem 248)
  • “A peace-seeking parliament of man” (poem 193)
  • “Once again nature, the ruby cutter, has cut its gems” (Bahariyyih)

Images. Spring as an army with lightning for a sword and thunder for a drum; breezes sent like prophets to wake dead branches; a nightingale as court preacher; rubies strung and hidden in a box (the pomegranate); a chain of five prisoners walking barefoot in snow; a samovar heated with dung (Balyuzi, ch. 11); ships driven by fire.

A possible arc. A village poet in a literary circle hears of the Tablet of the Bell; he declares his faith; he is beaten and driven out of the village; he walks to the capital with nothing; he writes that he has traded everything and lost nothing; he sees ships and telegraphs bring cities near and asks why hearts are still far; at the end of his life, in a war, he asks for a parliament of humankind. The arc follows the order of the poems in Mottahedeh’s article, so it is anchored in the sources.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • Do not present Ná’ím’s lines as the Writings, or as authorised translations. They are one scholar’s renderings of a Bahá’í poet’s Persian.
  • Do not say ‘Abdu’l-Bahá or Shoghi Effendi praised his poetry. The corpus does not show it. Balyuzi and Taherzadeh praise it.
  • Do not put a date on the beating or on his death without noting the disagreement above.
  • Do not say Bahá’u’lláh gave the prayer to Ná’ím in answer to a wish as a fact about the Tablet. It is Ná’ím’s own account, reported by Taherzadeh.
  • Do not attach the name Mírzá Na’ím to the governor of Nayríz.

Other poets of the early Faith

These notes say only what the corpus says. None of these poets has a poem in English in the corpus apart from the single couplet noted below. Their works are not scripture.

Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam (Mullá Muḥammad-i-Zarandí, 1831–1892)

The Bahaipedia article names him as the author of The Dawn-Breakers. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Memorials of the Faithful, describes him as a poet. In the section on Nabíl-i-Zarandí (MF 10), He says that in Baghdad Nabíl “spent his days in gladness now, writing odes to celebrate the praises of his Lord” and “was a gifted poet” (MF 10.2, `sources/reference_library/memorials-faithful.md:322`). In ‘Akká, “Day and night he sang the praises of the one Beloved of both worlds and of those about His threshold, writing verses in the pentameter and hexameter forms, composing lyrics and long odes” (MF 10.7, line 332). In the closing paragraph: “In particular his ode “Bahá, Bahá!” was written in sheer ecstasy.” (MF 10.11, line 340.)

The epithet “Poet-Laureate” is Shoghi Effendi’s. In God Passes By he writes of “Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam, who may well rank as His Poet-Laureate, His chronicler and His indefatigable disciple” (GPB VIII.9, `sources/reference_library/god-passes-by.md:537`). Marzieh Gail’s footnote to the Nabíl section of the Memorials quotes the phrase and refers to that paragraph (`sources/reference_library/memorials-faithful.md:1713`). The ode “Bahá, Bahá!” is named only. The corpus has no text of it.

Darvísh Ṣidq-‘Alí

‘Abdu’l-Bahá says in MF 11.2: “He had a fine poetic gift and wrote odes to sing the praises of Him Whom the world has wronged and rejected.” The section gives one couplet, from a poem he wrote as a prisoner in the barracks at ‘Akká (`sources/reference_library/memorials-faithful.md:353`), in the translation by Marzieh Gail as published by the Bahá’í Publishing Trust (the Memorials is a work of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá; the translator is cited as scholarship):

A hundred hearts Thy curling locks ensnare, And it rains hearts when Thou dost toss Thy hair.

On the journey from Baghdad, “filled with yearning love, he would sing poems” (MF 11.4, line 358).

‘Andalíb (Áqá ‘Alí-Ashraf, d. 1920)

The Bahaipedia article says he was “well-known for his poetry”, lived in Shíráz from 1889, and “composed poetry praising ‘Abdu’l-Bahá supporting Him as the appointed successor of Bahá’u’lláh” (`sources/bahaipedia/andalíb.md:9` and `:21`). It records that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá revealed the words for his tombstone, citing Balyuzi: “Verily, life in the nest of this world was too confined for ‘Andalíb, the beloved.” (`sources/bahaipedia/andalíb.md:26`; the name means “nightingale”). That text is prose. The corpus has no poem by him in English, and a search of Memorials of the Faithful and God Passes By finds no mention of him.

Varqá (Mírzá ‘Alí-Muḥammad, c. 1856–1896)

The Bahaipedia article says that besides teaching he was a physician and wrote poetry (`sources/bahaipedia/varqá.md:30`). Memorials of the Faithful names him only as a martyr (`sources/reference_library/memorials-faithful.md:65` and `:782`), not as a poet. The corpus has no English text of his verse. The 2004–06 anthology Reunion with the Beloved: Poetry and Martyrdom (Hatcher and Hemmat) is listed in the corpus as including Varqá, but the corpus copy has only the front matter ([source]).

A source for further work

A 2019 Bahá’í World article says that Baydá’í’s Memoir of the Poets of the First Bahá’í Century “contains biographical notes about, and examples of work by, no fewer than 134 Bábí and Bahá’í poets” (`sources/news/bahaiworld/2019-11-26_tales-of-magnificent-heroism.md:88`). That work is not in the corpus. It is the natural place to look for further early poets and Persian text.
