Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá: Bahá’u’lláh’s poem of the Cupbearer

Last updated 17 October 2026

Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá burqa‘ bar afkan az ‘idhár is a Persian poem by Bahá’u’lláh. It has fifteen lines, rhymes throughout on -ár, and is signed with the pen name “Darvísh” (Dervish). The title is the opening phrase: the speaker calls on the Cupbearer (sáqí) to lift a veil from the unseen realm. The speaker asks the Cupbearer for the wine of eternity, tells the seeker what the path costs, and ends with a call to revive the dead. This page is one of a series; see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

One couplet of the poem has an authorised English translation, because Bahá’u’lláh quotes it in The Seven Valleys. There is no authorised translation of the whole poem. The English text available for the rest is a set of three provisional versions by Franklin Lewis (2001), which this page quotes only briefly.

The poem

Where and when

Bahá’u’lláh left Baghdad in April 1854 for the mountains of Kurdistan, according to Lewis’s account of the dates in God Passes By, and later lived in Sulaymáníyyih. Shoghi Effendi describes the first part of that period:

“Attired in the garb of a traveler, coarsely clad, taking with Him nothing but his kashkúl (alms-bowl) and a change of clothes, and assuming the name of Darvísh Muḥammad, Bahá’u’lláh retired to the wilderness, and lived for a time on a mountain named Sar-Galú”

(Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VII.35; authorised text)

The next paragraph of God Passes By mentions the writings of that seclusion in general terms:

“In the odes He revealed, whilst wrapped in His devotions during those days of utter seclusion, and in the prayers and soliloquies which, in verse and prose, both in Arabic and Persian, poured from His sorrow-laden soul, many of which He was wont to chant aloud to Himself, at dawn and during the watches of the night”

(Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VII.36; authorised text)

God Passes By does not name this poem. Adib Taherzadeh does, in his account of the Kurdistan period:

“Apart from this poem, some prayers and meditations were revealed by Bahá’u’lláh in Kurdistan, which are written in His own hand and have been preserved for posterity. Among them is another poem known as Saqi-Az-Ghayb-i-Baqa. This is in Persian and again, like other odes of Bahá’u’lláh, is soul-stirring and very beautiful.”

(Adib Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, p. 64; scholarship)

“This poem” in the first sentence is the Arabic Ode of the Dove (Qaṣídiy-i-Varqá’íyyih), which Taherzadeh has just described. He summarises the Persian poem in these words:

“It expresses His longing for the day when the glory of His Countenance will be unveiled to men and the splendours of His Revelation will shed their light upon them.”

(Taherzadeh, vol. 1; scholarship)

Form and the pen name

Lewis, in his introduction (Lights of ‘Irfán 2, pp. 83–85), calls the poem a ghazal. He identifies the metre as the catalectic eight-foot ramal (the same family as the Mathnavíy-i-Mubárak, which uses the six-foot form) and notes the single mono-rhyme. The pen name “Darvísh” stands in the first half of line 15, the last line of the poem, which is the usual place for a ghazal poet to sign. Lewis counts at least eight poems that Bahá’u’lláh signed this way. The same note says the name has a second sense: “Dervish Muhammad” was the name Bahá’u’lláh used while living incognito among the Sufis of Sulaymáníyyih. The corpus has this introduction only as a PDF outside `sources/`, so this page summarises it and does not quote it.

Lewis dates the Dervish poems to the mid-1850s. He reads God Passes By as placing them in the earlier phase at Sar-Galú, between spring 1854 and winter 1854–55, but says he cannot rule out the Sulaymáníyyih lodge or the time just after the return to Baghdad. The abstract on the Bahá’í Library page describes the poem as one of Bahá’u’lláh’s early poems, written “during his sojourn in Kurdistan.” See “What is not settled” below.

The Persian text

The Persian text on which this page relies is from Má’idiy-i-Ásmání, vol. 4, pp. 209–211 (the edition by ‘Abdu’l-Ḥamíd Ishráq-Khávarí, which Lewis also used). The first couplet, which sets the address to the Cupbearer, and the last, which carries the signature, read:

ساقی از غیب بقا برقع بر افکن از عذار تا بنوشم خمر باقی از جمال کردگار

درویش جهان سوخت از این شعلهٔ جانسوز الهی وقت آنست که کنی زنده از این نغمه زار

(Bahá’u’lláh, Persian original; text of the Writings, saved copy [source])

The text in English

Authorised translation: one couplet. In The Seven Valleys, in the Valley of Love, Bahá’u’lláh quotes two lines that the authorised translation’s note 27 identifies only as “From a poem of Bahá’u’lláh”:

Kindle the fire of love and burn away all things;

Then set thy foot into the land of the lovers.

(Bahá’u’lláh, The Seven Valleys, CDB 2.22; authorised translation)

The Persian of these two lines matches the fourth couplet of this poem in the saved text (nár-i-‘ishqí bar furúz va jumlih hastí-há bisúz / pas qadam bardár va andar kúy-i-‘ushsháqán guzár). The note does not name the poem; the identification rests on that comparison. The rest of the poem has no authorised translation. Lewis observes in his introduction that Shoghi Effendi did not translate Bahá’u’lláh’s versified poems; Shoghi Effendi did render one passage of the Ode of the Dove in God Passes By (see The Ode of the Dove).

Provisional translation by Franklin D. Lewis (2001). Published as “A Short Poem by ‘Darvísh’ Muhammad, Bahá’u’lláh,” Lights of ‘Irfán 2 (Wilmette, IL: Irfan Colloquia, 2001), pp. 83–92, with the status “Permission: author and publisher” on the Bahá’í Library page. Lewis gives three versions, each described as provisional:

  1. A lineated prose version, one half-line per line, numbered 1a to 15b. It is the closest to the Persian.
  2. A free-verse version, with line breaks chosen for rhythm and sense and a little more interpretive freedom.
  3. A double sonnet of thirty lines, joined by a closing couplet. Lewis offers it as an experiment to show what a rhymed English form gains and loses. The line breaks of this version in the saved copy were rebuilt from the PDF layout, so it is not quoted here.

In the prose version the first line asks the Cupbearer to cast aside the veil from “the cheeks of the unseen immortal realm” (Lewis 1a; provisional translation by Franklin D. Lewis, 2001). The Persian word ‘idhár means cheek, so the request is to lift a veil from the face of the unseen realm.

Themes and images

The poem has five movements. The line numbers are Lewis’s.

1. The request to the Cupbearer (lines 1–3). The sáqí is the person who pours wine at a feast. In Persian poetry the call “O Sáqí” opens many poems. The speaker asks for the wine of eternity, drawn from the Creator’s beauty, asks for an ocean of the wine of inner meaning, and asks that he himself, veiled and frenzied with love, be roused to cry out. Lewis notes that the phrase can mean one hidden and mad with love, or one hidden by the madness of love.

Lewis explains the wine as a tradition: in some poems it is carousal, in some it dulls the world’s sorrows, and in others it is the intoxication of being in the presence of the numinous, or of love that overrides reasoning. He places this poem in the last group.

2. The fire and the path (lines 4–6). The Cupbearer is told to kindle a fire of love that burns up all existing things, and the seeker is then to step into the lovers’ precincts. Until the seeker is effaced of all attributes of creation, he cannot taste the wine of the Beloved’s lips. Then comes the sequence of line 6: climb to the top of the realm and “enter Poverty’s sheltering shade” (Lewis 6a), from where the Eternal realm is visible on every side.

Lewis points out that these lines allude to stages of the Seven Valleys: the Valley of Love, and the Valley of True Poverty, which is the last of the seven. The authorised translation of the Seven Valleys says of Love:

“he shall straightway step into THE VALLEY OF LOVE and be consumed in the fire of love”

(Bahá’u’lláh, The Seven Valleys, CDB 2.14; authorised translation)

and of Poverty:

“This station is that of dying to the self and living in God, of being poor in self and rich in the Desired One.”

(Bahá’u’lláh, The Seven Valleys, CDB 2.76; authorised translation)

The Seven Valleys was written after the return to Baghdad. The comparison shows the poem’s vocabulary (fire of love, effacement, poverty) is the one the later prose work uses.

3. The conditions (lines 7–8). The path is closed to anyone whose heart is occupied with concern for life; the one who may come is the one willing to give up heart and soul (Lewis 7b: “If you have a heart and soul to scatter, come forth and proffer it”). The custom of the path, the speaker says, applies if one hopes to be joined with “Bahá,” and anyone not fit for it is told to leave. The speaker’s voice is sharp here.

4. What the seeker may then see (lines 9–11). With the eye of admonition open and the paths of pride closed, the seeker will see Moses’ Mountain circling the place, the spirit of Jesus restless with love, a tablet affirming the Divine Unity in the two braids of the Friend, and a scroll sanctifying God’s Essence in the two cheeks of the Beloved. Lewis notes that the two terms behind these lines, tawḥíd (affirming God’s unity) and tajríd (sanctifying God from all attributes), are a pair from Islamic theology that Bahá’u’lláh also pairs in rhyme position in the Mathnavíy-i-Mubárak. The Friend’s braids and cheeks are the conventional features of the Beloved in Persian love poetry; here they are the text in which the believer is to read.

5. The assembly of the dead and the signature (lines 12–15). The seeker is told to drink the wine of joy from the fount of life and throw down his head at the Friend’s feet. The assembly is dead “in the path of the Friend,” and the speaker calls on the Messiah of the age to breathe life into it so that the birds of existence may fly from the body’s prison to the placeless space. The last couplet addresses the Dervish: the world is consumed by a divine flame, and it is time to bring it to life with a plaintive lament.

The Cupbearer in the Writings

The Writings use the same figure in prose. In the Persian Hidden Words:

“Take from the hands of the divine Cupbearer the chalice of immortal life, that all wisdom may be thine, and that thou mayest hearken unto the mystic voice calling from the realm of the invisible.”

(Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, HW Persian 62; authorised translation)

and:

“he hath drunk the cup of eternal life at the hands of the immortal Cupbearer”

(Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, HW Persian 58; authorised translation)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Memorials of the Faithful speaks of one who “drank the red wine of knowledge from the hands of the Cupbearer of grace” (Memorials of the Faithful, Mullá Muḥammad-‘Alí section). In each of these places the Cupbearer is the one through whom the wine of God’s bounty is given. The poem does not say who its Cupbearer is. Lewis presents the figure as the conventional addressee of Persian wine poetry, whose wine here stands for mystical intoxication; the passages above, in the Writings, name the Cupbearer as divine or immortal.

What is not settled

  • Date and place. The sources agree on the Kurdistan period, mid-1850s. Lewis’s reading of God Passes By puts the Dervish poems in the earlier phase at Sar-Galú; he does not exclude the Sulaymáníyyih lodge or the first months after the return to Baghdad. God Passes By itself does not name the poem, so the dating is an inference from the surrounding account.
  • Who “Bahá” is in line 8. Lewis notes that among the Sufis of Sulaymáníyyih, where the author was known as Dervish Muhammad, “Bahá” would have been taken as a reference to Bahá’u’d-Dín Naqshband, the founder of the Naqshbandí order. When the poem reached a Bábí audience, which Lewis thinks it later did in Baghdad, it would have been read as a reference to Bahá’u’lláh. The poem therefore supports both readings, and the sources do not say which the author intended.
  • How openly the poet speaks. According to Lewis, the poems signed “Darvísh” do not claim revelation in their own voice. Messianic themes in them can be read as veiled allusions in light of the later Declaration in April 1863, but a Sufi audience could have heard them as ordinary Sufi poetry. A page about this poem should not say it announces the Declaration.
  • The Messiah of the age (line 13). The poem addresses a figure with this title and asks for breath to revive a dead assembly. The sources consulted do not say who is meant. Taherzadeh, quoted above, describes the poem as a longing for the unveiling of Bahá’u’lláh’s own Revelation, which is one reading, but he does not discuss this line.
  • The metre of line 15a. Lewis observes that the hemistich with the signature (15a) is in a different metre from the rest. He suggests it may have been quoted from another poem or added later, and notes that other poems of Bahá’u’lláh also shift metre.
  • Variant text. A second text of what appears to be the same opening occurs in the Majmú‘ih-yi-Mubárak (INBA vol. 30, at `sources/afnan_library/inba/inba_v030.md:1778`) with different words in several places, for example khamr-i jání where the Má’idiy-i-Ásmání text has khamr-i báqí. The corpus copy is OCR text with evident errors, so this page does not rely on it. The variants have not been compared with the manuscripts.
  • Translation choices. Lewis’s own notes flag the doubtful readings: whether mastúr-i shaydá’í means “hidden and mad” or “hidden by madness,” whether daftar should be “tablet,” and whether mushaf should be “scroll.” His three versions differ on these points.

A separate poem: Sáqí bidih ábí

A different Dervish poem also begins with an address to the Cupbearer: Sáqí bidih ábí zán shu‘la-yi rúḥání. Its opening in Persian is found in the Majmú‘ih-yi-Mubárak (`sources/afnan_library/inba/inba_v030.md:1981`), which reads “ساقی بده آبی” followed by words that the OCR does not render reliably. Bahaipedia lists it as one of eight Dervish poems separate from Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá, with a gloss of “O Cup-bearer, give me a drop of the mystic flame,” and names Julio Savi (Lights of ‘Irfán 16) and Stephen Lambden as having translated parts of it provisionally. Bahaipedia is a secondary source. This page treats the two as distinct poems on the evidence of their different opening words and their separate entries in that list; it has not read either provisional translation of the second poem.

Images. The cupbearer and the veil lifted from a face; wine drawn from a source out of sight; an ocean of wine; a fire that burns away everything but the lovers; a long road narrowing to a door marked Poverty; a mountain that moves to circle the seeker; braids and cheeks as pages of a scroll; an assembly of the dead; birds leaving a cage; a lament ending the poem.

A possible arc. Thirst and request (the Cupbearer, the veil); the cost (fire, effacement, poverty); the test (the heart that is willing to give); the opening of sight (Moses’ Mountain, Jesus, the scroll); the plea for breath on the dead assembly; the release of the birds; a closing lament in a lower voice. The speaker should sound urgent and a little stern in the middle, as the poem does, and gentler at the end.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • That this English is the text of the Writings. It is a provisional translation by one scholar; only the couplet quoted in The Seven Valleys has an authorised translation.
  • That the poem announces the Declaration, names Bahá’u’lláh as the Messiah of the age, or reveals who the Friend is. The sources do not settle these points.
  • That the wine is literal. Lewis reads it as the Sufi image of intoxication with the divine, and the Writings use the Cupbearer for the source of spiritual life.
  • A firm date or place beyond “Kurdistan, mid-1850s.”

The Universal House of Justice wrote in 1968 that it had found nothing forbidding the use of the Names of the Manifestations or of the Central Figures in lyrics, “However, we feel that when they are used they should be used with reverence and respect, both in the manner in which they are incorporated in the lyrics and in the manner of presentation.” (Universal House of Justice, letter of 14 March 1968, in Lights of Guidance.) The word “Bahá” in line 8 should be treated with that care.
