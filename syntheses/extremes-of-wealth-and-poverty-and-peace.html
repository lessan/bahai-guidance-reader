The Inordinate Disparity: Wealth, Poverty and the Brink of War in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

In October 1985 the Universal House of Justice addressed The Promise of World Peace “To the Peoples of the World.” In Part II, after reviewing the declarations and conventions adopted by the United Nations, it singles out a few issues which, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven. The second, after racism, is the gap between rich and poor:

“The inordinate disparity between rich and poor, a source of acute suffering, keeps the world in a state of instability, virtually on the brink of war. Few societies have dealt effectively with this situation. The solution calls for the combined application of spiritual, moral and practical approaches. A fresh look at the problem is required, entailing consultation with experts from a wide spectrum of disciplines, devoid of economic and ideological polemics, and involving the people directly affected in the decisions that must urgently be made. It is an issue that is bound up not only with the necessity for eliminating extremes of wealth and poverty but also with those spiritual verities the understanding of which can produce a new universal attitude. Fostering such an attitude is itself a major part of the solution.” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, October 1985, Part II

This article asks one question of that paragraph: why does a statement on peace treat the distribution of wealth as a cause of war? It follows the answer through the statement itself, then back through the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, the talks and Tablets of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, the letters of Shoghi Effendi, and the later messages of the House of Justice.

A companion article, The Extremes of Wealth and Poverty: What the Writings Require, and What They Leave Open, covers the economic teaching in full: Ḥuqúqu’lláh, inheritance, the obligation to work, profit-sharing, the village storehouse, the Guardian’s refusal to name an economic system, and what the 1 March 2017 letter asks of individual believers. That article mentions the peace statement in one paragraph. This one is built around it. Where the two overlap, this article quotes only what bears on instability and war, and points to the other for the rest.

The paragraph, sentence by sentence

The paragraph has six sentences, and each does a separate job.

“The inordinate disparity between rich and poor, a source of acute suffering, keeps the world in a state of instability, virtually on the brink of war.” This is the diagnosis, and it has two parts. The disparity causes suffering, which is a moral fact about the people who suffer. It also keeps the world unstable, which is a fact about everyone. The verb is “keeps”: the disparity is described as a standing condition that holds the world near war, not a single event that might set one off. The qualifier “virtually” marks the claim as a description of proximity, not a prediction of a particular war.

“Few societies have dealt effectively with this situation.” The statement does not exempt any economic model. In Part I it had already said that the materialist ideologies “of the east or the west, whether of capitalism or socialism,” must “give account of the moral stewardship they have presumed to exercise.”

“The solution calls for the combined application of spiritual, moral and practical approaches.” All three are required together. A purely technical programme does not meet the standard, and neither does exhortation alone.

“A fresh look at the problem is required, entailing consultation with experts from a wide spectrum of disciplines, devoid of economic and ideological polemics, and involving the people directly affected in the decisions that must urgently be made.” This is a method, and it has three conditions: many disciplines, no polemics, and participation by the poor themselves. The third condition connects to the statement’s account of why the world is paralysed, discussed below.

“It is an issue that is bound up not only with the necessity for eliminating extremes of wealth and poverty but also with those spiritual verities the understanding of which can produce a new universal attitude.” Two things are bound together: a material goal (eliminating the extremes) and a change in understanding (the “spiritual verities”). The phrase “extremes of wealth and poverty” is the formula Shoghi Effendi used in God Passes By for one of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s principles; the companion article traces its provenance.

“Fostering such an attitude is itself a major part of the solution.” The last sentence places the change of attitude inside the solution, not before it as a precondition or after it as a result.

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The paragraph is short because the rest of the statement carries its argument. Read in place, it is the economic case of an argument that runs through all four parts.

Part I: the wealth of the Pharaohs

Part I sets out the statement’s account of why humanity is in conflict. Its central claim is that the belief that human beings are “incorrigibly selfish and aggressive” is false, and that this belief, once accepted, paralyses the will to build peace. It locates the source of that belief in materialism:

“Most particularly, it is in the glorification of material pursuits, at once the progenitor and common feature of all such ideologies, that we find the roots which nourish the falsehood that human beings are incorrigibly selfish and aggressive.” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, Part I

Part I also contains the statement’s sharpest description of the economic disparity. Some of the ideologies that replaced religion, it says, have chosen “to callously abandon starving millions to the operations of a market system that all too clearly is aggravating the plight of the majority of mankind, while enabling small sections to live in a condition of affluence scarcely dreamed of by our forebears.” It then asks:

“Why is the vast majority of the world’s peoples sinking ever deeper into hunger and wretchedness when wealth on a scale undreamed of by the Pharaohs, the Caesars, or even the imperialist powers of the nineteenth century is at the disposal of the present arbiters of human affairs?” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, Part I

It names the inward damage as well: “the spiritual damage reflected in the apathy that has gripped the mass of the peoples of all nations and by the extinction of hope in the hearts of deprived and anguished millions.”

So when Part II says the disparity “keeps the world in a state of instability,” the reader has already been told what the disparity rests on (the glorification of material pursuits), what it looks like (starving millions beside affluence beyond the Pharaohs’), and what it does to people (it extinguishes hope).

Part II: root causes and the paralysis of will

Part II opens by saying that banning particular weapons “will not remove the root causes of war.” Its next sentence lists economic weapons alongside military ones:

“Peoples are ingenious enough to invent yet other forms of warfare, and to use food, raw materials, finance, industrial power, ideology, and terrorism to subvert one another in an endless quest for supremacy and dominion.” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, Part II

Food, raw materials, finance and industrial power are economic goods. The statement treats them as instruments of war when the motive is “supremacy and dominion.” This is the first link between the economic and the military in Part II, and it comes before the seven issues are named.

The statement then names the obstacle to peace: “a paralysis of will.” It gives two roots. The first is the conviction that humanity is inevitably quarrelsome, which makes nations unwilling to subordinate national self-interest to world order. The second bears directly on the poor:

“It is also traceable to the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order in which they can live in peace, harmony and prosperity with all humanity.” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, Part II

This sentence explains why the disparity paragraph insists on “involving the people directly affected in the decisions.” The people who most need a new order are the people least able to say so. Their silence is part of the paralysis. A method that brings them into the decisions addresses one of the two named roots of the world’s inability to act.

After the seven issues, Part II closes with two points that apply to all of them:

“Two points bear emphasizing in all these issues. One is that the abolition of war is not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols; it is a complex task requiring a new level of commitment to resolving issues not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace. Based on political agreements alone, the idea of collective security is a chimera. The other point is that the primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism. For, in essence, peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude, and it is chiefly in evoking this attitude that the possibility of enduring solutions can be found.” — Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, Part II

The distribution of wealth is the clearest example of an issue “not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.” The second point repeats the disparity paragraph’s last sentence at a general level: the attitude is where enduring solutions come from. The next paragraph says what spiritual principle does that practical knowledge alone does not: it “induces an attitude, a dynamic, a will, an aspiration, which facilitate the discovery and implementation of practical measures.” The “new universal attitude” in the disparity paragraph is one application of that claim.

Part III: class as a prejudice, and the economic order of a united world

Part III names the oneness of humankind as the foundation of world order, and lists the prejudices its recognition requires abandoning: “race, class, color, creed, nation, sex, degree of material civilization, everything which enables people to consider themselves superior to others.” Two of those are economic. Class prejudice and prejudice based on “degree of material civilization” are the attitudes that let a rich person or a rich nation regard the poor as a different kind of people.

Part III then quotes Shoghi Effendi’s outline of a world commonwealth, which begins its list of features with economics:

“… A world community in which all economic barriers will have been permanently demolished and the interdependence of Capital and Labor definitely recognized; in which the clamor of religious fanaticism and strife will have been forever stilled; in which the flame of racial animosity will have been finally extinguished …” — Shoghi Effendi, quoted in The Promise of World Peace, Part III

Economic barriers stand in that sentence beside religious strife and racial animosity, the other causes of war the statement addresses.

Part IV: beyond the armistice

Part IV says that a peace forced by fear is not the goal. “Beyond the initial armistice forced upon the world by the fear of nuclear holocaust, beyond the political peace reluctantly entered into by suspicious rival nations,” lies “the unification of all the peoples of the world in one universal family.” It offers the Bahá’í community, “drawn from many nations, cultures, classes and creeds,” as “a model for study.” The word “classes” is in that list. The claim is that people of different economic standing can belong to one community and conduct its affairs by consultation.

Read whole, the statement gives the disparity three roles in the argument for peace: it is a product of materialism, it supplies weapons and motives for conflict, and it silences the people whose voice is needed to break the paralysis of will.

One word across a century: “inordinate”

The adjective the House of Justice chose has a history in the English texts.

In The Secret of Divine Civilization, written in 1875, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá says that wealth “is most commendable, provided the entire population is wealthy. If, however, a few have inordinate riches while the rest are impoverished, and no fruit or benefit accrues from that wealth, then it is only a liability to its possessor.”

In “The Unfoldment of World Civilization,” dated 11 March 1936, Shoghi Effendi describes the world that unity will produce: “economic barriers and restrictions will be completely abolished, and the inordinate distinction between classes will be obliterated. Destitution on the one hand, and gross accumulation of ownership on the other, will disappear.”

In 1985 the House of Justice writes of “the inordinate disparity between rich and poor.” “Inordinate” means beyond due measure. In each of the three texts the word does the same work. None of them objects to difference in wealth. Each objects to a difference that has lost all proportion. The doctrine of moderation that the companion article sets out is contained in that single adjective.

How the texts connect the disparity to instability and war

The statement asserts the connection in one sentence. The earlier texts explain it. Six connections recur.

1. One body: distress in one part reaches the whole

One of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s fullest treatments of the economic question in North America was an address to socialists and labour leaders in Coronation Hall, Montreal, on 3 September 1912. He began from the body:

“Hence, if a member of the body politic becomes afflicted, in reality, from the standpoint of sympathetic connection, all will share that affliction since this [one afflicted] is a member of the group of members, a part of the whole. Is it possible for one member or part to be in distress and the other members to be at ease? It is impossible!” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 3 September 1912

He then described the human family as a household in which “some members are clothed in most costly garments and some families are in need of food and shelter,” and gave his verdict in four words: “This household is not well arranged.”

He told a story to make the point to the powerful. A Persian king, feasting in his palace, declares that his treasury is full and his army cared for. A poor man at the gate answers him: “When we are comfortable, then you will be comfortable; when we are in misery, how can you, as a king, be in happiness?”

Shoghi Effendi stated the same principle as a fact of the modern world in The Promised Day Is Come (28 March 1941), written during the Second World War:

“The interdependence of the peoples and nations of the earth, whatever the leaders of the divisive forces of the world may say or do, is already an accomplished fact. Its unity in the economic sphere is now understood and recognized. The welfare of the part means the welfare of the whole, and the distress of the part brings distress to the whole.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come

The House of Justice repeated it in its letter of 1 March 2017: “The welfare of any segment of humanity is inextricably bound up with the welfare of the whole.”

This is the basic reason the disparity is a peace issue. If humanity is one body, then extreme poverty in one part is a wound in the whole, and the rich part cannot remain secure while it lasts.

2. Disparity left unaddressed turns to force

The second connection is the one ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá stated most directly. Where the gap is not closed by law and by willing generosity, the poor will seek to close it by force, and force produces disorder.

In the same Montreal address he described the economic solution as one under which “no difference or dissension will occur. No sedition or contention will take place.” Describing the village storehouse, he said its result would be that “in the village no one will remain in need or in want,” and added: “Yet no schism will assail the general order of the body politic.” Near the end he warned what would follow if governments did not legislate a fair settlement between capital and labour:

“Otherwise, the labor problem will lead to much destruction, especially in Europe. Terrible things will take place.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 3 September 1912

He illustrated it with a story of Turkish soldiers who struck for higher pay, received it, struck again, and finally “killed the king, saying: ‘Why didst thou not increase the income so that we might have received more?’”

In Some Answered Questions, chapter 78, answering a question about strikes, he describes the concentration of wealth and then its effect on peace:

“Thus you can observe, on the one hand, a single person who has amassed a fortune, made an entire country his personal colony, acquired immense wealth, and secured an unceasing flow of gains and profits, and, on the other, a hundred thousand helpless souls—weak, powerless, and wanting even a mouthful of bread. There is neither equality here nor benevolence. Observe how, as a result, general peace and happiness have become so wanting, and the welfare of humanity so undermined, that the lives of a vast multitude have been rendered fruitless!” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 78

The same chapter calls strikes a problem “which entirely disrupt the country,” and holds both sides responsible: “the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners” and “the gratuitous demands, the greed, and the intransigence of the workers.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá did not endorse the force he predicted. In a letter to an American philanthropist he wrote that the rich should share “of their own free will, and not because the poor have gained this end by force. For the harvest of force is turmoil and the ruin of the social order. On the other hand voluntary sharing, the freely chosen expending of one’s substance, leadeth to society’s comfort and peace” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, no. 79). A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 30 June 1937 repeated that labour problems “cannot assuredly be solved, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá tells us, through the sheer force of physical violence,” and that “the conflict between labour and capital can best be solved through the peaceful and constructive methods of co-operation and of consultation” (Social Action compilation).

The position is consistent. The disparity is a cause of upheaval. Upheaval is not a remedy for it. Both the hoarding and the seizing are forms of the same disorder.

3. Concentrated wealth as an instrument and motive of conquest

The third connection runs from wealth to war between nations. In a talk in New York on 30 May 1912, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá named the belief that conquest brings prosperity as one of the forces opposing peace:

“Governments, for instance, consider militarism as the step to human progress, that division among men and nations is the cause of patriotism and honor, that if one nation attack and conquer another, gaining wealth, territory and glory thereby, this warfare and conquest, this bloodshed and cruelty are the cause of that victorious nation’s advancement and prosperity. This is an utter mistake.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 30 May 1912

The image in Some Answered Questions of one person who has “made an entire country his personal colony” describes the same thing at the scale of a private fortune. In 1936 Shoghi Effendi described the future world in which “the enormous energy dissipated and wasted on war, whether economic or political, will be consecrated” to invention, health and research (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, “The Unfoldment of World Civilization”). He counted economic war as a kind of war. The 1985 statement’s list, “food, raw materials, finance, industrial power,” names the weapons of that kind.

4. War impoverishes the poor: the reverse flow

The texts also describe the causation running the other way. Preparation for war takes from the poor to pay for armies, and so widens the gap that destabilises the world.

Bahá’u’lláh addressed the kings directly. In the Súriy‑i‑Mulúk:

“Compose your differences, and reduce your armaments, that the burden of your expenditures may be lightened, and that your minds and hearts may be tranquillized. … We have learned that you are increasing your outlay every year, and are laying the burden thereof on your subjects. This, verily, is more than they can bear, and is a grievous injustice.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXVIII

In the same Tablet: “Know ye that the poor are the trust of God in your midst. Watch that ye betray not His trust, that ye deal not unjustly with them and that ye walk not in the ways of the treacherous.”

And in the Tablet to Queen Victoria, the counsel to the rulers names the poor and peace in the same sentence:

“O ye rulers of the earth! Wherefore have ye clouded the radiance of the Sun, and caused it to cease from shining? Hearken unto the counsel given you by the Pen of the Most High, that haply both ye and the poor may attain unto tranquillity and peace. We beseech God to assist the kings of the earth to establish peace on earth.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CXIX; The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, Tablet to Queen Victoria

The passage continues: “O kings of the earth! We see you increasing every year your expenditures, and laying the burden thereof on your subjects. This, verily, is wholly and grossly unjust. … Do not rob them to rear palaces for yourselves; nay rather choose for them that which ye choose for yourselves. … Your people are your treasures.”

Bahá’u’lláh’s sentence places the rulers and the poor in one clause, “both ye and the poor,” and makes their tranquillity a single outcome. The Research Department placed this passage near the front of its compilation Peace (August 1985), prepared in the same year as the statement.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá developed the point in The Secret of Divine Civilization (1875). He described the ruler who claims to seek peace while arming: “Peace is the pretext, and night and day they are all straining every nerve to pile up more weapons of war, and to pay for this their wretched people must sacrifice most of whatever they are able to earn by their sweat and toil.” Under a binding international covenant, he wrote, “the entire population would, first of all, be relieved of the crushing burden of expenditure currently imposed for military purposes.”

On 5 September 1912 in Montreal, two days after the labour address, he spoke at St. James Methodist Church:

“The greatest catastrophe in the world of humanity today is war. Europe is a storehouse of explosives awaiting a spark. All the European nations are on edge, and a single flame will set on fire the whole of that continent. … Armies and navies devour the substance and possessions of the people; the toiling poor, the innocent and helpless are forced by taxation to provide munitions and armament for governments bent upon conquest of territory and defense against powerful rival nations.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 5 September 1912

The First World War began less than two years later. In a later Tablet he summarised the economics of war in one clause: “war is nothing but the consumption of men and of wealth” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, no. 223).

The two directions together describe a cycle. Disparity produces grievance and the pursuit of dominance; the pursuit of dominance produces armaments; armaments are paid for by the poor; and the poor grow poorer. The 1985 statement’s word “keeps” fits a cycle of this kind.

5. Hopelessness and silence

The fifth connection is inward, and it is the one the 1985 statement adds most clearly to the earlier texts. Part I speaks of “the extinction of hope in the hearts of deprived and anguished millions.” Part II traces the paralysis of will partly to “the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order.” Extreme poverty does harm beyond the body. It removes from public life the voices of the people who have the most reason to want peace and justice, and so it weakens the will of humanity as a whole to change course.

The Hidden Words address the rich on this point: “O CHILDREN OF DUST! Tell the rich of the midnight sighing of the poor, lest heedlessness lead them into the path of destruction, and deprive them of the Tree of Wealth” (Persian, no. 49). The rich do not hear the poor, and that failure to hear is named as the road to destruction. The passage is addressed to the soul of the rich person. It is not a political forecast, but it describes in individual terms the same failure the statement describes for the world.

6. The root beneath the root: the struggle for existence

Every connection above rests on a single cause that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá named many times. In his first Tablet to the Central Organization for a Durable Peace at The Hague (17 December 1919), written after the First World War, he set war and peace side by side in a series of contrasts. The last one is:

“… war is the struggle for existence while peace is mutual aid and cooperation among the peoples of the world and the cause of the good pleasure of the True One in the heavenly realm.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Tablets to The Hague, 17 December 1919

Later in the same Tablet: “This matter of the struggle for existence is the fountain-head of all calamities and is the supreme affliction.” And among the teachings he lists for The Hague is that “religious, racial, political, economic and patriotic prejudices destroy the edifice of humanity.” On board the Celtic as he left New York on 5 December 1912, he named the obstacles to human happiness: “racial or religious prejudice, the competitive struggle for existence and inhumanity toward each other.”

The struggle for existence is the law of the animal world, and the extremes of wealth and poverty are what it produces when humans live by it. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá makes the comparison in Some Answered Questions: “This disparity is confined to the human race.” Among animals “a certain kind of justice and equality prevails,” and they “live in perfect peace and joy.” In Montreal he said: “If man were to care for himself only he would be nothing but an animal, for only the animals are thus egoistic.”

This is where the 1985 statement’s argument and ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s meet. The statement says the false belief that human beings are “incorrigibly selfish and aggressive” is rooted in “the glorification of material pursuits.” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá says the struggle for existence belongs to nature, and that human beings are created to rise above it. Both say the same thing: the economic disparity and war come from one mistaken idea of what a human being is. That is why the paragraph says the solution requires “spiritual verities” that “produce a new universal attitude.” Changing the attitude changes the idea that produces both the disparity and the wars.

What the texts promise when the disparity is addressed

If disparity holds the world near war, then addressing it should bring stability. The texts say this in plain terms.

In Philadelphia on 9 June 1912, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said of Bahá’u’lláh’s economic teaching: “This readjustment of the social economy is of the greatest importance inasmuch as it ensures the stability of the world of humanity; and until it is effected, happiness and prosperity are impossible.” In Pittsburgh on 7 May 1912 he called the inequality “one of the deep and vital problems of human society,” named the remedy as “legislative readjustment of conditions” together with willing generosity, and concluded: “The composure of the world will be assured by the establishment of this principle in the religious life of mankind.” In Jersey City on 19 May 1912 he described the end state: “There will be an equilibrium of interests, and a condition will be established which will make both rich and poor comfortable and content.”

The words in these talks are “stability,” “composure,” “equilibrium.” They are the opposites of the 1985 statement’s “instability.” Read together, the talks and the statement describe one principle from its two sides.

Bahá’u’lláh ties the whole question to justice, and justice to world order. In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf: “No light can compare with the light of justice. The establishment of order in the world and the tranquillity of the nations depend upon it.” The House of Justice quoted those words in its 1 March 2017 letter on economic life. In the Words of Paradise He states the purpose of justice: “The light of men is Justice. Quench it not with the contrary winds of oppression and tyranny. The purpose of justice is the appearance of unity among men” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh).

Not one issue in isolation

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s first Tablet to The Hague explains why the House of Justice addressed seven issues instead of one. Universal peace, he wrote, cannot be secured by itself:

“These manifold principles, which constitute the greatest basis for the felicity of mankind and are of the bounties of the Merciful, must be added to the matter of universal peace and combined with it, so that results may accrue. Otherwise the realization of universal peace by itself in the world of mankind is difficult.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Tablets to The Hague, 17 December 1919

Among the parties whose aims he says the teachings fulfil is “the party of ‘equality’ which seeks the solution of the economic problems: until now all proposed solutions have proved impracticable except the economic proposals in the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh which are practicable and cause no distress to society.” The economic principle is one of the principles that must be “combined” with peace. The 1985 statement’s “Two points” paragraph makes the same argument in modern terms: peace requires “resolving issues not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.”

The economic question nested in the world order

Shoghi Effendi placed the economic teaching inside the larger question of world unity. A letter written on his behalf on 27 January 1932 to Keith Ransom‑Kehler, then teaching in Australia, said that although the Faith “has much on the economic life of society, such as progressive income tax, a high death duty & consultation in industry between capital & labour; yet what it has on the international question is far more important and interesting, especially in the light of present world problems” (Messages to the Antipodes). This does not lower the importance of the economic teaching. It says where it belongs: the disparity is finally solved within a united world, and the united world requires the disparity to be solved. In 1936 he described a world commonwealth in which “the economic resources of the world will be organized,” and “the distribution of its products will be equitably regulated,” as part of the same order that would end war (“The Unfoldment of World Civilization”).

A letter written on his behalf on 26 December 1935 explains what is meant by calling the economic solution “divine in nature”: “religion alone can, in the last resort, bring in man’s nature such a fundamental change as to enable him to adjust the economic relationships of society. It is only in this way that man can control the economic forces that threaten to disrupt the foundations of his existence, and thus assert his mastery over the forces of nature.” The phrase “economic forces that threaten to disrupt the foundations of his existence” is the Guardian’s version of “the brink of war.” The remedy he names is the same as the 1985 statement’s: a change in human nature that only religion can bring about, which the statement calls a “new universal attitude.”

In 1931, in “The Goal of a New World Order,” he had asked whether “the fundamental cause of this world unrest” was “the failure of those into whose hands the immediate destinies of peoples and nations have been committed, to adjust their system of economic and political institutions to the imperative needs of a rapidly evolving age.” In 1936 he described humanity after the Great Depression and before the Second World War as “a vast, an enfeebled, and moribund organism, which is being torn politically and strangulated economically by forces it has ceased to either control or comprehend,” and warned that “a yawning gulf threatens to involve in one common disaster both the satisfied and dissatisfied nations, democracies and dictatorships, capitalists and wage-earners.” The same decade saw the economic collapse and the approach of war, and the Guardian wrote of them as one crisis.

After 1985: the House of Justice returns to the theme

The statement was presented to the Secretary‑General of the United Nations by Amatu’l‑Bahá Rúḥíyyih Khánum and delivered to heads of state; the Riḍván 1987 message reported that “more than a million copies, in some seventy languages” had been distributed. In later messages the House of Justice repeated the link between the disparity and instability, and the language grew more specific.

The Prosperity of Humankind, a statement of the Bahá’í International Community (1995), observes early in its argument that optimistic forecasts about development “have vanished into the ever-widening abyss that separates the living standards of a small and relatively diminishing minority of the world’s inhabitants from the poverty experienced by the vast majority of the globe’s population.”

Century of Light (2001), prepared under the supervision of the House of Justice, records that the early Western believers took up “the abolition of extremes of wealth and poverty that were undermining the unity of society.” Describing the close of the twentieth century, it notes the riots at meetings of the World Trade Organization, the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, and the protests against “gross disparities in the distribution of benefits and opportunities,” with warnings that “the consequences will be catastrophic in social and political, as well as in economic and environmental, terms.”

The letter of 1 March 2017 uses the statement’s own word:

“Unconscionable quantities of wealth are being amassed, and the instability this creates is made worse by how income and opportunity are spread so unevenly both between nations and within nations. But it need not be so.” — Universal House of Justice, 1 March 2017, to the Bahá’ís of the World (Messages 2001–2022)

The letter goes on: “The stresses emerging out of the long-term process of transition from a divided world to a united one are being felt within international relations as much as in the deepening fractures that affect societies large and small,” and “the world is in desperate need of a shared ethic, a sure framework for addressing the crises that gather like storm clouds.”

The message of 18 January 2019, written a hundred years after the opening of the Paris Peace Conference, reviewed the century’s attempts at peace and then the setbacks of the present one. It gives the clearest restatement of the 1985 diagnosis:

“Even as global poverty of the most extreme form has decreased, political and economic systems have enabled the enrichment of small coteries with grossly exorbitant wealth—a condition that fuels fundamental instability in world affairs.” — Universal House of Justice, 18 January 2019, to the Bahá’ís of the World

Two points in that sentence matter. It acknowledges that extreme poverty had fallen, so the 2019 diagnosis is about the gap and the concentration, not only about destitution. And it keeps the 1985 claim: the concentration “fuels fundamental instability.” The same message names the work Bahá’ís are doing in response: “They are learning how to nurture communities that embody those prerequisites of peace we identified in 1985,” including “the stirrings of social action” in which “can be seen their desire to remedy the numerous ills afflicting humanity.”

In September 2026 the Bahá’í International Community published a perspective by Bani Dugal, its Principal Representative to the United Nations from 2001 to 2025, who chose this issue to illustrate the 1985 statement. She described the connections between extremes of wealth and poverty and peace as “layered”: “the violence of poverty itself,” “the structural violence that allows poverty to exist and be perpetuated,” the way unmet needs prevent people from “contributing their full share to the work of building the type of safe and peaceful communities in which we all wish to live,” and, at the other end, “the energy required to accumulate and maintain vast wealth,” which “similarly acts as a formidable barrier to people lending their talents and personal capacities to the betterment of their community and society.” This is an individual’s reflection published by the Bahá’í International Community, not guidance of the House of Justice. It is useful because it shows how the Bahá’í representative at the United Nations applies the 1985 paragraph today.

The present field (external sources: description only, current as of October 2026)

The corpus states the principle. It cannot describe the world in 2026. The figures below come from outside sources, describe the field, and are not authority for any Bahá’í teaching. They will age. For current figures on the concentration of wealth itself (the World Inequality Report 2026, Oxfam’s January 2026 report, the UN count of 808 million people in extreme poverty), see the companion article wealth-and-poverty.

Armed conflict. The Uppsala Conflict Data Program reported on 9 June 2026 that it recorded 65 state‑based conflicts in 2025, the highest number since its records begin in 1946. Eight were conflicts between states, up from two in 2023. Thirteen reached the threshold of war (at least 1,000 battle‑related deaths in a year), the most since 1992. About 244,600 people were killed in organised violence in 2025 (Uppsala University press release, 9 June 2026).

Military spending. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute reported on 27 April 2026 that “world military expenditure reached $2887 billion in 2025,” an increase of 2.9 per cent in real terms and “the 11th year of consecutive rises,” with the global military burden rising “to 2.5 per cent, its highest level since 2009” (SIPRI press release; fact sheet). In terms of the texts above, this is the “outlay” that Bahá’u’lláh told the kings they were “increasing … every year,” and the burden ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said was laid on “the toiling poor.”

Research on inequality and violent conflict. The joint United Nations–World Bank study Pathways for Peace: Inclusive Approaches to Preventing Violent Conflict (2018) concluded that “exclusion from access to power, opportunity, and security creates fertile ground for mobilisation to violence,” and identified four areas where exclusion‑based grievances usually appear: access to power, to natural resources, to security and justice, and to basic services (World Bank record; quotations as given in the report’s published summary at comminit.com). Its emphasis on group exclusion and on inclusive decision‑making is close to the 1985 statement’s call for “involving the people directly affected in the decisions.” The match is partial. Pathways is an empirical study of when inequality leads to violence, and it finds the link depends on conditions such as weak institutions and group grievances. The 1985 statement makes a moral and historical claim about the world as a whole. The study supports the statement’s direction. It does not prove the statement, and the statement does not depend on it.

What the sources do not settle

  • “Brink of war” is not a forecast. The statement says the disparity “keeps the world in a state of instability, virtually on the brink of war.” It does not predict a particular war or claim that disparity alone causes any given conflict. It lists the disparity as one of seven issues, and its general claim is that war has several roots that must be addressed together.
  • The talks are records of spoken words. Much of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s most direct language on this topic, including the Montreal warning about “much destruction, especially in Europe,” comes from The Promulgation of Universal Peace and Paris Talks, which were compiled from notes taken by listeners; the Montreal labour address is marked “From Stenographic Notes.” Some Answered Questions (revised translation), the Tablets to The Hague, Selections, and The Secret of Divine Civilization are on firmer ground, and every main point in this article is also supported by at least one of those texts or by Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablets to the kings. One paragraph near the end of the Montreal address refers to “Bolshevistic principles,” a term that sits oddly in a talk given in 1912; this article does not quote it, and the provenance of that paragraph is not settled here.
  • Which direction comes first. The texts describe causation in both directions, from disparity to conflict and from war preparation to impoverishment. They do not say which is primary, and this article does not rank them.
  • The “people directly affected” in practice. The statement does not specify how the poor are to be brought into the decisions. Later guidance on social action, in which “social change is not a project that one group of people carries out for the benefit of another,” shows how the Bahá’í community applies the principle in its own work; the companion article and social-action-guidance cover this. How it would apply to international economic decisions is not described.
  • External figures are from April and June 2026 and will need re‑checking. The Pathways for Peace quotations were taken from a published summary of the report, because the World Bank and UNDP pages could not be retrieved for this article.

For the song

The heart of it, as the statement frames it. The gap between rich and poor is a wound in one body, and a world that lets a few live beyond the Pharaohs’ wealth while millions go hungry is a world kept on the edge of war; healing it needs just laws, the voice of the poor, and a new way of seeing each other.

Concrete images, people, places and moments.

  • The two storehouses of Montreal. In one week in September 1912, in the same city, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá used the word “storehouse” twice in English: on 3 September, a village storehouse that would leave “no one … in need or in want”; on 5 September, Europe as “a storehouse of explosives awaiting a spark.” The First World War began less than two years later. (The pairing is an observation about the English text of two separate talks.)
  • Coronation Hall, Montreal, 3 September 1912: an audience of socialists and labour leaders; the speaker an exile from Persia, released from imprisonment in ‘Akká only after the Young Turk Revolution of 1908.
  • The king and the poor man at the gate: a feast in a full palace, a voice from outside asking, “do you never worry about the poor in your land?”
  • One person who has “made an entire country his personal colony” beside “a hundred thousand helpless souls … wanting even a mouthful of bread” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 78).
  • The shepherd’s flock, the herd of deer, the songbirds: ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s picture of creatures that do not hoard, set against the human disparity “confined to the human race.”
  • The thousand sheep at the well: if nine hundred and ninety‑nine are killed, the last “would go on grazing, not thinking of the others” (Montreal). Caring only for oneself is the animal’s way.
  • Paris: “some are clothed in velvets, furs and fine linen” while “others have insufficient, poor and thin garments with which to protect them from the cold”; “When we see poverty allowed to reach a condition of starvation it is a sure sign that somewhere we shall find tyranny.”
  • Kings and their treasuries: Bahá’u’lláh, an exile and later a prisoner, writing to the kings to “reduce your armaments” because the cost is laid on their subjects; “Do not rob them to rear palaces for yourselves.”
  • The midnight sighing of the poor, unheard by the rich.
  • October 1985: the statement taken by Amatu’l‑Bahá Rúḥíyyih Khánum to the Secretary‑General of the United Nations, then to heads of state; more than a million copies in some seventy languages by Riḍván 1987.
  • The “subjugated masses” who cannot “articulate their desire for a new order”: a song can give them the voice the statement says they lack.

Misreadings a song must avoid.

  • Not a call to level everyone. The texts reject absolute equality. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: “absolute equality is just as untenable” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 78); “Equality is a chimera!” (Paris Talks). The target is the extremes and the “inordinate,” not difference.
  • Not a call to revolt or to take by force. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá warned that disparity would lead to upheaval. He did not endorse the upheaval: “the harvest of force is turmoil and the ruin of the social order.” The change comes “not through warfare, but welfare.” A song that cheers the poor seizing the palace contradicts its own source.
  • Not a song against the rich as people. Wealth “is most commendable, provided the entire population is wealthy.” The poor man at the gate speaks to the king’s conscience; he does not curse him. Bahá’u’lláh’s counsel is for “both ye and the poor.”
  • Not a sanctification of poverty. Poverty is called “miserable, demoralizing, degrading” in Paris. The poor are honoured; their poverty is to be ended.
  • Not anti-capitalist or anti-socialist. The statement indicts “the dogmas of materialism, whether of the east or the west, whether of capitalism or socialism.” It takes no side in that argument.
  • Not charity alone. Generosity is necessary, but the texts also call for laws: “There must be special laws made, dealing with these extremes of riches and of want” (Paris Talks).
  • Not a prediction of doom. “Brink of war” describes a condition that “need not be so” (2017). The statement is titled The Promise of World Peace, and it ends with Bahá’u’lláh’s promise: “These fruitless strifes, these ruinous wars shall pass away, and the ’Most Great Peace’ shall come.”
  • Not a claim that money alone causes war. The disparity is one of seven issues. Beneath all of them the texts place a single root: the belief that humans are bound to the struggle for existence. The song’s turn, if it has one, is from that belief to “mutual aid and cooperation.”
