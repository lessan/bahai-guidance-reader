Mathnavíy-i-Mubárak (The Blessed Mathnaví)

Last updated 17 October 2026

The Mathnavíy-i-Mubárak is a Persian poem by Bahá’u’lláh in rhyming couplets, about 318 lines in the English translations. It is probably the longest poem He wrote. Scholars date most of it to 1863, the year of His journey from Baghdad to Constantinople. No authorised English translation exists. Two complete provisional translations by individual translators are in the corpus (Franklin Lewis, 1999; Kay Lynn Morton, 2024), and a third, by John Hatcher and Amrollah and Ehsanollah Hemmat (2024), is published but not in the corpus. This page is one of the series described in Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poem

Form and language. The poem is in Persian. A mathnaví is a poem of rhyming couplets: each line has its own internal rhyme, and the rhyme changes from line to line. Lewis, in his translator’s introduction, describes the form as one “where the first and second hemistich (_mesrâ’_) of each line rhyme, but the rhyme differs in each successive stich (conforming to the pattern aa bb cc dd ee, etc.)”. He gives the metre as ramal-i musaddas-i mahzúf, the same metre as Rúmí’s Mathnaví.

The title. Lewis renders it “The Blessed Couplets”. He adds that mubárak (“blessed”) is also part of a title of Bahá’u’lláh, “The Blessed Beauty”, so a Persian Bahá’í reader would hear a double meaning: the Blessed Couplets, or the Couplets of the Blessed One. In his reading the title also points to Rúmí’s poem, the Mathnavíy-i ma‘naví (“Spiritual Couplets”).

When and where. The sources agree on the year, and disagree about where the poem was composed.

  • Lewis writes that the poem is “apparently the longest work he composed in verse, and perhaps also one of the last”, and that “the dating of many of his books, tablets, poems and his voluminous correspondence remains rather tentative”.
  • He reports that ‘Abdu’l-Ḥamíd Ishráq-Khávarí placed the poem among the Tablets revealed in Constantinople between 16 August and 12 December 1863, and noted that it ends with a lament for Bahá’u’lláh’s exile from Iraq and His house in Baghdad. He adds that Adib Taherzadeh also placed it in the Constantinople period (Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2, pp. 29–32; the Taherzadeh text itself is not in the corpus, and the Bahaipedia summary gives it as “300 lines”).
  • Lewis then reports that Vahid Rafati has shown the text could not have been composed entirely in Constantinople. Lewis’s own conclusion is that the poem has at least two parts: an earlier part, perhaps the first half, from the Baghdad period, and a later part completed in Constantinople in the autumn of 1863.
  • His evidence is a passage of Bahá’u’lláh’s own Kitáb-i-Badí‘ (written in Adrianople, 1866 or 1867). Lewis reports that it quotes lines 147–152 of the Mathnaví as “a few individual lines” revealed in Iraq, and that it places the closing lines (312–317) after Bahá’u’lláh’s arrival in “the Great City”, the name Bahá’í texts use for Constantinople. The Kitáb-i-Badí‘ is not in the corpus; this is Lewis’s report of it.
  • Many lines allude to Bahá’u’lláh as the fulfilment of the Báb’s promise, so Lewis dates much of the poem after the declaration in the Garden of Riḍván (21 April 1863), and before the Súriy-i-Aṣḥáb, the Tablet in which, Lewis says, Bahá’u’lláh announced His claim to the wider Bábí community in Adrianople, probably in 1864. Many lines in the poem describe Him, or God’s truth, as still veiled.
  • Morton’s catalogue entry on bahai-library.com describes the poem as written “in Constantinople in 1863 following His initial declaration in Baghdad”.

What the poem is addressed to. Lewis describes it as concerned with Bahá’u’lláh’s “disclosure of his station to the Babis and to humanity”. Lewis writes that Bahá’u’lláh completed the poem in Constantinople “partly in response to the Mevlevis’ devotion for Rumi’s great” Mathnaví, and that in the closing lines He “indirectly alludes” to it. The Mevlevi order, founded by Rúmí’s followers, was active in Constantinople. This is Lewis’s inference; the poem does not state a reason.

Relation to Rúmí’s Mathnaví, according to Lewis. These points are scholarship, not statements of the Writings.

  • Both poems use the mathnaví form and the same metre.
  • Rúmí’s poem begins with the reed pipe, and Bahá’u’lláh’s poem ends its argument with it (couplets 290–300 in Lewis’s numbering).
  • Rúmí’s poem is about 25,000 lines and includes long stories; Bahá’u’lláh’s is 318 lines and, Lewis says, “does not digress into stories”.
  • Lewis adds that the Mathnaví of Rúmí was widely read among nineteenth-century Iranians, including Bábís and Bahá’ís, and that Nabíl-i-Zarandí wrote two mathnavís that seem to take their inspiration from Bahá’u’lláh’s.

What the Writings and Shoghi Effendi say. Nothing was found in the corpus. God Passes By does not mention the poem. Lewis states that “Shoghi Effendi did not translate any of Bahá’u’lláh’s versified poems”; Shoghi Effendi did, however, render one passage of the Ode of the Dove in God Passes By (GPB VII.31). Lewis also reports two uses of the text by Bahá’ís in Bahá’u’lláh’s lifetime: Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí quoted about thirty lines in a letter to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (lines 25–41 and 147–157) while asking about the Master’s station, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quoted four lines from “the Mathnaví of the Abhá Beauty” in a letter, with variant readings. These are reports of letters not in the corpus.

Manuscripts and editions. The Persian text in the corpus is the page from Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá vol. 2, pp. 308–320, saved from OceanOfLights.org (632 verse lines). Lewis also cites a facsimile edition of the poem in the calligraphy of Mishkín-Qalam (Langenhain, 1992, with a postscript by Rafati), and a printed text of 1914 (1332 A.H.) edited by Faraj Alláh Zakí al-Kurdí. He notes that handwritten copies differ in a few readings.

The text in English

There is no authorised translation. Every English text is a provisional translation, which means a translation by an individual, not approved by the Universal House of Justice, and not the text of the Writings. The Persian opening

اى حيات العرش خورشيد وداد

The first line addresses the Beloved as “life of the Throne” and “sun of love”, which is how both English translators read it. (Persian original from Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá vol. 2, pp. 308–320, as saved from OceanOfLights.org. Licence: the poem is Bahá’u’lláh’s own text; the web transcription carries that site’s notice, so treat the licence as `unknown` until checked.)

Lewis (1999), short extracts

Lewis’s poem opens:

Life-force of God’s throne,

compassion’s sun!

and, near the end, on the reed pipe and the veil:

Since you have heard the reed pipe’s plaint of love,

The Piper felt a stranger to the world

and so He donned the reed pipe as His veil

(Lewis, 1999, Bahá’í Studies Review 9. Provisional translation by Franklin Lewis. Licence: `permission-needed`.)

Morton (2024), short extracts

Morton’s version rhymes. Her opening and one couplet from the middle:

O The Life of The Throne!

We’re in That Promised Day, and it will never end.

and from the ending:

Your Friend is detained! — Your Beloved Bahá!

(Morton, provisional translation, 2024, saved from bahai-library.com. Licence: `permission-needed`.) Morton’s introduction says she began in 2017 because she noticed that Lewis’s translation “was unrhymed”, and wished to see whether the poem “could be rendered in English in rhyming couplets, like the original”. The saved web text has some damaged characters (for example a lost “fl” ligature in “flowers”), so it should be checked against the source before it is quoted at length.

Themes and images

The summary here follows the translations. Couplet numbers are Morton’s, and Lewis numbers some lines differently (his reed-pipe couplets are 290–300; Morton’s are 291–300).

  1. An address to the Beloved, asking for the veils to be lifted. The poem begins by addressing the “Life of the Throne” and says that if people were not veiled from seeing His face, the poet “would say a word or two”. It asks for the veils to be torn, for the Face to be shown, and for the hearts of the world to be made mad with love. Lewis writes the request as: “Rend through these curtains of a hundred folds”. Morton has: “Burst open this curtain of hundreds of veils in place!”
  2. The atom, the seed and the drop. The small things of creation ask the Beloved for their share of light and rain. Lewis has: “The atoms beg their share of light from You:”.
  3. The spring that is not followed by autumn. A long middle section sets the “Eternal Spring” of this Revelation against the spring of nature. Lewis writes: “No fall will ever overtake such springs”, and “Such spring as this the soul cannot conceive”. Morton has: “This Spring won’t be followed by Fall, ever!” The spring is the Beloved’s face and utterance; Morton’s couplet 58 has “This Utterance is Life Itself — and has no death.” The earthly spring is a time of ardour that wanes; this one, as Lewis puts it, brings “love of God”. Lewis comments in a note that the spring of 1863 was a time of sorrow for Bahá’u’lláh, so the contrast between the two springs may carry that meaning.
  4. Fragrance and Joseph’s coat. The poet asks the breeze to carry the scent of the Beloved’s hair to the world. The poem recalls the scent of Joseph’s coat that restored Jacob’s sight, and the burning bush of Moses, as figures for a revelation that is perceived before it is seen.
  5. The eye and the ear that must be opened. Morton: “Open the other eye — and see The New Friend!”; and “The eye of the mystic sees Eternity’s Mysteries.”
  6. The Day that has not ended. In a short dialogue, a questioner asks a mystic whether he remembers the Day of Alast (the day God asked “Am I not your Lord?”). The older mystic answers, in Morton’s version, “We’re in That Promised Day, and it will never end.” Lewis’s notes discuss variant readings of the lines about the Day of Alast (lines 148–149), including the form in which ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quoted them.
  7. Moses, Noah, Jesus and the Staff. The poem addresses earlier Messengers by name and compares what happened in their times with “This” Revelation, in the pattern “That Staff… and This Staff”. Lewis identifies the Sinai and Burning Bush images with the Divine Lote-Tree.
  8. Union and separation. Near the end the poem tells the reader to leave off both “separation” and “reunion”, because the pair assumes two where the poem teaches there is one relation: God’s light is in the person, and the task is to make it bright. Morton’s couplet 262: “God’s House, O lad, is nothing but the human heart!”
  9. The reed pipe and the Piper. The poem now turns to Rúmí’s image. Lewis’s lines give the sequence: the reader has heard the pipe, should look at who plays it, and learn that the Piper “donned the reed pipe as His veil”. The reader is told to tear off the veil and show “alone the Piper to the world”. Morton has a related couplet on the self: “Man becomes a hollow reed, when ego’s burned away”. (The Persian wordplay on mani, “I-ness”, and ney, “reed”, is explained in the translators’ notes.)
  10. The lament for Baghdad. The poem ends with the poet in captivity writing to the city he has left. He asks whether a messenger will go to Iraq, asks the breeze to carry his words to the exiles of Baghdad, and addresses the city: its Friend is held, “like Husayn”, like Moses among the Egyptians, like Jesus among a hostile people (Lewis: “detained among the Jews”; Morton: “in the midst of the Romans”), like Joseph in the well. Lewis closes with: “Your nightingale’s afflicted in a cage!”

Images that recur through the poem: spring, flowers and the nightingale; hair, face and the Beloved; fire, the Burning Bush and Sinai; wine and the cup; the reed pipe; the Ark; the well.

What is not settled

  • Date and place. The year, 1863, is agreed. Lewis argues for two stages, Baghdad and Constantinople. Rafati is reported to have said it could not all have been written in Constantinople. Morton’s catalogue entry gives Constantinople only. The sources do not date the individual sections.
  • Length. Lewis and Morton both give 318 lines or couplets. The saved Persian page has 632 verse lines, which is 316 couplets. Taherzadeh is reported to have said “300 lines”. The count differs by edition and by how split lines are counted; the corpus does not settle this.
  • Edition. Lewis cites readings from Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá, from the 1914 text, and from the Mishkín-Qalam manuscript. He notes lines where they differ and where he chose a reading (one note concerns whether a word in a line about hearts should be read naqd or naqdh, “to assay” or “to deliver”, and he chooses “to deliver” on the evidence of a family recitation tradition). He says an authoritative decision would require the best manuscripts.
  • Who the lines address. Lewis suggests that one strophe is addressed to the Báb, and notes that a line Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí quoted uses the plural of the word for “branch” (aghṣán), a word Bahá’u’lláh later used in the singular of His sons. These are Lewis’s interpretations.
  • A variant in the last lines. The Persian text in the corpus reads sibṭiyán (descendants of Jacob’s tribes) in the line about “the Spirit of God”. Lewis translates “the Jews”; Morton translates “the Romans”. Lewis reports that the Kitáb-i-Badí‘ reads a different word, and takes the difference to be a revision by the author.
  • Translations compared. The three provisional translations differ in line numbering, in form, and in some readings. Lewis says in his introduction that he treats the poem as “a devotional text, intended for recitation like a prayer”. He asks readers to send him corrections. No translation here has the approval of the Universal House of Justice.
  • Hatcher and Hemmat. The text of their translation was not available in the corpus, so this page does not compare it with the others.

Concrete images. A spring that no autumn follows; a reed pipe and the one who plays it; a candle in a lamp-glass in a whirlwind; a bird in a cage; a wind carrying a scent from a loved one’s hair; a seed with its mouth turned up toward the sky; an atom asking for light; a city left behind and a messenger who cannot be sent.

A possible arc. Longing and a request to see the face (couplets 1–20); the spring that does not end (36–77); the call to open the eye and ear and live in the present Day (143–160); the candle in danger and the fire that lit it (167–185); the reed pipe and the Piper, with the self burned away (291–300); the lament for the city (306–318). A song could follow the poem’s turn from asking, to describing the new spring, to a closing sorrow for the friends left behind.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not call any of the English lines the translation of the poem authorised by the Bahá’í institutions, or quote them as the words of Bahá’u’lláh without saying they are a provisional translation by a named translator.
  • It must not state that the poem was written entirely in Constantinople, or give an exact date for any section.
  • It must not say Shoghi Effendi or the Universal House of Justice has commented on it; nothing in the corpus shows that.
  • The Kitáb-i-Badí‘, the letters of Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, and Taherzadeh’s account are known here only through Lewis and the Bahaipedia summary, and should be cited that way.
  • The poem’s reference to the self “burned” and the figure of the Beloved are mystical images of love for God, in the Persian tradition; a lyric should keep that sense and not turn the Beloved into a human lover.
