Delegation and the Limits of Authority in the Bahá’í Administrative Order

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í administrative order does not answer the delegation question with a map. It answers it with a rule of construction, and the rule turns on a distinction most readers miss on first encounter: authority and the power to act are two different things, distributed by opposite presumptions.

Authority — the right to decide a matter and bind others by the decision — is conferred, never assumed. It sits with elected bodies inside stated spheres, and everything else that decides does so, in the House of Justice’s own formulation, “to the extent that it is so delegated.” The presumption there runs upward and narrow: a body has only what has been given to it in writing, and what has not been given is retained.

The power to act runs the other way. “The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally is divinely conferred on elected institutions,” states The Institution of the Counsellors. “The power to act, however, resides primarily in the entire body of the believers.” The presumption there runs downward and wide: the individual and the community act on their own initiative unless the matter falls into a small reserved class — and Bahá’u’lláh’s command to teach, the Universal House of Justice observes, “confers an inescapable responsibility which cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution of the Cause.”

My position on the central question: almost every dispute that presents itself as a jurisdiction problem in Bahá’í community life is not one. Ask “am I allowed to do this?” and the guidance will usually answer yes, and you were supposed to have started already. The genuinely reserved categories are few, and they share a feature: they are the matters where one actor’s decision binds, commits, or represents somebody else — the community’s funds, its legal person, its public voice, another believer’s standing, or a precedent that would bend the direction of the whole. Everything outside that ring is either yours to do or yours to consult about — and consultation, not permission, is the instrument.

The second position, which follows from the first: the line between what is reserved and what is released moves, it moves downward, and it moves as a function of capacity rather than of principle. Decentralisation is not a Bahá’í value. Shoghi Effendi named both failures in a single sentence in 1927 — “the evils of over-centralization” on one side, “the perils of utter decentralization” on the other — and the Universal House of Justice, formalising Regional Bahá’í Councils seventy years later, called the balance between them “one of the subtle qualities of the Bahá’í Administrative Order.” Devolution here is not offloading. It increases the burden on the body that devolves, which must maintain “a corresponding increase in [its own] capacity… to keep fully informed.”

And the third, which is the one friends most often need to hear: an enormous territory has been left unlegislated on purpose, and the House of Justice has said so in as many words — “it is neither possible nor desirable for the Universal House of Justice to set forth a set of rules covering every situation.” In that territory nobody decides for you. That is not a gap in the system. It is the system’s most consequential delegation.

Two different things are being handed out

Start with the founding grant, because the shape of everything downstream is visible in it. Bahá’u’lláh establishes the institution in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas and, in the same verse, tells its members what standard governs their exercise of it:

“The Lord hath ordained that in every city a House of Justice be established wherein shall gather counsellors to the number of Bahá… It behooveth them to be the trusted ones of the Merciful among men and to regard themselves as the guardians appointed of God for all that dwell on earth. It is incumbent upon them to take counsel together and to have regard for the interests of the servants of God, for His sake, even as they regard their own interests.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶30

The verse refers to the Local House of Justice — the body now called the Local Spiritual Assembly, elected wherever nine adult believers reside. What it grants is trusteeship, not ownership; the standard for its decisions is other people’s interests treated as one’s own. That is a claim about the character of authority before it is a claim about its extent, and it explains why guidance on delegation, when it turns technical, keeps returning to posture rather than to procedure alone.

The extent is settled elsewhere. In the Eighth Leaf of the Exalted Paradise, Bahá’u’lláh assigns to the supreme body the residual domain — everything not written down: “It is incumbent upon the Trustees of the House of Justice to take counsel together regarding those things which have not outwardly been revealed in the Book, and to enforce that which is agreeable to them. God will verily inspire them with whatsoever He willeth” (Kalimát-i-Firdawsíyyih).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the same allocation in the Will and Testament, in the sentence that has become the shorthand for the whole architecture — “Unto this body all things must be referred. It enacteth all ordinances and regulations that are not to be found in the explicit Holy Text” (Will and Testament). And in a Tablet quoted in the introduction to the Aqdas He gives the design logic, which is a logic about time:

“Those matters of major importance which constitute the foundation of the Law of God are explicitly recorded in the Text, but subsidiary laws are left to the House of Justice. The wisdom of this is that the times never remain the same, for change is a necessary quality and an essential attribute of this world, and of time and place.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, cited in the introduction to The Kitáb-i-Aqdas

Read together, these establish the first delegation principle of the Faith, and it is a principle of reservation: the Text keeps the foundations and hands the rest, deliberately, to a body that can revise. Shoghi Effendi drew out the consequence in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh — the revealed Word preserves “the identity of His Faith”, while the House of Justice’s legislative elasticity “enables it, even as a living organism, to expand and adapt itself to the needs and requirements of an ever-changing society.” Immutability and elasticity are not in tension; they are assigned to different layers on purpose.

Now the second stream. The Institution of the Counsellors, prepared by the Universal House of Justice and issued on 1 January 2001, states both things being distributed in consecutive sentences:

“The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally is divinely conferred on elected institutions. The power to act, however, resides primarily in the entire body of the believers. This power is unlocked at the level of individual initiative and at the level of collective volition.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

The same document, three pages earlier, gives the rule of construction for everything that is not an Assembly:

“The Administrative Order conceived by Bahá’u’lláh accomplishes its divinely ordained purpose through a system of institutions, each with its defined sphere of action… Under its guidance, legislative, executive and judicial authority over the affairs of the Bahá’í community is exercised by Local and National Spiritual Assemblies. This authority is also exercised by Regional Councils, committees and other agencies established by these institutions, to the extent that it is so delegated.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Six words carry the weight: to the extent that it is so delegated. An Area Teaching Committee, an institute board, a Regional Council, a national committee — none holds anything by its own nature. Each holds what an Assembly has handed it, no more, and the handing must be traceable. This is why “who is in charge here?” so often has no satisfying answer in a cluster (who-is-in-charge-in-a-cluster): the agencies are not competing for a throne, they are holding separate delegated briefs, and what is missing is usually the consultation that would have made those briefs mutually legible.

The upward rule: what must be referred, and why the list stays short

The oldest operational statement of the delegation rule in Bahá’í administration is also the sharpest, and it is a hundred and four years old. Writing to the Bahá’ís of the United States and Canada on 5 March 1922, Shoghi Effendi laid down the default:

“…all matters without any exception whatsoever, regarding the interests of the Cause in … [a] locality, individually or collectively, should be referred exclusively to the Spiritual Assembly in that locality, which shall decide upon it, unless it be a matter of national interest, in which case it shall be referred to the National Body.” — Shoghi Effendi, 5 March 1922 (Bahá’í Administration, p. 23)

Note the structure. The presumption sits at the lowest competent level — the locality — and escalation is triggered by a substantive test, “national interest”, not by rank or by caution. A year later, in the letter of 12 March 1923 that called Local Assemblies into being everywhere, he repeated the allocation in the affirmative: “To it all local matters pertaining to the Cause must be directly and immediately referred for full consultation and decision” (The Local Spiritual Assembly). Directly and immediately — not eventually, and not after the National Assembly has been consulted.

The Constitution of the Universal House of Justice (1972) codifies the same shape and adds the ladder that makes it safe. A Local Spiritual Assembly “shall exercise full jurisdiction over all Bahá’í activities and affairs within its locality”; a National Spiritual Assembly “shall have exclusive jurisdiction and authority over all the activities and affairs of the Bahá’í Faith throughout its area.” The by-laws then provide the route upward for anyone who believes a jurisdiction has been exercised badly: any member of a local community may appeal from the Local Assembly to the National, any Bahá’í from the National Assembly to the Universal House of Justice. The procedural detail is the interesting part. An appellant “shall in the first instance make appeal to the Assembly whose decision is questioned” — but if that Assembly “refuses to submit the appeal, or fails to do so within a reasonable time, the appellant may take the case directly to the higher authority.”

That clause matters more than its length suggests. The delegation system does not depend on the good faith of the delegate. A body that will not forward a challenge to its own decision forfeits the right to stand between the appellant and the next tier.

So what, in practice, actually must go up? Reading the guidance as a whole, the reserved matters cluster into five recognisable kinds, and they share a family resemblance.

First, anything that speaks for the Faith to the outside. External affairs and relations with civil authorities are national-level responsibilities; where a Regional Bahá’í Council conducts them regionally it does so, in the 1997 enclosure’s language, “under the general guidelines and policies established by the National Spiritual Assembly… on behalf of the National Assembly.” An individual believer may of course speak about their beliefs; the reservation bites when someone purports to speak as the community.

Second, publication about the Faith. Review is the one gate that operates on individual creative work, and the House of Justice has been at pains to keep it narrow. Its letter of 29 January 2019 tells a National Assembly’s reviewing committee exactly how far its warrant runs:

“The mandate of review is specific. Its purpose is to ensure that works about the Bahá’í Faith by its members represent the teachings and the history of the Cause in an accurate and dignified manner and that their publication is timely. Reviewers must keep assiduously to the task at hand, recalling that their duty is only to determine whether there are misrepresentations of the teachings: the rest is the author’s responsibility. They should avoid entering into the domain of author and publisher by offering conceptual guidance or editorial recommendations.” — The Universal House of Justice, 29 January 2019 (muhj-2001-2022)

This is a delegation instrument written against the delegate’s own expansionary instinct. A committee given a gate will tend to widen it; the letter forecloses that in advance, and adds an appeal path — a difference between author and committee that the National Assembly cannot resolve “may be referred to the Universal House of Justice.” Guidance on Bahá’í scholarship makes the same point from the other side, listing among the causes of avoidable friction the “failure to use the appeal processes of the Cause by scholars” when a reviewing committee errs (Issues Related to the Study of the Bahá’í Faith).

Third, funds, property, and legal personality. Shoghi Effendi placed the Bahá’í Fund “under the exclusive control of the Spiritual Assembly” from its creation on 12 March 1923, with expenditure at the Assembly’s discretion. Regional Councils operating a regional branch of a national fund do so as agents; budgets go to the National Assembly for approval.

Fourth, anything touching another believer’s standing. However much authority a National Assembly devolves upon a Council, the 1997 enclosure states, “the authority to deprive a believer of his or her administrative rights, or to restore them, remains with the National Assembly” — and the “right of direct access to the National Assembly by a Local Spiritual Assembly is preserved.”

Fifth, initiatives that would commit the community’s shared resources or set a direction. The least codified and the most frequently misjudged; it is the subject of the next section.

Above all of it sits a limit on the Assemblies themselves, stated by Shoghi Effendi in a letter of 14 March 1927 to the Spiritual Assembly of Istanbul and quoted by the House of Justice seventy years later. Bahá’u’lláh, he wrote, “ordained that all matters be referred to authorized centers and specified assemblies. Even so, no assembly has been invested with the absolute authority to deal with such general matters as affect the interests of nations. Nay, rather, He has brought all the assemblies together under the shadow of the one House of Justice, one divinely-appointed center.” No tier is sovereign in itself. Every jurisdiction in this system is a jurisdiction within something.

The downward rule: initiative is a duty, not a permission

The mirror of the referral rule is a body of guidance so emphatic that it is difficult to read the Bahá’í order as institutionally cautious at all. The Riḍván 1996 message states the individual’s position in terms that leave institutions no room to absorb it:

“The role of the individual is of unique importance in the work of the Cause… Bahá’u’lláh’s command to each believer to teach His Faith confers an inescapable responsibility which cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution of the Cause. The individual alone can exercise those capacities which include the ability to take initiative, to seize opportunities, to form friendships, to interact personally with others, to build relationships… and to convert into action the decisions made by consultative bodies.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 1996 (muhj-1986-2001)

Cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution. The teaching duty is not delegated downward from an Assembly; it was never the Assembly’s to hold. The same message recalls Shoghi Effendi’s blunter formulation — that without the individual’s support, “at once wholehearted, continuous and generous,” every measure and plan of a National Spiritual Assembly is “foredoomed to failure.”

The 28 December 2010 message to the Counsellors then describes the maturity that the two rules together are meant to produce, in the sentence I would put at the head of any consultation about jurisdiction:

“And so it is that all come to discern with ease those areas of activity in which the individual can best exercise initiative and those which fall to the institutions alone. ‘With heart and soul’, the friends follow the directives of their institutions, so that, as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá explains, ‘things may be properly ordered and well arranged’. This, of course, is not a blind obedience; it is an obedience that marks the emergence of a mature human race which grasps the implications of a system as far-reaching as Bahá’u’lláh’s new World Order.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

The competence being described is discernment, exercised by the individual, about which class a given act falls into. The system does not propose to answer that question case by case from above; it proposes to raise people who can answer it themselves. That is why so much of the practical guidance on delegation is addressed to formation rather than to rules.

The same message turns immediately to the institutional side and imposes the reciprocal discipline, quoting Shoghi Effendi: those who serve on institutions understand that “their function is not to dictate, but to consult, and consult not only among themselves, but as much as possible with the friends whom they represent,” and are never “led to suppose that they are the central ornaments of the body of the Cause.” Authority’s proper posture follows: “institutions invested with authority see themselves as instruments for nurturing human potential, ensuring its unfoldment along avenues productive and meritorious.” An institution whose reflex is to ask “did they clear this with us?” has, on this account, misunderstood what it is for — a point the Riḍván 2012 message compresses into an aphorism: “a Bahá’í institution… aims not to control but to nurture and encourage.”

Nor is individual initiative here a licence for improvisation. Turning Point’s analysis of the Four and Five Year Plans is careful about what kind of initiative is being praised: “It is not the kind of unrestricted individualism that, if left free to reign in a community, results in alienation and, eventually, stagnation.” What distinguishes it is that it operates inside a shared framework — and the reason given is efficacy rather than obedience: “even though individuals may strive to be guided in their actions by their personal understanding of the Divine Texts, and much can be accomplished thereby, such actions, untempered by the overall direction provided by authorized institutions, are incapable of attaining the thrust necessary for the unencumbered advancement of civilization.” Riḍván 2008 names the resulting quality with an adjective worth keeping: “how well disciplined is this individual initiative… Far from restricting them, this framework enables them to seize opportunities, to build relationships, and to translate into reality a vision of systematic growth.”

1927: the two opposite errors, named in one paragraph

The pivot of the whole subject is a letter Shoghi Effendi wrote on 18 October 1927 to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States and Canada, which had just decided to push as much routine work as possible out to its committees. He did not simply approve or disapprove. He named the two failure modes that bracket the correct answer, and insisted the Assembly hold the balance between them:

“They should, within the limits imposed upon them by present-day circumstances, endeavor to maintain the balance in such a manner that the evils of over-centralization which clog, confuse and in the long run depreciate the value of the Bahá’í services rendered shall on one hand be entirely avoided, and on the other the perils of utter decentralization with the consequent lapse of governing authority from the hands of the national representatives of the believers definitely averted.” — Shoghi Effendi, 18 October 1927 (Bahá’í Administration, pp. 143–144)

The paragraph then gives the two errors their concrete form. Over-centralisation looks like this: “The absorption of the petty details of Bahá’í administration by the personnel of the National Spiritual Assembly is manifestly injurious to efficiency and an expert discharge of Bahá’í duties.” Under-centralisation looks like this: “the granting of undue discretion to bodies that should be regarded in no other light than that of expert advisers and executive assistants would jeopardize the very vital and pervading powers that are the sacred prerogatives of bodies that in time will evolve into Bahá’í National Houses of Justice.”

That phrase — committees as “expert advisers and executive assistants” — has done a century of work in Bahá’í administrative reasoning, and the House of Justice would cite it seventy years later as the reason a new kind of body was needed rather than a new layer of committees. A committee’s role, the Guardian wrote in the same letter, is “to make thorough and expert study of the issue entrusted to their charge, advise by their reports, and assist in the execution of the decisions which in vital matters are to be exclusively and directly rendered by the National Assembly.” Advise; assist in execution. Not decide.

What makes the letter decisive is that its other main section imposes the matching restraint on individuals — same letter, same people, same principle. Under the heading “Concentration of Resources”, Shoghi Effendi cautions the American believers that “by an undue multiplication of their activities, and the consequent dissipation of their forces,” they may “defeat the very purpose which animates them”, and states the resulting requirement:

“That I feel is chiefly the reason why such stress has been laid in the past upon the necessity for consultation on the part of individual believers with their elected national representatives in the matter of initiating plans of action above and beyond the plans which the deliberations of the National Spiritual Assembly have already evolved.” — Shoghi Effendi, 18 October 1927 (Bahá’í Administration)

His examples are instructive precisely because they are not obvious: affiliating the community with outside bodies whose ideals are sympathetic; founding magazines beyond those already serving the Faith; committing financial support to philanthropic institutions; “advancing the cause of any particular activity to which we may feel sentimentally inclined.” All of these should be approached only after ascertaining “that the institutions representing the paramount interests of the Cause are already assured of adequate and continuous assistance.”

This is the fifth reserved category, and its logic is now visible. The test is not whether an initiative is good. It is whether the initiative draws on shared and finite resources, or commits the community’s name, in a way the community has not decided upon. A study circle in your living room does not. A new national periodical does. A one-off act of neighbourhood service does not. A standing organisation that will need funds, volunteers, and the Faith’s reputation does.

Even this restraint is bounded by its own reasoning. The Guardian is protecting concentration, not seeking control — the sentence immediately preceding is about “the abundant signs of unfaltering energy” he rejoices to see, and in the same document he tells the Assembly that its duty is to “foster the spirit of individual initiative and enterprise.” One letter, both directions.

Devolution as an instrument: what the 1997 Councils document actually does

The most detailed delegation instrument the Bahá’í world possesses is the enclosure to the Universal House of Justice’s letter of 30 May 1997, and it repays reading as an instrument rather than as history. The letter itself explains why a new species of body was required, and the reasoning is entirely about the 1927 dilemma:

“The expansion of the Bahá’í community and the growing complexity of the issues which are facing National Spiritual Assemblies in certain countries have brought the Cause to a new stage in its development. They have caused us in recent years to examine various aspects of the balance between centralization and decentralization.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 May 1997 (The National Spiritual Assembly)

Without such an institution, the letter continues, a national committee structure large enough to meet the need would risk either “over-complexity through adding a further layer of committees under the regional committees, or the danger of excessive decentralization through conferring too much autonomy on committees which are characterized by the Guardian as ‘bodies that should be regarded in no other light than that of expert advisers and executive assistants.’” The Guardian’s rule was not wrong; a committee simply could not carry the load, and stretching the category to carry it would have broken the rule.

So a Council is defined by what it can do that a committee cannot: “It provides for a level of autonomous decision making on both teaching and administrative matters, as distinct from merely executive action, below the National Assembly and above the Local Assemblies.” The distinction between deciding and merely executing is the whole of the difference.

The enclosure specifies the delegation with a precision rare in Bahá’í administrative texts, and four of its provisions transfer to any delegation any institution makes.

The delegate acts without seeking repeated approval. “Regional Bahá’í Councils are allowed to develop their own strategies and programs, and to carry out their day-to-day work without having to obtain further approval from the National Spiritual Assembly.” Delegation that requires clearance at each step is not delegation.

The delegating body sets a threshold for intervening, in advance. “The National Assembly allows the council a wide latitude for autonomous action, intervening in its work only in matters which the Assembly regards as being of major importance.”

Reporting replaces approval. “Through their frequent reports and the minutes of their meetings, the National Assembly is kept informed of their activities and maintains its overall supervision.” Information flows continuously; decisions do not have to.

Retained powers are enumerated, not implied. Administrative rights, the Local Assembly’s direct access to the National Assembly, budget approval, and the routing of World Centre correspondence stay put — and the enclosure closes that last one with an anti-circumvention clause: direct correspondence “should not be misconstrued as a means to bypass the institution of the National Spiritual Assembly in matters requiring guidance or decision.”

Above all, the letter states the counter-intuitive cost of devolving, and it is the sentence that separates Bahá’í devolution from the managerial kind:

“The greater degree of decentralization involved in the devolution of authority upon Regional Bahá’í Councils requires a corresponding increase in the capacity of the National Spiritual Assembly itself to keep fully informed of what is proceeding in all parts of the territory over which it has ultimate jurisdiction.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 May 1997 (national-spiritual-assembly)

Delegation here is not a way of having less to do. It converts a workload of deciding into a workload of knowing, and the second is not obviously lighter. Any Assembly that devolves in order to be relieved has, on the document’s own logic, devolved wrongly.

Why devolve at all? Because for one whole category of work, distance is disqualifying. A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 5 January 2006 draws the line between two kinds of institutional responsibility with unusual clarity:

“The administration of teaching is preeminent among the categories of responsibility in which a National Spiritual Assembly exercises its authority… The execution of this responsibility is of a different character, however, from that of, say, the administration of justice; for whereas the latter is properly concentrated in the activity of the Assembly, which must itself render judgments on cases submitted to it, the former is essentially concerned with efforts initiated and maintained at the base of the community and thus calls for a decentralized mode of management… This closeness of attention is not possible from the top, whatever mechanisms may be set in place at the National Center.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 5 January 2006 (national-spiritual-assembly)

This is the operative test for delegation in the Bahá’í order, and it is neither efficiency nor empowerment. It is epistemic: does deciding this well require knowledge that exists only at the base? Judicial matters do not — the facts come to the Assembly. Teaching does — the relevant knowledge is who is receptive on which street this month, and it cannot travel upward fast enough to be acted on. Where the knowledge lives determines where the decision belongs.

The same instrument was extended to smaller national communities as the National Growth Committee, formalised in the 30 December 2021 message that launched the Nine Year Plan, with the House of Justice granting the NGC “the necessary latitude to foster the movement of clusters, drawing relevant insights from what has been learned about Regional Councils.” The rationale is administrative in the plainest sense — “creating an entity that is wholly occupied with promoting growth should enable an Assembly to give greater attention to other important matters.” By 31 December 2025 the coverage was complete: “With Regional Bahá’í Councils or a National Growth Committee now established in every country, the institutional means are in place everywhere for the process of growth to be systematically advanced” (rbc-vs-nsa, national-growth-committee-vs-rbc).

Delegation has also run downward past the institutions altogether. The Riḍván 2010 message records something easy to miss in a message about culture:

“As learning has come to distinguish the community’s mode of operation, certain aspects of decision making related to expansion and consolidation have been assigned to the body of the believers, enabling planning and implementation to become more responsive to circumstances on the ground. Specifically, a space has been created, in the agency of the reflection meeting, for those engaged in activities at the cluster level to assemble from time to time in order to reach consensus on the current status of their situation… and to determine their immediate steps forward.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

Assigned to the body of the believers. Not to a new committee — to the gathered friends, in a space with no membership roll and no minutes (reflection-meeting-planning). Alongside it the message names the more formal space where “decisions pertaining to growth are taken” — the joint interactions of the training institute, the Area Teaching Committee, and the Auxiliary Board members. Two tiers of delegation, one formal and one not, created because growth required them and not because a theory called for them.

The reciprocal duty falls on the Local Assembly, which in the cluster era acts, in the House of Justice’s phrase, “not as an executor of projects but as the voice of moral authority” — ensuring that as the friends apply the teachings to local conditions, “the integrity of their endeavours is not compromised.” Moral authority is a real form of jurisdiction in this order. It is simply not an executive one.

The territory nobody governs: what is left to conscience, and why

The most striking delegation in the Bahá’í system is the one that stops at the individual and goes no further. It is deliberate, it is defended, and the House of Justice has explained the reasoning more than once.

The clearest statement of the design principle comes in a letter of 3 January 1982:

“The human tendency in past Dispensations has been to want every question answered and to arrive at a binding decision affecting every small detail of belief or practice. The tendency in the Bahá’í Dispensation, from the time of Bahá’u’lláh Himself, has been to clarify the governing principles, to make binding pronouncements on details which are considered essential, but to leave a wide area to the conscience of the individual. The same tendency appears also in administrative matters.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 3 January 1982 (universal-house-of-justice-compilation)

The letter then applies the identical logic one level up: the Guardian held that the working of National Spiritual Assemblies “should be uniform in essentials but that diversity in secondary matters was not only permissible but desirable” — which is why “a number of points are not expressed in the National Bahá’í Constitution; these are left to each National Spiritual Assembly to decide for itself.” Under-legislation is a single principle operating at every tier at once, and the Guardian’s own compression of it — quoted in a 2012 letter to a National Assembly — is the best short statement in the corpus: “This is the whole spirit of Bahá’u’lláh’s system: rigid conformity to great essential laws, elasticity, and even a certain necessary element of diversity, in secondary matters” (uniform-vs-contextual-across-cultures).

A letter of 5 June 1988 names what such a silence protects against:

“…in most areas of human behaviour there are acts which are clearly contrary to the law of God and others which are clearly approved or permissible; between these there is often a grey area where it is not immediately apparent what should be done. It has been a human tendency to wish to eliminate these grey areas so that every aspect of life is clearly prescribed. A result of this tendency has been the tremendous accretion of interpretation and subsidiary legislation which has smothered the spirit of certain of the older religions.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 5 June 1988 (universal-house-of-justice-compilation)

The same letter states the affirmative reason, which is developmental: “This is the age in which mankind must attain maturity, and one aspect of this is the assumption by individuals of the responsibility for deciding, with the assistance of consultation, their own course of action in areas which are left open by the law of God.” And it forecloses the obvious request: “it is neither possible nor desirable for the Universal House of Justice to set forth a set of rules covering every situation. Rather is it the task of the individual believer to determine, according to his own prayerful understanding of the Writings, precisely what his course of conduct should be.”

The most operationally useful text on this boundary is the letter of 12 August 1984, which sorts the “laws” of the Faith into three classes and tells Assemblies how to treat each:

“Some affect the society and the social relationships, and the Spiritual Assemblies are responsible for their enforcement… Others, although of very great importance, are not sanctionable, because their observance is a matter of conscience between the individual and God; among these fall the laws of prayer and fasting and the law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh. Then there are those high ethical standards to which Bahá’u’lláh calls His followers, such as trustworthiness, abstention from backbiting, and so on; generally speaking, obedience to these is a matter for individual conscience, and the Assemblies should not pry into people’s lives to see whether or not they are following them.” — The Universal House of Justice, 12 August 1984 (muhj-1963-1986)

The letter closes both loops. To the individual: “merely because an offence is not punishable” does not make it “less grave.” To the Assembly: “distinguish clearly between those laws which it is their duty to enforce, those which should be left strictly to the conscience of the individual, and those in which it may have to intervene if the misbehavior is blatant and injurious to the good name of the Faith.” Three classes, three institutional postures — enforce, abstain, intervene only at the threshold of public damage.

Ḥuqúqu’lláh is the worked example, and the codification of the law is unambiguous: computation and payment “within the general guidelines set forth, are exclusively a matter of conscience between the individual and God”, demanding or soliciting it is prohibited, and “only appeals, reminders and exhortations of a general nature” are permissible (Codification of the Law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh). The House of Justice has repeatedly declined to close the remaining ambiguities: whether a given household item is “needful” is “left to the discretion of the individual to decide”; whether contributions to the Fund count as expenses is “left to the judgement of each individual in the light of his own circumstances”; and on capital used to earn income, “no specific text has been found” and “the Universal House of Justice leaves such matters to the consciences of individual believers” (Ḥuqúqu’lláh — The Right of God). A body empowered to legislate, faced with a genuine gap, declining to fill it and saying so — that is the delegation decision in its purest form (huqququllah-not-a-tax).

Sometimes the deferral is explicitly temporary. On the Aqdas’s prohibition of gambling, the notes record that the details “have not been outlined in the Writings”, that specifying them is left to the Universal House of Justice, and that the House has indicated “this is a matter that will be considered in detail in the future. In the meantime, the Assemblies and individuals are counseled not to make an issue of these matters and to leave it to the conscience of the individual believers” (kitab-i-aqdas, Note 169). Provisional, and labelled as such — itself a discipline, since an unlabelled silence tends to harden into a rule invented by whoever speaks loudest.

The same architecture protects practice. Meditation is enjoined and simultaneously unspecified: “There are no set forms of meditation prescribed in the teachings, no plan, as such, for inner development. The friends are urged—nay enjoined—to pray, and they also should meditate, but the manner of doing the latter is left entirely to the individual” (prayer-devotional-life). What that pattern means for a believer’s daily life is worked out in daily-practices and self-improvement; the structural point here is that the Faith legislates form only where form is load-bearing, and leaves it open everywhere else.

Even the vote is protected this way. Individual Rights and Freedoms describes a Bahá’í election as “devoid of nominations and campaigning, in which the individual’s freedom to choose is limited only by his own conscience, exercised in private in an attitude that invites communion with the Holy Spirit” — and adds a claim worth sitting with: “In no other system do individuals exercise such a breadth of freedom in the electoral process.”

That message also supplies the balancing frame for this entire article. Freedom in the Bahá’í conception is not the absence of structure but the product of it:

“Contemplating Bahá’u’lláh’s warning that ‘whatsoever passeth beyond the limits of moderation will cease to exert a beneficial influence’, we come to appreciate that the Administrative Order He has conceived embodies the operating principles which are necessary to the maintenance of that moderation which will ensure the ‘true liberty’ of humankind. All things considered, does the Administrative Order not appear to be the structure of freedom for our Age?” — The Universal House of Justice, 29 December 1988 (muhj-1986-2001)

And it names what actually holds the arrangement together, which is not enforcement: the pattern set for institutional and individual behaviour “depends for its efficacy not so much on the force of law, which admittedly must be respected, as on the recognition of a mutuality of benefits, and on the spirit of cooperation maintained by the willingness, the courage, the sense of responsibility, and the initiative of individuals.” The complementary right is stated just as firmly — the individual “has the right to be heard by it, to receive its guidance and assistance, and to appeal from any Assembly decision which he conscientiously feels is unjust or detrimental to the interests of the community” — and offering criticism through the proper channels is “not only the right, but the vital responsibility” of a loyal member. Those channels are treated at length in consultation; the point for delegation is that voice is a conferred part of the system, not a tolerated deviation from it.

How the line moves, and what moves it

If the boundary shifts, something must be governing the shift. The guidance is consistent about what: demonstrated need and demonstrated capacity, in that order. Not principle, not preference, not a theory of empowerment.

The 31 December 2025 message to the Counsellors states the rule for creating new structure as plainly as it has ever been stated:

“Naturally, a new feature would not be introduced unless the needs of growth demand it; nevertheless, we look to you and your auxiliaries to be alert to when existing arrangements at any level of the community need to evolve to meet the requirements of growth, and then, in your interactions with relevant institutions, to encourage the emergence of new arrangements in a suitable form.” — The Universal House of Justice, 31 December 2025 (20251231-001)

The same message reports, approvingly, a whole class of collective endeavour that has emerged without structure: groups of friends organising to improve some aspect of community life “without the need for new layers of administrative structure.” It describes the community itself as “a visible protagonist in the Plan”, steering “the trajectory of its own development with creativity and ingenuity” — an observation the Riḍván 2026 message carries forward, noting “the emergence of the community as a more conspicuous protagonist of the Plan, organizing itself in order to meet particular needs.” Capacity that appears at the base is not automatically institutionalised; it is left to run until administering it becomes necessary.

Movement in the other direction — an institution reaching down — draws consistent correction. Writing to a National Assembly on 9 August 2012 as Regional Councils took up their work, the Secretariat cautioned that “the richest experience exists at the grassroots” and that a growth process “should not come to revolve around their expectations or to rely on their personal presence”, then named the specific mechanism by which well-meaning institutions do damage:

“All those who aim to assist the process of learning at this level must remain sensitive to conditions in individual clusters, lest frequent requests for reports or summons to gatherings—however well intentioned—sap energy or dissipate focus.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 9 August 2012 (national-spiritual-assembly)

The 29 December 2015 message applies the same restraint to planning: those serving at regional or national level “may do much to advise the friends and expand their vision of what can be accomplished, but they would not seek to impose their own expectations on the planning process.” And a letter of 9 February 2017 gives a National Assembly the instruction in its most usable form — agencies must “be afforded sufficient latitude to function in a spirit of learning”, and the Assembly’s task is to “guide them without becoming excessively directive in your oversight of their work.”

Note what these three passages share. None forbids the institution from acting. Each targets a style of acting — the report request, the summons, the imposed expectation, the excessive directiveness — that is formally within the institution’s rights and substantively corrosive. The limits on delegated authority here are not exhausted by the list of things a body may not decide; they extend to how a body exercises what it may.

The learned arm illustrates the principle from another angle, because its authority is precisely zero and its influence is considerable. Auxiliary Board members “do not direct Local Spiritual Assemblies or individual believers in their activities, but are entirely free to make suggestions and recommendations they judge wise and necessary”; conversely, “National and Local Spiritual Assemblies cannot give any directions to Auxiliary Board members”, and a National Assembly wanting a Board member to undertake a service “must convey its request to the Counsellors” (the-institution-of-the-counsellors). The House of Justice’s 24 April 1972 elucidation names how unusual this is: institutions “comprising individuals who play such a vital role, who yet have no legislative, administrative or judicial authority… is a feature of Bahá’í administration unparalleled in the religions of the past.” Influence without jurisdiction is a real category in this order, and not a consolation prize (counsellors-vs-spiritual-assemblies).

Even between two institutions with clear roles, the guidance resists mechanical application. A letter of 25 March 2012 warns a National Assembly against “a sterile focus on roles” leading to “a rigid application of the inadequate generalization that Counsellors advise, National Assemblies decide, and Counsellors support” — and against the opposite extreme, “the imposition of the stricture that everything is to be decided together.” Both errors replace judgement with a rule. The document those two institutions study together says the same in a sentence: their attitude to each other “is not motivated by a legalistic application of the rules of their functional relationship.”

What the wider world has learned about the same problem

The Bahá’í corpus supplies the principles; it does not describe how comparable questions are handled elsewhere. What follows is description of the contemporary field only — not authority for any Bahá’í principle.

The closest secular analogue is subsidiarity, given legal force in Article 5(3) of the Treaty on European Union: outside its exclusive competences the Union acts only where member states cannot sufficiently achieve the objective and where Union-level action adds value by reason of scale or effects. The summary formula is “Europe where necessary, national where possible.” The enforcement machinery is where the contrast sharpens. Under Protocol No. 2, national parliaments have eight weeks from the forwarding of a draft legislative act to file reasoned opinions; if one third of national parliament votes object (one quarter for police and criminal-judicial cooperation), the Commission must review the proposal — the “yellow card”. It has been triggered three times: the Monti II proposal on collective action (May 2012), the European Public Prosecutor’s Office (October 2013), and the revision of the Posting of Workers Directive (May 2016). The stronger “orange card” has never been used.

Two differences matter. EU subsidiarity is adversarial by construction: the lower tier’s protection is a challenge mechanism with thresholds and deadlines, exercised against the centre. The Bahá’í order has an escalation route — the Constitution’s appeal ladder — but it is a route for redress, not a veto held by a lower tier over a higher, and Individual Rights and Freedoms spends several paragraphs explaining why the “loyal opposition” model is not imported. And the triggering tests differ: subsidiarity asks at what level can the objective be achieved; the 5 January 2006 letter asks where does the knowledge live. These often coincide, but they are not the same question.

The empirical literature on administrative decentralisation converges on the point the 1997 letter made from principle. Systematic reviews of studies published between roughly 2001 and 2025 report a mixed record: measurable gains in some settings for local health and education service delivery, and failures elsewhere that track closely to weak local administrative and financial capacity. Research on Indonesia and on Latin American education systems identifies local government capacity — particularly financial management and inter-agency coordination — as the variable separating success from failure. Devolving a function to a body that cannot yet carry it does not produce local ownership; it produces a gap. That is field description, not Bahá’í teaching. But it does suggest that when the House of Justice makes each Council conditional on a case-by-case judgement that “this kind of decentralization is judged by the Universal House of Justice to be appropriate”, and requires a matching rise in the National Assembly’s own capacity, it is not being cautious for caution’s sake.

The third contrast is the one most worth naming. In secular systems delegation is typically accompanied by monitoring — the delegating body reduces its risk by watching more closely, and the cost of watching has collapsed over two decades. The Bahá’í guidance requires the delegating body to “keep fully informed”, then hedges that requirement immediately with a warning against the very instrument that makes it cheap: “frequent requests for reports or summons to gatherings—however well intentioned—sap energy or dissipate focus.” A system whose monitoring becomes frictionless is a system under permanent temptation to re-centralise without ever formally doing so. That tension is live now in a way it was not in 1997.

Taking a position: a working rule for someone deciding how to act

The guidance, taken together, supports a rule short enough to use.

Start from the presumption that it is yours to do. The duty to teach “cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution of the Cause.” Hosting a devotional gathering, tutoring a study circle, animating a junior youth group, visiting a family, beginning a modest act of service in your neighbourhood, raising a question in consultation, forming your own understanding of the Writings — none of these requires anyone’s leave, and treating them as though they did is itself a departure from the guidance. The Guardian’s diagnosis of the friends’ “lack of courage and of initiative” and their “feeling of inferiority” was that these “do not only paralyse their efforts but actually serve to quench the flame of faith in their hearts.”

Refer upward when your act would bind, commit, or represent someone else. Concretely: when you would speak for the community rather than for yourself; when you would publish about the Faith; when money, property, or a legal entity in the Faith’s name is involved; when another believer’s standing or a dispute between believers is at issue; or when — the 1927 test — you would initiate “plans of action above and beyond the plans which the deliberations of the National Spiritual Assembly have already evolved” and those plans would draw on the community’s shared and finite resources. The trigger is not the size of the idea but the ownership of what it consumes.

When you are unsure, the move is consultation — not silence, and not a permission request. The commonest error is a believer who treats an unclear boundary as a prohibition and simply stops; the second commonest is an institution that treats an unclear boundary as an occasion to legislate. Both are answered by the same instrument. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s instruction — “It is incumbent upon everyone not to take any step without consulting the Spiritual Assembly” — is not a licensing regime; read alongside the Guardian’s insistence that an Assembly’s function “is not to dictate, but to consult”, it describes a two-way conversation whose product is usually clarity rather than a verdict.

If you serve on an institution, delegate by writing down four things. The 1997 enclosure is the template and it generalises: (1) what the delegate may decide without returning for approval; (2) the threshold at which you will intervene; (3) what flows back to you, how often, in what form; (4) what you retain, enumerated rather than implied. A delegation missing any of these is not a delegation; it is a future jurisdiction dispute. And accept the cost: devolution “requires a corresponding increase in the capacity” of the body that devolves. If handing something over is going to make your Assembly’s life easier, you have probably handed over the deciding and kept none of the knowing — and the appeal, when it comes, will find you uninformed.

For a reader with technical skill, the leverage here is unusually specific. The binding constraint on Bahá’í devolution is not authority; it is the cost of the information flow that authorises it. A letter of 24 June 2010 identifies “the flow of guidance, information, and funds” as one of the purposes the administrative structure exists to serve, noting that all of it “require[s] formal systems and instruments”. The 1997 model — wide latitude, reporting instead of approval — holds only where reporting is cheap, timely and honest. Software that lowers the cost of a Regional Council keeping a National Assembly genuinely informed, or of a cluster’s hard-won learning reaching the adjacent cluster rather than only the tier above, does more for the health of the delegation than any amount of clarification about who may decide what.

But build it against a stated constraint, because the same technology cuts the other way. The purpose is to make the delegate legible, not supervised. Every extra field on a form, every added reporting cadence, every dashboard that lets a national office watch a neighbourhood in real time moves the system toward the failure the 9 August 2012 letter named. The test for any such instrument is not “does this give the institution better visibility?” but “does this reduce the total time the friends spend reporting while increasing what the institution actually knows?” A reporting system that only ever grows is re-centralisation with a friendly interface. The 31 December 2025 rule applies to software as much as to structures: a new feature would not be introduced unless the needs of growth demand it.

The measure of the whole arrangement is not tidiness. It is the one the House of Justice named in 2010, describing what a matured community looks like: that “all come to discern with ease those areas of activity in which the individual can best exercise initiative and those which fall to the institutions alone.” Where friends and institutions have to keep asking who decides, the answer is rarely a better org chart. It is the consultation that has not yet been held.

Open questions / honest limits

  • There is no authoritative list of reserved matters. The five categories set out above are my synthesis of scattered guidance — the 1922 and 1927 letters, the 1972 Constitution, the 1997 enclosure, the 2019 review letter — not a formulation the Universal House of Justice has issued. Treat the grouping as a reading aid, not as a rule. Where a specific case is genuinely unclear, the guidance’s own answer is to ask the relevant institution, not to reason from a synthesis.
  • The 1927 “concentration of resources” test is easier to state than to apply. Shoghi Effendi’s examples — affiliation with outside bodies, founding magazines, philanthropic commitments — belong to a community of a few thousand believers in one country. What counts as “above and beyond” for an individual today, in a world where launching a website, an organisation, or a fundraising campaign costs almost nothing, is not settled by the text and this page does not settle it.
  • The relationship between the Riḍván 2010 assignment of “certain aspects of decision making… to the body of the believers” and the formal authority of elected institutions has not been fully worked out in the guidance. The reflection meeting reaches consensus and determines next steps; it is not a body with conferred authority, and the message does not attempt a jurisprudence of the arrangement. Accounts of how it works in practice are field experience, not doctrine (three-protagonists-interaction-and-failure-modes).
  • Several matters remain labelled deferrals rather than settled questions — gambling, the penalties for offences left to the House of Justice to determine, the details of marriage between relatives, aspects of the Bahá’í calendar. A synthesis cannot close them and this one does not try.
  • The line between “matters of conscience” and matters an Assembly must address is a threshold, not a boundary. The 12 August 1984 letter locates it with a phrase — conduct that “becomes a flagrant disgrace and brings the name of the Faith into disrepute” — requiring judgement in every application. Different Assemblies will place it differently, and the guidance appears to accept that.
  • Consultation is treated here only as an instrument of delegation. Its own principles, requisites and failure modes belong to consultation.
  • The external material will age and grounds nothing. The EU subsidiarity mechanics are current as of 2026 but the treaty framework and the yellow-card count can change; the decentralisation literature is mixed, drawn from heterogeneous settings, and the reviews cited summarise other people’s studies rather than reporting new evidence. None of it is authority for a Bahá’í principle.
  • This page does not cover the organisational history of the institutions (bahai-institutions-architecture), the question of whose word finally decides (bahai-authority-framework), the mechanics of cluster classification (cluster-development-framework), or the specific delegation questions that arise in social action beyond the LSA’s role as “the voice of moral authority” (social-action-guidance).
