The Ode of the Dove (Qaṣídiy-i-‘Izz-i-Varqá’íyyih)

Last updated 17 October 2026

The Qaṣídiy-i-Varqá’íyyih, full title Qaṣídiy-i-‘Izz-i-Varqá’íyyih, is an Arabic ode of 127 verses by Bahá’u’lláh. Shoghi Effendi calls it an ode revealed in Kurdistán “in praise of the Maiden personifying the Spirit of God”. In English it is called the Ode of the Dove. No authorised English translation exists. Two full provisional translations are available, by Juan Cole (1997) and by John S. Hatcher, Amrollah Hemmat and Ehsanollah Hemmat (2019), and this page compares them. The poem is one of a series; see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poem

When, where and why it was written

Bahá’u’lláh spent about two years in Kurdistán, first alone in the mountains and then in the town of Sulaymáníyyih (GPB VII.37–39; the town is in what is now Iraqi Kurdistán). The sources give the dates of the poem only loosely. Christopher Buck writes that during “his two-year sojourn in the mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan (1854–1856), Bahá’u’lláh composed a mystical ode in Arabic”. The Wilmette Institute outline by Jonah Winters gives “Sometime between April 1854-March 1856”. Juan Cole’s header for his translation gives “1856(?)”. See “What is not settled” below.

Shoghi Effendi describes the occasion in God Passes By. He first tells how a delegation of doctors and students from the seminary in Sulaymáníyyih, headed by Shaykh Ismá‘íl, came to question Bahá’u’lláh (GPB VII.39). Shoghi Effendi then quotes the learned men’s request:

“No one among the mystics, the wise, and the learned,” they claimed, while requesting this further favor from Him, “has hitherto proved himself capable of writing a poem in a rhyme and meter identical with that of the longer of the two odes, entitled Qaṣídiy-i-Tá’íyyih composed by Ibn-i-Fáriḍ. We beg you to write for us a poem in that same meter and rhyme.”

(God Passes By, GPB VII.40; authorised text by Shoghi Effendi)

He continues in the same paragraph:

This request was complied with, and no less than two thousand verses, in exactly the manner they had specified, were dictated by Him, out of which He selected one hundred and twenty-seven, which He permitted them to keep, deeming the subject matter of the rest premature and unsuitable to the needs of the times. It is these same one hundred and twenty-seven verses that constitute the Qaṣídiy-i-Varqá’íyyih, so familiar to, and widely circulated amongst, His Arabic speaking followers.

(GPB VII.40)

In the next paragraph he reports their judgment of the result:

Such was their reaction to this marvelous demonstration of the sagacity and genius of Bahá’u’lláh that they unanimously acknowledged every single verse of that poem to be endowed with a force, beauty and power far surpassing anything contained in either the major or minor odes composed by that celebrated poet.

(GPB VII.41)

Earlier in the same chapter Shoghi Effendi had already quoted the ode itself, as an expression of Bahá’u’lláh’s grief in those years (GPB VII.31). That passage is quoted under “The text in English” below.

Form and language

The poem is in Arabic. Taherzadeh notes that Bahá’u’lláh was a Persian. Its model is the Tá’íyyih of Ibn al-Fáriḍ (an Arabic mystical poet; Bahaipedia gives the poem’s other title as al-Tá’íyyatu’l-kubrá). According to Shoghi Effendi’s account, it follows that poem in rhyme and metre, as the delegation asked. Adib Taherzadeh, whose account (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, pp. 62–64) follows Shoghi Effendi’s, adds a comment on its language:

With the use of only one or two words Bahá’u’lláh often makes reference to a verse of the Qur’an or a certain tradition of Islam.

The title word varqá’ means “dove”. Winters suggests that the dove is Bahá’u’lláh Himself, because in the Persian Notes (below) He speaks of “the Dove of the divine Essence”. This is Winters’s reading, not a statement from the Writings about the title.

The Notes

After His return to Baghdád, Bahá’u’lláh added notes in Persian to the poem. Taherzadeh describes them:

After His return to Baghdad, Bahá’u’lláh wrote some footnotes to this poem; in these He gave the meanings in Persian of the difficult words and also interpreted some of its abstruse verses.

Cole has translated part of these Notes (Bahá’u’lláh’s Notes to His “Ode of the Dove”, 1997), with his own commentary. They are a primary text that explains some of the poem’s allusions, for example the “shadows of the clouds” in verse 4 as a reference to Qur’án 2:210. They are provisional in translation like the poem.

Subject and speakers

Taherzadeh gives the theme:

The theme of the Qasidiy-i-Varqa’iyyih is the praise and glorification of the Most Great Spirit which had descended upon Him in the symbolic form of the ‘Maid of Heaven’.

He adds that it is a dialogue between Bahá’u’lláh “as the Bearer of God’s Message” and the Holy Spirit personified as the Maid of Heaven, and that it shows “the relationship between the person of the Manifestation of God and the Holy Spirit which animates and sustains Him”.

Both translations show how the dialogue is arranged. In the translations the speakers alternate as follows (verse numbers are the translators’):

  • Verses 1–36: Bahá’u’lláh praises the Maiden and laments His separation from her.
  • Verses 37–61: she answers, saying she has been sought by many and that the lover must accept suffering.
  • Verses 62–97: He answers, accepting whatever she decrees, and recounts His sufferings, including the trials of earlier Prophets.
  • Verses 98–123: she speaks again, saying that she is beyond all He or others have understood of her, and tells Him to keep hidden what He has seen.
  • Verses 124–127: closing verses of blessing on those who are loyal, who love, and who are sincere.

This outline is the writer of this page’s reading of the two translations, not a statement from a source.

Early reception

Bahá’í histories record that the poem was read before the Declaration. Taherzadeh tells that Mullá Muḥammad-Riḍá of Yazd, shown the poem by Raḍíyu’r-Rúḥ, exclaimed:

‘I can see the Promised One of the Bayan made manifest and seated upon the throne of the words which have been revealed in this Tablet’.

(Taherzadeh, vol. 1, pp. 84–85)

Buck cites the same account from Samandar and notes that Denis MacEoin treats the ode as of limited use for tracing Bahá’u’lláh’s claims, while Cole reads self-reference in its language. The book Stories of Bahá’u’lláh and Some Notable Believers also says that Mírzá Mihdí, the Purest Branch, was chanting the verses of the ode on the prison roof when he fell through a skylight and was badly wounded.

The text in English

Authorised translation

None. The Bahá’í World Centre has not published an authorised translation of the ode. Bahaipedia (tier D) states that neither the poem nor its Notes “have been officially translated, but provisional translations of both exist”.

Shoghi Effendi did translate one passage, in God Passes By. It is the only authorised English text known to this page. It is a rendering of lines about the sufferings of the earlier Prophets; Shoghi Effendi does not give verse numbers:

Noah’s flood is but the measure of the tears I have shed, and Abraham’s fire an ebullition of My soul. Jacob’s grief is but a reflection of My sorrows, and Job’s afflictions a fraction of my calamity.

(GPB VII.31; authorised translation by Shoghi Effendi; licence `ok-with-credit`)

The subject matches verses 25–26 and 72–75 of the poem. The sentence is a rendering of the ode, but the source does not say which verses it translates, so this page does not assign it a verse number.

Provisional translation by Juan Cole (1997)

Cole’s translation covers all 127 verses and is online at bahai-library.com. The header marks it “Provisional translations”; the listing gives its permission status as “translator”. Cole writes that he made a first version in 1977–78 in Beirut and later redid it as “a series of iambic pentameter quatrains rhyming in abcb”. He says that meter and rhyme make the translator depart from the literal meaning, but that they keep “something of the feel of the original”. He also remarks that “perhaps this should in fact be looked at as an attempt at a song lyric”, noting that Bahá’u’lláh encouraged such things to be chanted and sung. Provisional translation by John S. Hatcher, Amrollah Hemmat and Ehsanollah Hemmat (2019)

Published as “Bahá’u’lláh’s ‘Ode of the Dove’: A Provisional Translation”, Journal of Bahá’í Studies 29:3 (Ottawa: Association for Bahá’í Studies North America, 2019), pp. 43–66, with a companion article by Hatcher on the symbol of the veiled Ḥúríyyih. It renders the poem in unrhymed lines, one couplet to a verse, with the speakers marked by quotation marks, and has footnotes. The bahai-library.com listing gives its permission status as “publisher”. Licence for a published lyric: `permission-needed` (ask the publisher and translators).

Other translations

Winters records earlier provisional translations by Denis MacEoin (Bahá’í Studies Bulletin 2:2, September 1983), with comments by Cole in 2:3 and 2:4. A link to MacEoin’s translation is given on Bahaipedia. It is not in the corpus and is not used here. A thesis by Brian A. Miller (University of California, Berkeley) on the Ode of the Dove and Ibn al-Fáriḍ’s Naẓm al-sulúk includes a translation; the extract in the corpus is poorly scanned and is not quoted.

A machine translation (Gemini 4 Argon, 17 October 2026), not reviewed by a translator, is published as a separate page: machine translation of the Ode of the Dove. It is labelled as a machine translation and is not quoted here.

The two translations compared

Both are provisional, and both are quoted below only in short extracts for discussion. In the Hatcher–Hemmat text the verse numbers (which sit at the end of the first line of each verse) are left out.

The opening, verse 1. Cole’s version is a rhymed quatrain unit; Hatcher and the Hemmats follow the sentence order of the Arabic more closely.

Cole: I was enthralled by light rays from a face / Whose advent dimmed and darkened every star,

Hatcher–Hemmat: The light from such a countenance so attracted me; / its brightness outshone every bright sun,

The Maiden’s first reply, verse 37. The two agree on the sense, with different tone.

Cole: Then from behind Me She cried out, ”Be still! / and hold Thy tongue from all that it hath told.

Hatcher–Hemmat: Suddenly, from behind me, Her voice said, “Hush! / Cease thy lamenting about all thou hast endured!

The price of love, verse 60. Both keep the paradox that poison is drink.

Cole: In My faith poison’s as a healing drink; / in My Path, fate’s wrath is a tender grace.

Hatcher–Hemmat: but by the standards of Faith, deadly poison is like a refreshing drink, / and according to My creed, destiny’s rage is like affection.

The trials of the Prophets, verse 72. Compare with Shoghi Effendi’s sentence above, which names the same figures in a different order.

Cole: The grief of Jacob, Joseph’s prison cell; / the Friend’s white hot flames, and Job’s tragic check;

Hatcher–Hemmat: Jacob’s grief and Joseph’s confinement, / Job’s afflictions and Abraham’s fire,

Cole translates Khalíl (“Friend”, the title of Abraham) as “the Friend”; Hatcher–Hemmat give “Abraham’s fire”. Shoghi Effendi’s “Abraham’s fire” agrees with the second.

A place name, verse 92. Here the translators read the Arabic differently. The Arabic of this verse has the word al-Quds (which means “holiness” and also names Jerusalem) and the letter Ṭá’.

Cole: I knew Jerusalem by the Friend’s glow; / I journeyed in Tihran when taking flight.

Hatcher–Hemmat: By the light of His friendship, I enjoyed the company of holiness, / and I immigrated from the land of Tá during the time of my remoteness.

Cole takes the letter Ṭá’ to stand for Ṭihrán and writes the city’s name; Hatcher–Hemmat keep the letter, “the land of Tá”. The original gives only the letter, so “Ṭihrán” is the translator’s reading.

Idolatry and unity, verse 99. The Maiden says that idolatry, in her sight, is unity.

Cole: Forget all that Thou hast known and adored; / idolatry, for Me, is unity;

Hatcher–Hemmat: Relinquish all thou hast understood before—and thence worshipped. / Indeed, for Me (even) idolatry is but a recognition of the unity of God.

Hatcher–Hemmat’s line is longer and adds “of God”. They put words they supply in parentheses, here “(even)”.

In general: Cole’s version is rhymed and metred, and by his own account departs from the literal meaning where rhyme and metre require. Hatcher–Hemmat is unrhymed, runs longer, uses parentheses for supplied words and has footnotes. Neither is the text of the Writings; both are labelled provisional.

Themes and images

Separation and longing. The first part is a lover’s lament. In Cole’s version: “To win Her I have wept in every eye” (verse 11). The Maiden’s answer is that He is one of many lovers, and that love requires suffering: “Cease claiming to love, or accept all this” (Cole, verse 61).

Suffering as the price of the path. Verse 60 (above) and the long list of the earlier Prophets’ trials in verses 72–75 place Bahá’u’lláh’s grief beside that of Adam, Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Joseph, Job, Jonah, David, Eve, Mary, Isaiah and Zachariah. Shoghi Effendi used the passage to show “the agonies of His sorrow-laden heart” (GPB VII.31).

Exile and solitude. Verse 76 in Cole: “See how I roamed the lands without a friend / save for the beast within the wilderness.” In Hatcher–Hemmat the same verse speaks of “companionship with the wild beasts in this far desert”. Verse 81 mentions scars of iron on the neck and marks of fetters on the feet; Taherzadeh’s summary of the poem says that Bahá’u’lláh “recounts the cruel fashion in which His enemies had imprisoned Him with chains and fetters”.

The Maiden and the Spirit. She is described as light that dims the stars (verse 1), as the source of musk, the trumpet of Resurrection, and the dawn of the sun (verses 3–6), and later as the one before whom Sinai’s brightest glow is nothing (verse 100 in Cole). In verses 41–42 she says of herself, in Hatcher–Hemmat: “Compared to My dawning, the midday sun is like a star”.

Revelation beyond understanding. In the last part the Maiden tells Him to hold fast to the Cause in its outward form, to know the face of light in its hidden form, and to keep a mystery from others. Hatcher–Hemmat verse 123 ends her speech with the warning that if the veil were lifted “all existence will vanish in the twinkling of an eye”.

Blessing. The last four verses are beatitudes. In Cole: “Blessed are the lovers for the blood they shed; / blessed are the ones who with My love unite.” (verse 126)

What is not settled

  • Date. The sources give 1854–1856 (Buck), April 1854 to March 1856 (Winters) or “1856(?)” (Cole’s header). Shoghi Effendi places the writing in the time of the retirement to Kurdistán (GPB VII.31), and the request in the stay at Sulaymáníyyih (GPB VII.39–40).
  • Place. Shoghi Effendi says the ode was revealed “during the days of His retirement to the mountains of Kurdistán” (GPB VII.31) but also tells the story of the request at Sulaymáníyyih. Taherzadeh places it in the Sulaymáníyyih sojourn; Winters says “in retreat in the mountains of Kurdistan, north of Baghdad, near the town of Sulaymaniyyih”.
  • The date of the Notes. Taherzadeh says only that they were written after the return to Baghdád. Bahaipedia (tier D) says “likely in late 1856” and gives a reason, a reply to criticism of the ode by some Bábís, citing Cole. This page does not rely on that.
  • How Bahá’u’lláh’s claim is reflected. Buck reports a disagreement among scholars: MacEoin finds the ode of limited use for dating the development of Bahá’u’lláh’s claim; Cole reads the ode as showing a “messianic secret”. The early Bábí reaction recorded by Taherzadeh, Samandar and Buck shows that some readers took it as a claim. Shoghi Effendi’s account does not say this, and this page does not either.
  • The dialogue’s meaning. Bahaipedia (citing Stephen Lambden) says the Maiden stands for spiritual perfections and that the lover’s wish for union is a way of speaking about the wish to declare His station. Miller, in a dissertation, argues that Bahá’u’lláh used the request to give form to an experience He had while in retreat on the mountain. Both are scholarly interpretations, not statements of the Writings.
  • Variant readings. The oceanoflights text used here (Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá, vol. 2, pp. 321–339) is a modern typesetting without verse marks. Cole notes that for the word “tha’r” (blood) in verse 25 he relied on a manuscript in the hand of Zaynu’l-Muqarribín supplied by the World Centre. The translators differ in places (verse 92 above), and no critical edition is in the corpus.
  • Whether a translation may be published. The Universal House of Justice (policies of 4 July 1999, in Messages 1986–2001, appendix to 305) allows believers to translate for their own use, with publication in journals, provided the translations are labelled as “provisional translations”. Cole’s and Hatcher–Hemmat’s texts are labelled that way. This does not make them the text of the Writings.

For songwriting

Status of the texts. There is no authorised English text of the ode as a whole. Any lyric that quotes a translation needs the translator’s or publisher’s permission, and a published lyric should say that the text is a provisional translation and name the translator. The Arabic text saved in the corpus comes from OceanOfLights.org and carries that site’s rights notice; check it before printing any Arabic. The only authorised English wording is Shoghi Effendi’s passage.

  • “Jacob’s grief is but a reflection of My sorrows” — Shoghi Effendi, GPB VII.31, authorised; `ok-with-credit`.

Concrete images. A face so bright that stars fade; the trumpet of the Last Day; a lover scratching signs of nearness in the sand of every plain; a hand stretched out and answered with a sword; wild beasts in the desert; iron scars on the neck; the houris in mourning black; poison taken as a healing drink; a veil torn from a hidden face; a dove.

A possible arc. (1) Wonder at a light that outshines the sun. (2) Longing and the lament of separation. (3) The refusal: love must cost something. (4) The lover consents and takes up the suffering. (5) A second reply: the beloved is beyond every name, and the lover is to keep a secret. (6) Blessing on the faithful, the loving and the sincere.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not present a provisional translation as the text of the Writings, or call it “the Ode of the Dove in English” without saying whose translation it is.
  • It must not say that the Maiden is a woman, a lover in the human sense or a historical person. Shoghi Effendi and Taherzadeh describe her as the personification of the Holy Spirit or Spirit of God; Cole explains the use of the unrequited-love convention of Arabic and Persian poetry as a symbol for the unknowability of God.
  • It must not say that this poem announces Bahá’u’lláh’s claim, or that He made a claim in it, which no authorised source says.
  • It must not give a date, place or number of verses beyond what the sources support: 127 verses, selected from about two thousand, written in Kurdistán about 1854–56.
  • It must not end on the Maiden’s refusal or on a promise of reunion; the closing verses are blessings on the faithful, and the poem ends there.
  • It should not quote the verse-92 place names without saying that the original gives the letter Ṭá’.
