How Guidance Evolves: Progressive Revelation and the Supersession of Earlier Guidance

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í system draws a hard line in exactly one place, and everything about how guidance evolves follows from where that line falls. On one side stands what God has revealed — immutable for the duration of the Dispensation, alterable by no institution and no Guardian. On the other stands what human institutions have enacted — designed from the outset to be amended and repealed, by the same body that enacted it. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states the rule in a single sentence of His Will and Testament: “The House of Justice is both the initiator and the abrogator of its own laws.” Shoghi Effendi names the pair the whole architecture rests on: “Such is the immutability of His revealed Word. Such is the elasticity which characterizes the functions of His appointed ministers.”

My position on the central question: earlier guidance is honoured rather than falsified because Bahá’í supersession runs on the grammar of timeliness, not the grammar of error. When a law of a former Dispensation is set aside, the Writings do not say it was mistaken; they say the conditions it fitted have passed. Bahá’u’lláh’s own formulation, in the Glad-Tidings, is that ordinances such as holy war and the ban on association with other peoples “had been laid down and affirmed according to the exigencies of the time.” Laid down. Affirmed. And now, for a different age, replaced. That is a claim about the patient’s condition, not about the physician’s competence — which is precisely the image the Writings choose: “The All-Knowing Physician hath His finger on the pulse of mankind.”

The failure I see most often among practising Bahá’ís is not disobedience when guidance shifts. It is misfiling. Friends attach a piece of guidance to the wrong level — treating a method as though it were a law, an institutional arrangement as though it were scripture, a Riḍván message’s emphasis as though it were the Aqdas — and then, when the guidance moves, they experience either scandal or vertigo. The mirror error is equally common and equally destabilising: reading the revealed Text as a period piece that the institutions will eventually soften. Both errors make ordinary, anticipated change feel like rupture.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest: when guidance changes, the useful question is never “so were we wrong before?” It is “at what level was this, and what has changed in the conditions it was fitted to?” Almost every pastoral difficulty around a change of direction dissolves once that question is asked first. The Universal House of Justice models the discipline itself — its 10 July 2014 calendar letter names what is newly decided, then states plainly that “unless specifically abrogated by these new provisions, previous guidance and clarifications pertaining to the calendar and the observance of the Nineteen Day Feast and Holy Days remain binding.” A community that narrated its own changes with that precision would rarely have to argue about them.

The line is drawn once, and everything follows from where it falls

The power to repeal is not an inference drawn by later administrators from the silence of the Text. It is conferred in writing, with a worked example attached, in the constitutional document of the Faith.

“And inasmuch as this House of Justice hath power to enact laws that are not expressly recorded in the Book and bear upon daily transactions, so also it hath power to repeal the same. Thus for example, the House of Justice enacteth today a certain law and enforceth it, and a hundred years hence, circumstances having profoundly changed and the conditions having altered, another House of Justice will then have power, according to the exigencies of the time, to alter that law. This it can do because that law formeth no part of the Divine Explicit Text. The House of Justice is both the initiator and the abrogator of its own laws.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Will and Testament, Part I

Read the clause that does the real work: “because that law formeth no part of the Divine Explicit Text.” Repealability is not a concession to human fallibility. It is a consequence of provenance. What the House of Justice enacts is repealable because of where it came from, not because it might turn out to have been a mistake — and the same paragraph that grants the power of repeal states, of the House’s decisions, that “whatsoever they decide hath the same effect as the Text itself.” Binding now; alterable later; never wrong in the interval.

Shoghi Effendi draws the consequence out in a letter of 21 March 1930, in the section of The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh headed “A Living Organism.” Having quoted Bahá’u’lláh’s provision in the Eighth Leaf of the Words of Paradise — “It is incumbent upon the Trustees of the House of Justice to take counsel together regarding those things which have not outwardly been revealed in the Book” — and then ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s grant of the power to repeal, he sets the two against each other:

“Such is the immutability of His revealed Word. Such is the elasticity which characterizes the functions of His appointed ministers. The first preserves the identity of His Faith, and guards the integrity of His law. The second enables it, even as a living organism, to expand and adapt itself to the needs and requirements of an ever-changing society.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, 21 March 1930

Immutability and elasticity, each doing a job the other cannot. Strip out the immutability and the Faith becomes whatever the current generation finds congenial; strip out the elasticity and it becomes a museum. The Guardian’s metaphor is deliberate: a living organism is not less itself for changing, and it is not more itself for refusing to.

The boundary is then stated from the other direction, in the negative, more than once. A letter written on the Guardian’s behalf in 1935 — quoted in the introduction to the authorised Kitáb-i-Aqdas — establishes that the House of Justice “can supplement but never invalidate or modify in the least degree what has already been formulated by Bahá’u’lláh. Nor has the Guardian any right whatsoever to lessen the binding effect much less to abrogate the provisions of so fundamental and sacred a Book.” The same introduction restates it as the operative rule of the whole legal system: “Although the Universal House of Justice is explicitly authorized to change or repeal its own legislation as conditions change, thus providing Bahá’í law with an essential element of flexibility, it cannot abrogate or change any of the laws which are explicitly laid down in the sacred Text.”

When the House of Justice came to write its own Constitution in 1972, the power appears in the enumerated list of its duties without ornament, sandwiched between legislating and adjudicating: “To enact laws and ordinances not expressly recorded in the Sacred Texts; to abrogate, according to the changes and requirements of the time, its own enactments; to deliberate and decide upon all problems which have caused difference; to elucidate questions that are obscure.” Nine words carry the entire mechanism of Bahá’í precedence — to abrogate, according to the changes and requirements of the time, its own enactments — and the two governing limits are inside the sentence: according to the changes and requirements of the time (the trigger is conditions, not error), and its own (the object is never the Text).

The distinction between what may be amended and what may not track a further distinction the House has taken care to make explicit — between interpretation, which is closed, and elucidation, which is not. A letter written on its behalf on 27 August 1998 puts it with unusual sharpness:

“The divinely inspired legislation of the House of Justice does not attempt to say what the revealed Word means—it states what must be done in cases where the revealed Text or its authoritative interpretation is not explicit… The major distinction between the two functions of elucidation and interpretation, to repeat the point differently, is that legislation with its resultant outcome of elucidation is susceptible of amendment by the House of Justice itself, whereas the Guardian’s interpretation is a statement of truth which cannot be varied.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 27 August 1998 (universal-house-of-justice-compilation)

A statement of truth which cannot be varied on one side; susceptible of amendment on the other. This is why a believer who has grasped the architecture is not disturbed when a House letter revises an earlier House letter, and would be gravely disturbed by anything purporting to revise ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. The two events belong to different categories. The full architecture is laid out in bahai-authority-framework and AuthorityAndInfallibility; what concerns us here is the single consequence — that the Faith has a designed, licensed, scripturally warranted mechanism for changing its own mind about its own enactments, and no mechanism at all for changing its mind about the Word.

Why law changes at all: the physician, not the legislature

The doctrinal ground for supersession between Dispensations is not administrative convenience. It is an account of what religion is for, and it was given in answer to a question from outside the Faith.

Mánikchí Ṣáḥib — Mánikchí Limjí Hataria (1813–1890), a Zoroastrian born in India and appointed in 1854 as an emissary on behalf of the Parsees of India to assist their coreligionists in Iran — put a series of questions to Bahá’u’lláh, among them one about the heavenly Scriptures. The reply, revealed at his request in pure Persian and preserved in The Tabernacle of Unity, contains what has since become the most quoted formulation of progressive revelation in the corpus:

“As to thy question concerning the heavenly Scriptures: The All-Knowing Physician hath His finger on the pulse of mankind. He perceiveth the disease, and prescribeth, in His unerring wisdom, the remedy. Every age hath its own problem, and every soul its particular aspiration. The remedy the world needeth in its present-day afflictions can never be the same as that which a subsequent age may require. Be anxiously concerned with the needs of the age ye live in, and centre your deliberations on its exigencies and requirements.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Tabernacle of Unity (also Gleanings, CVI)

The image settles the question of honour before it is asked. A physician who changes a prescription because the patient’s condition has changed is not repudiating the earlier prescription; changing it is the same competence that issued it. Bahá’u’lláh does not say the remedy of a former age was ineffective. He says it was for that age — and the closing instruction turns the whole doctrine outward into a working obligation: be anxiously concerned with the needs of the age ye live in. Progressive revelation is not chiefly a theory about the past. It is a standing warning against administering yesterday’s medicine.

Mánikchí Ṣáḥib was not satisfied, and a second Tablet followed — revealed on 1 July 1882 and addressed to Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl, then serving as his secretary. Asked outright “Which of these creeds is acceptable and which of these leaders is to be preferred?”, Bahá’u’lláh does not answer with a ranking. He quotes the physician passage back and says that “the answer to all that his honour the Ṣáḥib hath asked lieth enshrined within this all-embracing, this weighty and incomparable utterance.” The question “whose religion is right?” is answered by a doctrine of timeliness. That is the move the rest of this article traces downward through every level of the Bahá’í order.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá develops the same figure at length in Some Answered Questions, in a chapter whose title states the doctrine outright — “Baptism and the Changing Law of God.” The question put to Him is the sharpest form of the objection: if baptism was useful, why was it abrogated; if useless, why did John practise it?

“The body of the world can be compared to that of a man, and the Prophets and Messengers of God to able physicians. A human being does not remain always in the same condition: Different ailments occur and each calls for a specific remedy. Thus an able physician does not treat all ailments in the same manner but varies the treatments and remedies in accordance with the requirements of these various ailments and conditions… This change and alteration is required by the condition of the patient and is an evident proof of the skill of the physician.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 20

An evident proof of the skill of the physician. The Master does not merely defend the change; He makes the change into evidence. A religion whose social ordinances never altered across four thousand years would, on this reading, be evidence of inattention.

But the argument only works because a prior distinction is in place, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states it with legal precision in His commentary on the eleventh chapter of Revelation. The religion of God has two parts. The first “suffers neither change nor alteration: It is the Holy of Holies, which constitutes the essence of the religion of Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Christ, Muḥammad, the Báb, and Bahá’u’lláh… It is faith, knowledge, certitude, justice, piety, high-mindedness, trustworthiness, love of God, and charity.” The second is different in kind:

“The second part of the religion of God, which pertains to the material world and which concerns such things as fasting; prayer; worship; marriage; divorce; manumission; legal rulings; transactions; and penalties and punishments for murder, assault, theft, and injury, is changed and altered in every prophetic Dispensation and may be abrogated—for policies, transactions, punishments, and other laws are bound to change according to the exigencies of the time.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 11

Note what is on the immutable list: not doctrines but qualities — trustworthiness, justice, piety, charity. Nothing that changes between Dispensations touches them, which is why truthfulness is not a Bahá’í law in the sense that the Fast is a Bahá’í law; it is a feature of the Holy of Holies, and no Manifestation has ever relaxed it. This is also the reason a believer can read the Torah’s penal code as divinely given and simultaneously as unenforceable. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the point without hedging in The Promulgation of Universal Peace: “Moses lived in the wilderness of Sinai where crime necessitated direct punishment. There were no penitentiaries or penalties of imprisonment. Therefore, according to the exigency of the time and place it was a law of God that an eye should be given for an eye and a tooth for a tooth. It would not be practicable to enforce this law at the present time.” It was a law of God. It is not practicable now. Both statements stand.

The same talks name the failure mode. The Jewish leadership rejected Christ, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says, on the ground that “we see this person abrogating the commands of the Pentateuch, disturbing our blessed Sabbath and abolishing the law of divorce” — and He identifies the cause of the error precisely: “blind and tenacious adherence to imitations of ancient forms and teachings.” Not devotion to the Torah; imitation of its forms. The distinction is the one this whole article turns on, and it recurs in every era.

The Qiblih: what a change of direction actually does to a community

Doctrine tells you why guidance changes. It does not tell you what happens in the room when it does. For that, the corpus offers a single case treated at length, and Bahá’u’lláh chose it deliberately: the change of the Qiblih.

The Kitáb-i-Íqán recounts it as history and then as diagnosis. Muḥammad, having fled to Yathrib, continued to pray towards Jerusalem until the direction was changed mid-prayer:

“On a subsequent day, when the Prophet, together with His companions, was offering the noontide prayer, and had already performed two of the prescribed Rak‘ats, the Voice of Gabriel was heard again: ‘Turn Thou Thy face towards the sacred Mosque.’ In the midst of that same prayer, Muḥammad suddenly turned His face away from Jerusalem and faced the Ka‘bih. Whereupon, a profound dismay seized suddenly the companions of the Prophet. Their faith was shaken severely. So great was their alarm, that many of them, discontinuing their prayer, apostatized their faith.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán

Two rak‘ats towards one city and two towards another. It is worth pausing on how small the change was and how large the casualty list. Nothing doctrinal moved; a compass bearing moved. And people who had followed the Prophet through persecution walked out of the prayer.

Bahá’u’lláh’s commentary is what makes the passage load-bearing. He removes any suggestion that the earlier direction was defective: “He, the ideal King, could easily have left the Qiblih unchanged, and could have caused Jerusalem to remain the Point of Adoration unto His Dispensation, thereby withholding not from that holy city the distinction of acceptance which had been conferred upon it.” Jerusalem held a distinction of acceptance, conferred by God, unrevoked in dignity. The change was not a correction. And then the purpose: “such things as throw consternation into the hearts of all men come to pass only that each soul may be tested by the touchstone of God, that the true may be known and distinguished from the false.”

The draw-out is uncomfortable and should be stated plainly. In this account, a change of direction is not an unfortunate side-effect of guidance that has to be managed; it is one of guidance’s instruments. It functions as an assay. What it separates is not the obedient from the disobedient but those whose attachment was to the Object from those whose attachment had migrated to the form — and the migration is invisible until the form moves. Any believer today who has watched a familiar activity, vocabulary, or committee structure retired by a new Plan message, and has felt something that was not merely inconvenience, has been handed a small edition of the same instrument.

There is a further turn. In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas Bahá’u’lláh addresses the people of the Bayán and quotes a provision the Báb had already made:

“He said: ‘The Qiblih is indeed He Whom God will make manifest; whenever He moveth, it moveth, until He shall come to rest.’” — the Báb, quoted by Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶137

A mobile Point of Adoration, legislated in advance to move. The Bábí community was given, as law, the expectation that its own law would be relocated. Whether a Bábí experienced Bahá’u’lláh’s Revelation as fulfilment or as demolition depended entirely on whether he had taken that provision seriously — which is to say, on whether he had understood the level at which his own law operated.

The Báb wrote a book designed to be superseded

The Bayán is the extreme case, and it settles the question of whether Bahá’í supersession implies retrospective disparagement. It does not, because the Báb Himself built supersession into His own legislation and warned His followers against loyalty to it.

The editorial letters written on Shoghi Effendi’s behalf, reproduced in the introduction to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, state the legal position: “The Báb states that His laws are provisional and depend upon the acceptance of the future Manifestation. This is why in the Book of Aqdas Bahá’u’lláh sanctions some of the laws found in the Bayán, modifies others and sets aside many.” Sanctioned, modified, set aside — three different fates for three different provisions, all decided by the later Manifestation, all anticipated by the earlier.

The Báb’s own language to the people of the Bayán is more startling than any commentary on it:

“At the time of the manifestation of Him Whom God shall make manifest everyone should be well trained in the teachings of the Bayán, so that none of the followers may outwardly cling to the Bayán and thus forfeit their allegiance unto Him. If anyone does so, the verdict of ’disbeliever in God’ shall be passed upon him.” — the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, Persian Bayán

Study the Book thoroughly — so that you will not cling to it. And elsewhere in the same excerpts, the point is pushed to its limit: “wert thou to perform thy deeds for the sake of the Point of the Bayán, they would be regarded as performed for one other than God, inasmuch as on that Day the Point of the Bayán is none other than Him Whom God shall make manifest.” Fidelity to the letter of the earlier revelation, at the hour of the later one, is reclassified as infidelity.

The specific abrogations are documented in the Notes to the Aqdas, and their variety is instructive. Bahá’u’lláh “abrogates this specific law of the Bayán” requiring the destruction of all books except those written in vindication of the Cause. He abrogates the Bayán’s “payment of a fine in reparation for causing sadness to one’s neighbor.” He sets aside the provision that objects unparalleled of their kind be rendered to Him Whom God shall make manifest. He confirms the Báb’s own abrogation of the Islamic condemnation of gold and silver utensils, and of the prohibition on men wearing silk — so that in one case He upholds a Bábí repeal and in another He repeals a Bábí law. Meanwhile the Bayán’s prohibition of marriage with a non-Bábí, and its bar on property passing to a non-Bábí spouse, had already been “explicitly held in abeyance by the Báb” and were annulled by Bahá’u’lláh before they could take effect at all.

And yet the Guardian’s instruction on how to hold the two Dispensations together is emphatic in the other direction: “The Faith of the Báb should not be divorced from that of Bahá’u’lláh. Though the teachings of the Bayán have been abrogated and superseded by the laws of the Aqdas, yet due to the fact that the Báb considered Himself as the Forerunner of Bahá’u’lláh, we would regard His Dispensation together with that of Bahá’u’lláh as forming one entity.” Abrogated and undivorced. That sentence is the whole thesis of this article in miniature.

Abrogation is not repudiation — and the apparent contradiction has a connecting concept

Here is a genuine difficulty, and the honest way through it is the method the authority framework prescribes: where two authoritative statements appear to conflict, the missing item is a connecting concept, not a correct party.

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas contains, by the House of Justice’s own summary of its contents, “the abrogation of specific laws and ordinances of previous Dispensations” as one of four categories of its legislation. The Book annuls congregational prayer save for the dead; annuls the Prayer of the Signs; abrogates the Islamic law on remarriage with a divorced wife; lifts the restrictions on the surface for prostration; relieves the restrictions on clothing and the trim of the beard. The Glad-Tidings open by announcing “that the law of holy war hath been blotted out from the Book.”

And Shoghi Effendi writes, in “The Golden Age of the Cause of Bahá’u’lláh” (21 March 1932):

“The Revelation, of which Bahá’u’lláh is the source and center, abrogates none of the religions that have preceded it, nor does it attempt, in the slightest degree, to distort their features or to belittle their value. It disclaims any intention of dwarfing any of the Prophets of the past, or of whittling down the eternal verity of their teachings.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh

Both are true, and the connecting concept is the two-part structure of religion. Abrogation operates on ordinances, never on religions and never on their Authors. What the Aqdas retires is a set of prescriptions; what the Guardian says is not retired is the Faith those prescriptions served, the station of its Founder, and the eternal verity of its teaching. The same paragraph names the primary purpose as the opposite of demolition: “to enable every adherent of these Faiths to obtain a fuller understanding of the religion with which he stands identified.”

Bahá’u’lláh Himself supplies the missing sentence — the one that keeps the superseded law honourable — at the close of the Bishárát:

“In former religions such ordinances as holy war, destruction of books, the ban on association and companionship with other peoples or on reading certain books had been laid down and affirmed according to the exigencies of the time; however, in this mighty Revelation, in this momentous Announcement, the manifold bestowals and favors of God have overshadowed all men…” — Bahá’u’lláh, Bishárát (Glad-Tidings)

Laid down and affirmed according to the exigencies of the time. No hedging, no embarrassment, no suggestion that the earlier communities had misheard. This is the sentence a Bahá’í should have ready whenever the question arises — about the Torah’s penalties, the Bayán’s severities, or a Bahá’í institutional arrangement retired in 1997.

Two guard-rails complete the doctrine and both point away from triumphalism. The first is the relativity of religious truth. Shoghi Effendi states it as the bedrock: “religious truth is not absolute but relative… Divine Revelation is progressive, not final,” and warns that to contend that revelation has ended “would indeed be nothing less than sheer blasphemy.” In The Dispensation of Bahá’u’lláh (8 February 1934) he applies the same standard to the Bahá’í Revelation itself: it “emphatically repudiates the claim to be regarded as the final revelation of God’s will and purpose for mankind,” and to hold otherwise “would be tantamount to a betrayal of its cause.”

The second guard-rail is a floor, not a ceiling. Bahá’u’lláh legislates in the Aqdas that “whoso layeth claim to a Revelation direct from God, ere the expiration of a full thousand years, such a man is assuredly a lying impostor” (¶37). Supersession between Dispensations is therefore real, expected, and rate-limited. A letter written on the House of Justice’s behalf on 3 January 1982 draws exactly the conclusion a reader should take away, and does so by contrast with the alternatives:

“The practice in the past in these two religions, and also to a great extent in Islám, has been to assume that the Revelation given by the Founder was the final, perfect revelation of God’s Will to mankind, and all subsequent elucidation and legislation has been interpretative… The Bahá’í premises are quite different. Although the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh is accepted as the Word of God and His Law as the Law of God, it is understood from the outset that Revelation is progressive, and that the Law, although the Will of God for this Age, will undoubtedly be changed by the next Manifestation of God.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 3 January 1982 (universal-house-of-justice-compilation)

Understood from the outset. A Bahá’í holds the Aqdas as the Law of God and simultaneously expects it to be changed — not eroded by reinterpretation, but changed openly by a future Manifestation after a thousand years at minimum. That combination is unusual enough to be worth naming: it is the reason the Faith has no doctrinal machinery for quietly explaining its own laws away, and no need for any.

Inside the Bahá’í order: three kinds of change, and only one is repeal

Most of what a believer experiences as “the guidance changing” is not repeal at all. Distinguishing the three cases is the single most useful analytical move available, and each has a documented, dated trail.

1. Progressive application — the law does not change; its bindingness does

The Aqdas was revealed around 1873 and its laws were never intended to fall on the whole world at once. Bahá’u’lláh Himself counsels graduated application: “Indeed, the laws of God are like unto the ocean and the children of men as fish, did they but know it. However, in observing them one must exercise tact and wisdom… One must guide mankind to the ocean of true understanding in a spirit of love and tolerance.” The 1935 letter on the Guardian’s behalf sorts the laws into those “universally and vitally applicable at the present time” and those “formulated in anticipation of a state of society destined to emerge from the chaotic conditions that prevail today.”

When the authorised English translation was published in 1992, the House of Justice was explicit that publication changed nothing: “The number of laws binding on Bahá’ís is not increased by the publication of this translation. When it is deemed timely, the Bahá’í community will be advised which additional laws are binding upon believers.” Timely is the operative word, and the trail of its use is unusually legible:

The 28 December 1999 letter opens by naming its own method: “In the course of the Four Year Plan, we have been reviewing those laws of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas which are not yet universally applied, in order to determine which of them it would be timely to implement now.” And it reports the change as a completion rather than a reversal: “a number of aspects of the law, such as those concerning ablutions, traveling and the compensation for prayers missed, remained to be made universally applicable. This step is now taken.” Nobody who had been observing the shorter form was in error; the scope of obligation moved. The devotional consequences are worked out in daily-practices.

Nothing in this category is repeal. The law was Bahá’u’lláh’s throughout; only the community’s readiness changed — the theme developed in readiness-and-maturity.

2. Elucidation and supplementary legislation — filling a gap the Text left open

The 2001 list contained a line that reads, in retrospect, like a promissory note: the Bahá’í festivals “are being celebrated by the Western friends on their anniversaries in the Gregorian calendar until such time as the Universal House of Justice deems it desirable to pass supplementary legislation necessary for the full implementation of the Badí‘ calendar.” Thirteen years later, that legislation arrived, and the letter that carried it is the finest available model of how to make a change that everyone will feel.

The 10 July 2014 message first locates itself in the tradition rather than announcing a break: “In keeping with the principle governing the gradual unfoldment and progressive application of the Teachings, the provisions of the Badí‘ calendar have been set forth over time.” It then narrates the chain of custody — the Báb introduced the calendar, Bahá’u’lláh provided clarifications, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá elucidated aspects, Shoghi Effendi directed its adoption in the West — and names why the matter reached the House at all: “When responding to questions concerning the calendar, both ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi left these matters to the Universal House of Justice.” The gap was left deliberately, by the interpretive tier, for the legislative tier to close.

Three decisions follow: Ṭihrán as the standard for computing the vernal equinox; the Twin Birthdays fixed to the first and second days after the eighth new moon following Naw-Rúz; the remaining Holy Days set within the Badí‘ months. Then the sentences that do the pastoral work:

“Unless specifically abrogated by these new provisions, previous guidance and clarifications pertaining to the calendar and the observance of the Nineteen Day Feast and Holy Days remain binding, such as the beginning of the day at sunset, the suspension of work, and the hours at which certain Holy Days are commemorated. In future, a change in circumstances may well require additional measures.” — The Universal House of Justice, 10 July 2014 (muhj-2001-2022)

Four moves in two sentences: state the default (everything stands), name the exception (only what is specifically abrogated), enumerate examples so nobody has to guess, and announce in advance that this too may change. The House also acknowledged the cost directly — “Bahá’ís of both East and West will find some elements of the calendar to be different from those to which they have been accustomed” — and disclosed a judgement call rather than burying it: “we have decided to set aside certain discrepancies in the historical record.” A Local Assembly announcing a change of its own practice could do worse than copy the structure sentence for sentence.

3. Repeal and replacement of the House’s own enactments

This is the narrow category the Constitution’s clause actually names, and it is used more often than the Faith’s reputation for stability suggests — usually on administrative instruments rather than on anything a believer would call a law.

A plain example: on 29 September 2000, after consultation with the International Teaching Centre, the House of Justice approved revised statements of the purpose and responsibilities of the Continental Pioneer Committees and instructed that “these documents now supersede the ones dated January 1990.” Ten-year-old institutional documents, replaced, named, dated. Another: the Codification of the Law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh was “prepared by the Research Department of the Bahá’í World Centre in 1987, and revised in 2007, at the request of the Universal House of Justice” — a codification of a revealed law, itself revised twenty years on to incorporate later guidance.

The covering guidance on Ḥuqúqu’lláh contains the sentence that should be attached to any codification anywhere in the Bahá’í world:

“It should be emphasized, however, that the friends should not attempt to read into it an element of rigidity or total comprehensiveness. The questions put to Bahá’u’lláh, the Master and Shoghi Effendi were from friends residing in places and times with infinitely simpler economic systems and relationships than those which obtain today… The subject will undoubtedly occupy the House of Justice in evolving legislation, as necessary, for a long time to come.” — The Universal House of Justice (muhj-2001-2022; see HuququllahComputation)

The House also flags certain of its own policies as temporary by design. Writing on its behalf about pre-publication review, the Secretariat noted “the important fact that the process of review in the Bahá’í Faith is temporary, being limited to this stage of its development when books published by Bahá’ís could seriously mislead the public if they too gravely distort its message” (issues-related-study-bahai-faith). A policy that announces its own contingency at the moment of enactment is doing something worth imitating: it pre-empts the misfiling that would otherwise turn its eventual retirement into a scandal.

Institutions that are extended, not replaced

The most consequential Bahá’í changes of direction have been institutional, and the pattern in each is the same: a function is preserved while its vessel changes.

The hardest case came first. In 1963 the newly elected House of Justice resolved that “there is no way to appoint or to legislate to make it possible to appoint a second Guardian to succeed Shoghi Effendi.” In November 1964, after fourteen days of consultation with the Hands of the Cause in the Holy Land, it reported a parallel finding: “There is no way to appoint, or to legislate to make it possible to appoint, Hands of the Cause of God.” The institution that had preserved the Faith through the interregnum could not be replenished.

What followed was not abolition but extension. The Riḍván 1964 World Centre goals had been framed as “development of the Institution of the Hands of the Cause of God, in consultation with the body of the Hands of the Cause, with a view to the extension into the future of its appointed functions of protection and propagation” — the language of continuity written into the goal before the mechanism existed. On 21 June 1968 the House cabled its decision to establish eleven Continental Boards of Counsellors, and the cable’s own framing is the doctrine: the step “ensures extension future appointed functions their institution.” The letter of 24 June 1968 set out the constraints the House had worked within, including that “any such institution must grow and operate in harmony with the principles governing the functioning of the Institution of the Hands of the Cause of God,” and that the new body should come into being soon enough “to take full advantage of the opportunity of having the Hands themselves assist in launching and guiding the new procedures.”

That last clause is the pastoral genius of the decision. The people whose institutional role was being superseded were asked to launch its successor. Nobody was retired into irrelevance; the Hands “were then freed of responsibility for directing the work of Auxiliary Board members and were all given worldwide responsibilities.” Twenty-five years later, when the Four Year Plan was launched, the community “could still count three Hands of the Cause of God in its front ranks.”

The general principle received its clearest statement in the letter of 30 May 1997 establishing Regional Bahá’í Councils:

“The institutions of the Administrative Order of Bahá’u’lláh, rooted in the provisions of His Revelation, have emerged gradually and organically, as the Bahá’í community has grown through the power of the divine impulse imparted to humankind in this age. The characteristics and functions of each of these institutions have evolved, and are still evolving, as are the relationships between them.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 May 1997 (muhj-1986-2001)

And immediately, the boundary: the evolution of these institutions, “while following many variants to meet changing conditions in different times and places, should strictly follow the essential principles of Bahá’í administration which have been laid down in the Sacred Text and in the interpretations provided by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and the Guardian.” Variants in the arrangements; strictness in the principles. This is the immutability-and-elasticity pairing again, one level down, and it is the reason a believer can regard a new institutional layer as a development rather than an innovation. The full institutional map is in bahai-institutions-architecture; how authority is distributed across it is treated in delegation-and-authority, and the distinctive non-commanding authority of the appointed arm in protection-without-command.

Method is not law: how the Plans changed direction without disowning the past

The change of direction that living Bahá’ís have actually lived through is the one that began in 1996, and it is the best available case study in metabolising a shift without falsifying what preceded it.

The Riḍván 1996 message, launching the Four Year Plan, asked the Bahá’í world to build a network of training institutes “on a scale never before attempted.” The sentence that made this a change of direction rather than an addition is a model of how to retire an approach:

“The endeavor of individuals to conduct study classes in their homes, the sponsorship by the institutions of occasional courses of instruction, and the informal activities of the community, though important, are not adequate for the education and training of a rapidly expanding community.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 1996 (muhj-1986-2001)

Though important, are not adequate. Two clauses, doing two jobs: the first refuses to disparage twenty years of home study classes; the second states without softening that they will not carry what is coming. This is the exact register that makes supersession bearable, and it is available to any Assembly, coordinator or committee announcing a change.

Century of Light, prepared under the House of Justice’s supervision and released at Naw-Rúz 158 B.E. (2001), goes further and gives the honest retrospective. Of the mass-teaching decades it says the “burst of enrolments brought with it, however, equally great problems,” that “the high expectations of the early years were in some respects quite unrealistic,” and that the easy early successes “did not, by themselves, build a Bahá’í community life that could meet the needs of its new members and be self-generating.” That is as candid an assessment as the Bahá’í literature contains. And then, in the same passage, the sentence that keeps the earlier era honoured:

“Had it not been for the painstaking, always difficult and often frustrating consolidation work pursued during these years, however, the subsequent strategy of systematizing the promotion of entry by troops would have had very little with which to work.” — Century of Light, 2001

The earlier approach was not a detour that the community eventually corrected. It was the apparatus by which the community learned what it needed to know in order to change. “The pressure of these urgent and interlocking challenges launched the Bahá’í world on a learning process that has proved to be as important as the expansion itself.” Read that way, the 1996 turn is not a repudiation of the 1960s and 1970s; it is their yield.

Twenty-five years on, the Riḍván 2021 message closed the series with an explicit accounting. It described how “various principles, concepts and strategies of universal relevance to the growth process began to crystallize into a framework for action that could evolve to accommodate new elements” — and then supplied the caution that prevents the framework from calcifying into the next thing that will have to be superseded:

“But a framework is not a formula. By taking into account the various elements of the framework when assessing the reality of a cluster, a locality, or simply a neighbourhood, a pattern of activity could be developed that drew on what the rest of the Bahá’í world was learning while still being a response to the particulars of that place.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

The same message names error where error genuinely occurred, and locates it correctly — in execution, not in direction: “A commitment to learning also meant being prepared to make mistakes—and sometimes, of course, mistakes brought discomfort. Unsurprisingly, new methods and approaches were handled inexpertly at first because of a lack of experience; on occasion, a newly acquired capacity of one kind was lost as a community became absorbed in developing another.” And it draws the comparison that dignifies both eras rather than ranking them: “while souls who arose to open a new locality in 1996 lacked nothing for courage, faith and devotion, today their counterparts everywhere combine those same qualities with knowledge, insights and skills that are the accumulation of twenty-five years of effort.”

Then the horizon widened again. The message of 30 December 2021 to the Counsellors announced that the new series of Plans “will last a full twenty-five years… and conclude at Riḍván 2046,” focused on a single aim — “the release of the society-building power of the Faith in ever-greater measures” — and stated that by its conclusion “the Bahá’í community will need to have acquired capacities that can scarcely be glimpsed at present.” A community told in advance that it cannot yet imagine the capacities it will need has been told, in advance, that its present methods will be superseded. That is the most useful inoculation available, and it was administered deliberately. The arc of the Plans themselves is catalogued in bahai-plans; the mechanics of the current framework in cluster-development-framework; the epistemology in learning-in-action and the individual’s part in initiative-and-plan.

What a community owes a superseded direction

The counsel that follows is mine, drawn from the material above and addressed to the reader who will actually have to say something when the guidance moves — at a Feast, in an Assembly meeting, or to a friend who has been serving one way for a decade and has just been asked to serve another.

Ask the level question before the content question. Is this the revealed Text, an interpretation of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá or the Guardian, an enactment of the House of Justice, an elucidation, a national or regional administrative decision, or a method that a Plan message has commended? Only the third and below can be repealed at all, and only the fifth and sixth change often. Half of the distress around a change of direction comes from a believer having filed a method at the level of law. The 3 January 1982 letter describes the Faith’s deliberate reticence here: “The tendency in the Bahá’í Dispensation, from the time of Bahá’u’lláh Himself, has been to clarify the governing principles, to make binding pronouncements on details which are considered essential, but to leave a wide area to the conscience of the individual.” A wide area is not an oversight; it is designed room.

State what has changed in conditions, not what was wrong before. This is the whole rhetorical technique of the corpus, and it is learnable. Bahá’u’lláh: “laid down and affirmed according to the exigencies of the time.” The House of Justice in 1999: “in order to determine which of them it would be timely to implement now.” The House in 1996: “though important, are not adequate.” Nowhere is the earlier arrangement described as an error, and nowhere is the change described as optional. Institutions that skip the first half of this move — announcing the new without honouring the old — generate resistance they did not need to generate.

Name explicitly what remains binding. The 10 July 2014 letter’s default clause — unless specifically abrogated by these new provisions, previous guidance… remain binding — is worth adopting verbatim as a habit of institutional communication. A change announced without a scope statement invites everyone to guess at its edges, and the guesses will differ, and the differences will be argued about.

Announce contingency in advance where it is genuinely present. “In future, a change in circumstances may well require additional measures.” “The process of review in the Bahá’í Faith is temporary.” “The subject will undoubtedly occupy the House of Justice in evolving legislation… for a long time to come.” Guidance that discloses its own provisionality at the moment of issue cannot later be experienced as a betrayal.

Refuse the retrospective disparagement, in both directions. The temperament that says “we wasted twenty years on that” and the temperament that says “the old way was better” are the same error wearing opposite clothes: both treat a change of level as a verdict on quality. Century of Light’s account of the mass-teaching decades — unrealistic expectations and indispensable groundwork — is the model of holding both.

Expect the change to be an assay, and be gentle about what it exposes. The Íqán is unsentimental: many companions of the Prophet left the prayer. Some attachment to form is inevitable and human; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s diagnosis of the deeper failure is “blind and tenacious adherence to imitations of ancient forms.” The pastoral task when a familiar activity is retired is not to shame the attachment but to reconnect the person to what the form was for. That is a conversation, not an announcement — which is one reason consultation is the instrument the Faith gives for exactly this moment.

And keep the humility clause in view. Writing to an individual believer on 7 December 1969, the House of Justice ended a set of elucidations on the relationships among the institutions with this: “we must all remember that we stand too close to the beginnings of the System ordained by Bahá’u’lláh to be able fully to understand its potentialities or the inter-relationships of its component parts.” It quoted a letter written on Shoghi Effendi’s behalf on 25 March 1930: the contents of the Master’s Will “needs at least a century of actual working before the treasures of wisdom hidden in it can be revealed.” On that reckoning the Will and Testament passed its first century in 2021. We are early. Guidance will keep evolving because the system is young, not because it is unsettled.

The contemporary field: what a reversal costs elsewhere, and why the difference matters

The corpus supplies the principles. It cannot describe how a change of authoritative guidance actually lands in 2026, when believers metabolise institutional messages inside the same information environment that metabolises everything else. Here external evidence is worth weighing — strictly as description of the field, never as authority for a Bahá’í principle.

The first thing worth knowing is that in the surrounding culture, a change of guidance carries a specific default meaning: the earlier guidance was wrong. That is not a prejudice; it is an accurate description of how most authoritative guidance actually works. In clinical medicine the phenomenon has a name — medical reversal — and a measured frequency. Herrera-Perez and colleagues, reviewing 3,017 randomised controlled trials published in JAMA and The Lancet (2003–2017) and the New England Journal of Medicine (2011–2017), identified 396 reversals, amounting to 6% of all original articles and 13% of all randomised trials (eLife, 11 June 2019). Medications were the most common intervention reversed, at 33%. In that domain, when the guidance changes, the previous standard of care is retrospectively judged to have been mistaken, and patients were treated under it.

The second thing worth knowing is that changes of guidance are expensive in trust, and the cost is measurable. Kreps and Kriner, in five survey experiments with more than 6,000 American adults conducted in May and June 2020 (Science Advances, 25 September 2020), found that exposure to elite criticism of COVID-19 projection models reduced support for using such models to guide reopening policy by 13 percentage points, with spillover to trust in science generally — and that criticism from politically aligned elites did more damage than criticism from opponents. More recently, Dries, McDowell, Schneider and Rebitschek ran two preregistered experiments (n = 601 and n = 1,001; PNAS Nexus, 5 March 2025) and found a crossover effect: communicating uncertainty lowered trust among those whose prior beliefs the evidence already confirmed, and raised it among those whose priors it contradicted. The people most disturbed by an acknowledgement of provisionality are the ones who were already agreeing with you.

Both findings should be held loosely — they are studies of Western, largely American publics, on political and scientific rather than religious authority, and none of them tests a Bahá’í proposition. But the operational lesson survives translation, and it cuts against a comfortable assumption. The Bahá’í grammar of supersession differs in kind from medical reversal — but the trust cost still arrives if a community narrates its own changes in the reversal register. If a coordinator explains a new approach by saying “we used to do it wrong,” the listener’s culture supplies the rest: a body that was wrong before may be wrong now. The Writings offer a different sentence, and it is not spin — it is the accurate description: the conditions changed, and the guidance was fitted to the conditions.

For a reader with technical skills, the highest-leverage contribution here is unglamorous and specific. The Bahá’í world’s guidance is superbly versioned at the top and almost unversioned by the time it reaches a cluster. The House of Justice dates everything, names what it supersedes, and states what remains binding — the 8 February 2001 letter literally exists to replace a list circulated in May 1993; the 2014 calendar letter enumerates what survives it. But the derived artefacts most friends actually use — slide decks, training summaries, locally produced handouts, community WhatsApp forwards, and increasingly AI-generated answers drawing on scraped corpora — typically carry no date, no issuing level, and no supersession chain. The failure mode is predictable: guidance retired in 2014 circulates in a 2026 group chat with the same apparent authority as guidance issued last Riḍván.

What is worth building is therefore not another archive of texts. It is a level-tagged, date-stamped, supersession-aware layer over the guidance: every statement carrying who issued it, when, at what level of authority, and what it replaces or is replaced by. A system that can answer “is this still current, and at what level was it ever binding?” would remove most of the confusion this article has been describing — and it would do so without adjudicating a single doctrinal question, because the House of Justice has already published the answers in its own letters. For the non-technical reader the equivalent contribution is a habit: when quoting guidance, give the date and the source type. “A letter of 10 July 2014” rather than “the House of Justice says.” It costs four words and it prevents the misfiling that everything else follows from.

Open questions / honest limits

  • Where exactly the boundary of “explicitly revealed” runs is not, in every case, settled in advance. The rule is clear — the House of Justice may not change what Bahá’u’lláh has explicitly revealed — but which provisions count as explicit, and which are matters of application left open, is itself a judgement the House makes. This page does not attempt to adjudicate any particular case.
  • The corpus consulted here does not record subsequent changes to the review policy. The Secretariat’s statement that pre-publication review is “temporary, being limited to this stage of its development” is quoted from a compilation of letters written in the 1990s. Whether and how that policy has since been modified is not documented in the sources available to this page, and readers should not infer either continuity or change from its inclusion.
  • “Timeliness” is asserted by the sources, not explained by them. The Writings and House letters state that guidance changes when conditions and capacity warrant it; they do not offer criteria by which an outside observer could predict a change, and this page has not invented any. The determination is the House’s, and it is made in its own sphere.
  • The Riḍván 2021 retrospective is the community’s account of itself. It is authoritative guidance, not an independent historical audit, and Century of Light was prepared under the House of Justice’s supervision rather than as detached historiography. Both are cited here as what they are.
  • The distinction between essential and accidental ordinances does not, by itself, sort every case. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s two-part division is clear at the poles — trustworthiness on one side, penalties for theft on the other — and less clear in the middle, where questions of personal status and community practice sit. Individual believers’ deductions carry no authority here; the Aqdas introduction quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá to that exact effect.
  • The external research will age and does not transfer cleanly. The medical-reversal figures cover three journals and specific date ranges; the trust experiments sample American adults in particular political moments; none studies religious authority. They are cited as description of the surrounding field and would be misused as evidence about Bahá’í institutions.
  • This page does not treat covenant-breaking, the claims of sects that arose around the succession, or the interpretive canon’s closure in 1957 beyond what the argument required. Those belong to covenant-axis and bahai-authority-framework.
