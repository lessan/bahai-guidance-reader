Unbridled Nationalism and the Wider Loyalty

Last updated 8 October 2026

The third of the seven issues singled out in The Promise of World Peace (Universal House of Justice, October 1985), read against the Writings, the Guardian and the House of Justice’s later letters.

The paragraph

Part II of The Promise of World Peace names seven issues that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” The third reads in full:

“Unbridled nationalism, as distinguished from a sane and legitimate patriotism, must give way to a wider loyalty, to the love of humanity as a whole. Bahá’u’lláh’s statement is: ‘The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.’ The concept of world citizenship is a direct result of the contraction of the world into a single neighborhood through scientific advances and of the indisputable interdependence of nations. Love of all the world’s peoples does not exclude love of one’s country. The advantage of the part in a world society is best served by promoting the advantage of the whole. Current international activities in various fields which nurture mutual affection and a sense of solidarity among peoples need greatly to be increased.” — Universal House of Justice, October 1985 (promise-of-world-peace)

The paragraph has six sentences, and they do six different jobs. The first draws a distinction and states a demand: one kind of attachment to the nation must give way, and another kind is called “sane and legitimate” and left standing. The second gives the scriptural ground. The third gives the historical ground: world citizenship follows from facts, the shrinking of distances and the dependence of nations on one another. The fourth answers the obvious objection, that loving humanity means ceasing to love one’s country. The fifth gives the reason the two loves do not compete: the part does best when the whole does well. The sixth turns to practice and asks for more of what already builds affection between peoples.

The paragraph does not condemn the nation, national feeling or national loyalty. What it condemns is named by its adjective. Unbridled means without restraint: a national attachment that recognises no claim above itself. Everything else the sources say on this subject elaborates that one distinction.

Two existing pages touch the same ground. unity-in-diversity treats Shoghi Effendi’s “watchword” passage from The Goal of a New World Order as a statement about how difference is held together in structure; OnenessOfHumanity treats “The earth is but one country” as the sociological side of the oneness of humanity. This page takes up the question neither of them addresses: where the line falls between a love of country the Faith upholds and a nationalism it calls an idol, and why the House of Justice placed that line among the conditions of peace.

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The issue closest to the “paralysis of will”

Part II opens by setting aside partial measures. Banning nuclear weapons, poison gas and germ warfare “will not remove the root causes of war.” The House of Justice then names the obstacle:

“There is, however, a paralysis of will; and it is this that must be carefully examined and resolutely dealt with. This paralysis is rooted, as we have stated, in a deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind, which has led to the reluctance to entertain the possibility of subordinating national self-interest to the requirements of world order, and in an unwillingness to face courageously the far-reaching implications of establishing a united world authority.” — The Promise of World Peace, Part II

Of the seven issues, nationalism is the one that names the paralysis most directly. Racism, extremes of wealth and poverty, religious strife, the denial of women’s equality, ignorance and the lack of a common language all feed the conviction that humans are incorrigibly quarrelsome. Unbridled nationalism is the form that conviction takes in politics: a refusal to subordinate “national self-interest to the requirements of world order.” The paragraph on nationalism is therefore the point at which Part II’s diagnosis and its list of issues meet.

After the seven issues, the statement adds two points that bear “in all these issues.” The second matters most here:

“The other point is that the primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism. For, in essence, peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude, and it is chiefly in evoking this attitude that the possibility of enduring solutions can be found.” — The Promise of World Peace, Part II

Read with this, the nationalism paragraph is about a loyalty and a love, which are inner states, before it is about treaties and institutions. The wider loyalty is the attitude; the international machinery of Part III comes after it.

What the other three parts say about the nation

The statement returns to the nation in every part, and the treatment is consistent.

  • Introduction. Among the “favorable signs” the House of Justice lists “the achievement since the Second World War of independence by the majority of all the nations on earth, indicating the completion of the process of nation building.” National independence is counted as progress. The nation is a stage that has been completed, not an error.
  • Part I. The House of Justice indicts the twentieth century’s “man-made ideologies” that, instead of embracing the oneness of mankind, “have tended to deify the state, to subordinate the rest of mankind to one nation, race or class.” It asks: “Where are the breakthroughs into new realms of cultural achievement produced by the aggrandizement of this race, of that nation or of a particular class?” Here nationalism appears as one of the “substitute faiths.”
  • Part III. World order “can be founded only on an unshakable consciousness of the oneness of mankind,” which “requires abandonment of prejudice—prejudice of every kind—race, class, color, creed, nation, sex, degree of material civilization, everything which enables people to consider themselves superior to others.” National prejudice is defined by its effect: it lets one people consider itself superior. Part III then quotes Shoghi Effendi twice. The first passage (1931) says the Bahá’í world order seeks “neither to stifle the flame of a sane and intelligent patriotism in men’s hearts, nor to abolish the system of national autonomy.” The second foresees “the inevitable curtailment of unfettered national sovereignty” and “a world community in which the fury of a capricious and militant nationalism will have been transmuted into an abiding consciousness of world citizenship.” Part III also quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s proposal for a “Most Great Covenant” in which “the limits and frontiers of each and every nation should be clearly fixed.”
  • Part IV. The statement quotes Shoghi Effendi again: “Unity of family, of tribe, of city-state, and nation have been successively attempted and fully established. World unity is the goal towards which a harassed humanity is striving. Nation-building has come to an end. The anarchy inherent in state sovereignty is moving towards a climax. A world, growing to maturity, must abandon this fetish.”

Taken together: the nation is an achieved stage of human organisation, to be kept and given a fixed and agreed place in a larger order. What must be abandoned is treating the nation as the highest object of loyalty: deifying the state, holding sovereignty as unfettered, and letting national identity justify contempt for others.

The paragraph’s sources in the Guardian’s writings

Almost every phrase in the 1985 paragraph has a source in three works by Shoghi Effendi: The Goal of a New World Order (28 November 1931) and The Unfoldment of World Civilization (11 March 1936), both in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, and above all the section of The Promised Day Is Come (28 March 1941) headed “The Wider, Inclusive Loyalty.”

The 1985 paragraph is therefore a compressed restatement, for a general readership, of an interpretation the Guardian had already given. That matters for how it is read: the paragraph’s brevity hides a fully argued position, and the 1941 passage is the place to go for that argument.

The Writings: an old verse and a new one

Bahá’u’lláh’s two sayings

The scriptural source of the distinction is a passage in the Lawḥ-i-Dunyá (Tablet of the World). Bahá’u’lláh sets a saying from the past beside one of His own:

“Of old it hath been revealed: ‘Love of one’s country is an element of the Faith of God.’ The Tongue of Grandeur hath, however, in the day of His manifestation proclaimed: ‘It is not his to boast who loveth his country, but it is his who loveth the world.’ Through the power released by these exalted words He hath lent a fresh impulse and set a new direction to the birds of men’s hearts, and hath obliterated every trace of restriction and limitation from God’s holy Book.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Dunyá, in Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh (tablets-bahaullah); also Gleanings, XLIII (gleanings-writings-bahaullah)

The second saying does not cancel the first. It moves the ground of honour. Loving one’s country stays; what loses its standing is the claim to boast of it. Honour now belongs to love for the world. The Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd gives the form that the House of Justice quotes:

“That one indeed is a man who, today, dedicateth himself to the service of the entire human race. The Great Being saith: Blessed and happy is he that ariseth to promote the best interests of the peoples and kindreds of the earth. In another passage He hath proclaimed: It is not for him to pride himself who loveth his own country, but rather for him who loveth the whole world. The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd, in Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh (tablets-bahaullah)

In The Promised Day Is Come Shoghi Effendi renders the same saying a third way: “Let not a man glory in that he loves his country; let him rather glory in this, that he loves his kind.” The three English verbs (boast, pride himself, glory) all name the same thing. The verse is about where a person locates their sense of worth, not about whether they may love the place they come from.

Love of homeland in Bahá’u’lláh’s own life

Bahá’u’lláh’s love for His own country is on record in the Most Holy Book. He left Ṭihrán in exile on 12 January 1853 (God Passes By). Years later, in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, He addressed the city:

“Let nothing grieve thee, O Land of Ṭá, for God hath chosen thee to be the source of the joy of all mankind.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶91 (kitab-i-aqdas)

The notes to the Aqdas explain that Ṭá is the initial letter of Ṭihrán. The city that banished Him is told it will be “the source of the joy of all mankind.” Love of country and love of humanity are joined in the one sentence, with no tension between them.

The Guardian’s interpretation of the old verse

Shoghi Effendi explains the first saying historically. “Of old”, he writes, refers to the Islamic Dispensation, and Islam “introduced … the conception of the nation as a unit and a vital stage in the organization of human society.” The nation was a necessary stage: “This principle was established and stressed by the Apostle of God, inasmuch as the evolution of human society required it at that time.” (The Promised Day Is Come.)

He then warns against reading Bahá’u’lláh’s new saying as a rejection of the old one:

“A word of warning should, however, be uttered in this connection. The love of one’s country, instilled and stressed by the teaching of Islám, as ‘an element of the Faith of God,’ has not, through this declaration, this clarion-call of Bahá’u’lláh, been either condemned or disparaged. It should not, indeed it cannot, be construed as a repudiation, or regarded in the light of a censure, pronounced against a sane and intelligent patriotism, nor does it seek to undermine the allegiance and loyalty of any individual to his country, nor does it conflict with the legitimate aspirations, rights, and duties of any individual state or nation. All it does imply and proclaim is the insufficiency of patriotism, in view of the fundamental changes effected in the economic life of society and the interdependence of the nations, and as the consequence of the contraction of the world, through the revolution in the means of transportation and communication—conditions that did not and could not exist either in the days of Jesus Christ or of Muḥammad. It calls for a wider loyalty, which should not, and indeed does not, conflict with lesser loyalties. It instills a love which, in view of its scope, must include and not exclude the love of one’s own country. It lays, through this loyalty which it inspires, and this love which it infuses, the only foundation on which the concept of world citizenship can thrive, and the structure of world unification can rest. It does insist, however, on the subordination of national considerations and particularistic interests to the imperative and paramount claims of humanity as a whole, inasmuch as in a world of interdependent nations and peoples the advantage of the part is best to be reached by the advantage of the whole.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come, 28 March 1941 (promised-day-come)

The key word is insufficiency. Patriotism is not wrong; it is no longer enough. It was once the widest loyalty human beings could practically hold. The world has since become one interconnected place, and the widest loyalty must now cover the whole of it. The two loves are related as smaller and larger circles, with the smaller inside the larger. The passage puts the one condition at its end: national considerations are subordinate to the claims of humanity. That condition is the bridle that the 1985 paragraph’s adjective refers to.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: love for Persia, and against “imaginary lines”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s writings and talks contain both halves of the distinction, and the clearest example of each comes from His own life.

A love letter to Persia

In 1875 ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote The Secret of Divine Civilization, an anonymous treatise addressed to His countrymen. The book’s notes record that “the original Persian text written in 1875 carried no author’s name.” Its tone is that of a patriot pleading with a nation he loves:

“O people of Persia! Look into those blossoming pages that tell of another day, a time long past. Read them and wonder; see the great sight. Írán in that day was as the heart of the world; she was the bright torch flaming in the assemblage of mankind.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Secret of Divine Civilization (secret-divine-civilization)

“O people of Persia! The heart is a divine trust; cleanse it from the stain of self-love, adorn it with the coronal of pure intent, until the sacred honor, the abiding greatness of this illustrious nation may shine out like the true morning in an auspicious heaven.” — The Secret of Divine Civilization

This is love of country in the sense the Faith upholds. It takes the form of a call to reform: education, justice, honest government and the end of blind imitation. It does not say that Persians are better than anyone else; it says Persia can recover what it has lost. The same book proposes a “Most Great Covenant” among the nations in which “the limits and frontiers of each and every nation should be clearly fixed” and armaments strictly limited, the passage the House of Justice quotes in Part III of the 1985 statement. One book written for one nation contains both the love of a homeland and the plan for a world order, which shows how little the two conflict.

Patriotic prejudice

Against this stands ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s repeated condemnation of “patriotic prejudice”, which He lists with religious, racial and political prejudice as “destructive of the human edifice.” His fullest written statement is in the first Tablet to the Central Organization for a Durable Peace at The Hague, dated 17 December 1919, a year after the First World War ended:

“As to the patriotic prejudice, this is also due to absolute ignorance, for the surface of the earth is one native land. Every one can live in any spot on the terrestrial globe. Therefore all the world is man’s birthplace. These boundaries and outlets have been devised by man. In the creation, such boundaries and outlets were not assigned. … God has set up no frontier between France and Germany; they are continuous. Yet, in the first centuries, selfish souls, for the promotion of their own interests, have assigned boundaries and outlets and have, day by day, attached more importance to these, until this led to intense enmity, bloodshed and rapacity in subsequent centuries. In the same way this will continue indefinitely, and if this conception of patriotism remains limited within a certain circle, it will be the primary cause of the world’s destruction. No wise and just person will acknowledge these imaginary distinctions.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, First Tablet to The Hague, 17 December 1919 (tablets-hague-abdul-baha)

The tablet continues:

“In short, for a few days we live on this earth and eventually we are buried in it, it is our eternal tomb. Is it worth while that we should engage in bloodshed and tear one another to pieces for this eternal tomb? Nay, far from it, neither is God pleased with such conduct nor would any sane man approve of it.”

The phrase to notice is “if this conception of patriotism remains limited within a certain circle.” The fault ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names is a limit that has hardened. Borders become a reason for enmity when people “attached more importance to these” than to the humanity on both sides.

He spoke in the same terms in his talks. In Paris on 24 October 1911: “Patriotic love is finite; the love of one’s country causing hatred of all others is not perfect love!” (Paris Talks, “The Universal Love”). At Stanford University on 8 October 1912, two years before the First World War, He described how boundary lines “created by despots and conquerors” had led “armies of soldiers, civilians and tillers of the soil” to die “for a delusion such as ‘we are Germans,’ ‘our enemies are French,’” and named the alternative: “patriotism in the larger sense which includes all races and native lands” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace). Both talks survive as notes taken by listeners, so the Tablet to The Hague is the firmer text for His exact words; the talks agree with it in substance.

Is this a contradiction?

‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls borders “imaginary” and also proposes that the frontiers of every nation be “clearly fixed” by a binding world pact. These two statements are compatible. Borders are made by people, not by creation, and so they are no ground for one people to claim superiority or to shed blood. Because they are human arrangements, they can be settled justly by agreement among all nations, and doing so removes them as causes of war. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not ask for borders to be erased; He asks that people stop worshipping them.

A 1917 Tablet of the Divine Plan places patriotism and nationalism in their proper rank:

“In the contingent world there are many collective centers which are conducive to association and unity between the children of men. For example, patriotism is a collective center; nationalism is a collective center; identity of interests is a collective center; political alliance is a collective center; the union of ideals is a collective center, and the prosperity of the world of humanity is dependent upon the organization and promotion of the collective centers. Nevertheless, all the above institutions are, in reality, the matter and not the substance, accidental and not eternal—temporary and not everlasting.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablet to the Bahá’ís of the United States and Canada, 8 March 1917, Tablets of the Divine Plan (tablets-divine-plan)

Patriotism and nationalism here are real goods that bring people together, and the prosperity of the world depends on them. They are not ultimate. The trouble begins when a temporary bond is treated as the eternal one.

Shoghi Effendi: from “sane patriotism” to “false gods”

The Guardian wrote about nationalism across the two decades in which it did the most harm, and his language grows sharper as events unfold. His position does not change.

1931. In The Goal of a New World Order he names “a narrow and brutal nationalism, which the post-war theory of self-determination has served to reinforce” as chiefly responsible for prohibitive tariffs, and records a year’s world spending on “national competitive armaments” of “no less than a thousand million pounds.” In the same letter he states that the Bahá’í world order “can conflict with no legitimate allegiances, nor can it undermine essential loyalties,” that it does not seek “to stifle the flame of a sane and intelligent patriotism,” and that it “insists upon the subordination of national impulses and interests to the imperative claims of a unified world.”

1936. In The Unfoldment of World Civilization he describes “an intolerant and militant nationalism” that “insisted on deifying the state, has inculcated the war-spirit, and incited to racial animosity,” and he writes the sentences the House of Justice would later quote: “Nation-building has come to an end. The anarchy inherent in state sovereignty is moving towards a climax.” He also describes what the Faith does for its own members:

“While preserving their patriotism and safeguarding their lesser loyalties, it has made them lovers of mankind, and the determined upholders of its best and truest interests.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Unfoldment of World Civilization, 11 March 1936 (world-order-bahaullah)

1938. In The Advent of Divine Justice he describes the persecuted Bahá’ís of Persia as “among the most disinterested, the most competent, and the most ardent lovers of their native land,” and their persecutors as unable to appreciate “their high sense of world citizenship which the advocates of an excessive and narrow nationalism can never hope to appreciate” (advent-divine-justice). In one sentence the Bahá’ís are the best patriots and the truest world citizens, and the narrow nationalists are the ones who misunderstand both.

1941. In The Promised Day Is Come, written during the Second World War, he names nationalism first among three idols:

“The chief idols in the desecrated temple of mankind are none other than the triple gods of Nationalism, Racialism and Communism, at whose altars governments and peoples, whether democratic or totalitarian, at peace or at war, of the East or of the West, Christian or Islamic, are, in various forms and in different degrees, now worshiping. Their high priests are the politicians and the worldly-wise, the so-called sages of the age; their sacrifice, the flesh and blood of the slaughtered multitudes; their incantations outworn shibboleths and insidious and irreverent formulas; their incense, the smoke of anguish that ascends from the lacerated hearts of the bereaved, the maimed, and the homeless.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come (promised-day-come)

He then defines the doctrine he means: “The theories and policies, so unsound, so pernicious, which deify the state and exalt the nation above mankind.” This is the strongest language in the corpus on the subject, and it appears in the same work, a few pages before the passage that defends “a sane and intelligent patriotism.” That placement settles the reading. The idol is the nation exalted above mankind, and the condemnation does not touch the love of a homeland. Shoghi Effendi’s charge is that nationalism has taken the place of God: people give it the worship, sacrifice and final loyalty that belong to God alone.

Telling the two apart

The sources do not give a checklist, and the House of Justice does not define where “sane” ends. They do, however, describe the two attitudes consistently, and the descriptions can be gathered without adding to them.

Two features recur. The first is direction: sane patriotism expresses love for one’s own people as service to them, and unbridled nationalism expresses it as hostility toward others. The second is rank: sane patriotism holds the nation below God and below humanity, and unbridled nationalism puts nothing above it. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s appeal to Persia in 1875 is a worked example of the first column.

The House of Justice’s 1970 letter to National Spiritual Assemblies in Africa, written as nations there were winning independence, states the practical form:

“The Faith is not opposed to the true interests of any nation, nor is it against any party or faction. It holds aloof from all controversies and transcends them all, while enjoining upon its followers loyalty to government and a sane patriotism. This love for their country the Bahá’ís show by serving its well-being in their daily activity, or by working in the administrative channels of the government instead of through party politics or in diplomatic or political posts.” — Universal House of Justice, 8 February 1970 (muhj-1963-1986)

The same letter describes African peoples at that moment as “swept by the heady enthusiasms of new-found independence” with “their vision obscured by the haze of materialism and the dust of nationalistic passions and age-old tribal rivalries.” The House of Justice did not ask the Bahá’ís of newly independent nations to love those nations less. It asked them to love them without partisanship.

This leads to a limit that a reader needs to keep in mind. Bahá’ís do not take sides in political disputes. Shoghi Effendi described the Faith as “essentially non-political, supra-national in character, rigidly non-partisan, and entirely dissociated from nationalistic ambitions, pursuits, and purposes” (world-order-bahaullah). A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 27 April 2017 adds that Bahá’ís and their institutions “should not take positions on the political decisions of governments, including disputes among governments of different nations.” The distinction on this page describes an attitude and a principle. It is not a means of labelling particular governments, parties or leaders as nationalist.

After 1985: the House of Justice watches the tide

The House of Justice’s letters since 1985 show the issue rising and falling with world events.

  • Riḍván 1990: “Nationalism and racism still work their treachery in men’s hearts” (muhj-1986-2001).
  • Riḍván 1992, after the Soviet Union dissolved: “With the fresh tide of political freedom resulting from the collapse of the strongholds of communism has come an explosion of nationalism. The concomitant rise of racism in many regions has become a matter of serious global concern.” (muhj-1986-2001).
  • April 2002, to the world’s religious leaders, the most hopeful assessment:

“The beleaguered battalions of nationalism face a similar fate. With each passing crisis in world affairs, it becomes easier for the citizen to distinguish between a love of country that enriches one’s life, and submission to inflammatory rhetoric designed to provoke hatred and fear of others. Even where it is expedient to participate in the familiar nationalistic rites, public response is as often marked by feelings of awkwardness as it is by the strong convictions and ready enthusiasm of earlier times.” — Universal House of Justice, April 2002, To the World’s Religious Leaders (muhj-2001-2022)

  • Century of Light (2001), prepared under the House of Justice’s supervision, describes fascism and its imitators: “Fuelled by a manic nationalism, this aberration of the human spirit deified the state, discovered everywhere imaginary threats to the national survival of whatever unhappy people it had fastened upon, and preached to all who would listen the notion that war has an ‘ennobling’ influence on the human soul” (century-of-light).
  • 2 March 2013, to the Bahá’ís of Iran, whom Shoghi Effendi had called “the most ardent lovers of their native land,” the House of Justice asks for “a love of one’s country that cannot be manipulated” (muhj-2001-2022).
  • 27 April 2017, a letter to an individual written on the House of Justice’s behalf:

“For many decades following the second great war of the twentieth century, humanity moved, with fits and starts, toward the promise of a united world. The failure to complete the project of the unification of nations, however, left gaps in relations in which supranational problems could fester and threaten the security and well-being of peoples and states, leading to a recrudescence of prejudice, of divers expressions of factionalism, and of virulent nationalism that are the very negation of Bahá’u’lláh’s message of peace and oneness.” — on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 27 April 2017 (muhj-2001-2022)

The 2017 letter gives a cause the 1985 paragraph only implies. When the unification of nations stops short, problems that cross borders have no body able to solve them, and nationalism grows in the gap. Read this way, the paragraph’s last sentence, that activities which “nurture mutual affection and a sense of solidarity among peoples need greatly to be increased,” is a practical remedy and not a pious wish.

The present state of the field

This section describes the world as external sources report it in October 2026. It is description, not authority for any Bahá’í principle, and the figures will change.

Shoghi Effendi’s 1931 complaint about “national competitive armaments” costing “a thousand million pounds” in a year has a present-day counterpart. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute’s fact sheet of April 2026 reports that world military expenditure “rose by 2.9 per cent in real terms to reach $2887 billion in 2025,” the eleventh consecutive year of growth, and that the share of world GDP spent on the military rose from 2.4 to 2.5 per cent. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s 1875 warning, quoted in Part III of the statement, applies directly: “if the preparations for war and the military forces of any nation should be allowed to increase, they will arouse the suspicion of others.”

The “favorable signs” the 1985 statement counted have counterparts too. On 22 September 2024 the UN General Assembly adopted the Pact for the Future (resolution A/RES/79/1) without a vote, after about nine months of negotiation, with commitments on international peace and security and on reforming global governance. Whether such agreements are put into effect is a separate question; the statement itself says that the idea of collective security based on political agreements alone “is a chimera.”

What the sources leave open

  • No definition of the line. The statement and the Guardian describe the two attitudes; neither gives a test for a given policy or a given person. This page gathers their descriptions and does not add criteria of its own.
  • Not a political tool. Because of the principle of non-involvement in politics, nothing here supports calling a specific government, party or movement “unbridled nationalist.” The House of Justice’s own later letters speak of trends, not of named actors.
  • Text and translation. The Lawḥ-i-Dunyá saying appears in three English renderings (boast, pride himself, glory); the article uses each where its source uses it. Two of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s statements quoted above (Paris, 1911; Stanford, 1912) survive in notes taken by listeners and carry less textual certainty than His Tablets; the Tablet to The Hague is used as the main text for His view.
  • Dates not in the corpus. The corpus does not give a date of revelation for the Lawḥ-i-Dunyá or the Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd, and this page gives none.
  • Web figures age. The SIPRI figures and the status of the Pact for the Future describe the field as of October 2026.

For the song

The heart of it

Love your country, and do not worship it: the statement asks that the nation stay inside a wider loyalty to humanity, because “the advantage of the part in a world society is best served by promoting the advantage of the whole.”

Short quotations that would sing

  1. “The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd (quoted in The Promise of World Peace)
  2. “It is not his to boast who loveth his country, but it is his who loveth the world.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Dunyá
  3. “Let not a man glory in that he loves his country; let him rather glory in this, that he loves his kind.” — Bahá’u’lláh, as rendered by Shoghi Effendi in The Promised Day Is Come
  4. “Love of all the world’s peoples does not exclude love of one’s country.” — The Promise of World Peace, 1985
  5. “Let nothing grieve thee, O Land of Ṭá.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶91, addressing Ṭihrán, the city that exiled Him
  6. “Patriotic love is finite.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, 24 October 1911
  7. “Love must be free from boundaries!” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, same talk
  8. “God has set up no frontier between France and Germany; they are continuous.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablet to The Hague, 1919
  9. “It is our eternal tomb.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablet to The Hague, of the earth people fight over
  10. “a love of one’s country that cannot be manipulated” — Universal House of Justice, 2 March 2013
  11. “must include and not exclude the love of one’s own country” — Shoghi Effendi, 1941
  12. “Nation-building has come to an end.” — Shoghi Effendi, 1936 (quoted in The Promise of World Peace)

Concrete images, people, places and moments

  • Doves. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “if a dove from the east and a dove from the west, a dove from the north and a dove from the south chance to arrive, at the same time, in one spot, they immediately associate in harmony” (Tablet to The Hague). “The blessed animals engage in no patriotic quarrels.”
  • A river. “Man declares a river to be a boundary line between two countries, calling this side French and the other side German, whereas the river was created for both and is a natural artery for all” (Montreal, 1 September 1912, notes).
  • The grave as homeland. We live on the earth a few days and are buried in it; it is “our eternal tomb”; why tear one another to pieces over it (Tablet to The Hague). At Stanford: “Shall man fight for the tomb which devours him, for his eternal sepulcher?”
  • Soldiers and farmers. “Armies of soldiers, civilians and tillers of the soil fought and died … shedding their innocent blood for a delusion such as ‘we are Germans,’ ‘our enemies are French’” (Stanford University, 8 October 1912, two years before the First World War).
  • The exile who blessed his city. Bahá’u’lláh left Ṭihrán in exile on 12 January 1853, and later told the city it would be “the source of the joy of all mankind.”
  • An anonymous love letter. In 1875 ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote, without signing it, a book to His countrymen that repeatedly calls “O people of Persia!”, recalls when Írán “was as the heart of the world,” and in the same book proposes a world covenant fixing every nation’s frontiers.
  • The persecuted patriots. The Bahá’ís of Iran, called by Shoghi Effendi “the most ardent lovers of their native land,” denied rights in that land, and named in the closing part of the 1985 statement: “the persecution they still endure in the land in which their Faith was born.”
  • The ladder of loyalties. Family, tribe, city-state, nation, and now the world: “Unity of family, of tribe, of city-state, and nation have been successively attempted and fully established.” Each larger circle contains the smaller ones.
  • The idol’s temple. Shoghi Effendi’s 1941 picture of nationalism as a false god: priests, altars, “their sacrifice, the flesh and blood of the slaughtered multitudes,” “their incense, the smoke of anguish.”
  • A thousand million pounds. The arms spending Shoghi Effendi recorded for one year in 1931; SIPRI’s figure for 2025 is $2887 billion.
  • A letter delayed by war. The Central Organization for a Durable Peace wrote to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá during the war; their letter of 11 February 1916 reached Him only after it, and He answered at once on 17 December 1919.
  • The moment of independence. 1985 counts “the achievement … of independence by the majority of all the nations on earth” as a sign of hope, which means a nation’s independence day is not the enemy of this song.

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • Against love of country. The paragraph explicitly protects “a sane and legitimate patriotism.” A song that mocks flags, anthems or homesickness as such contradicts its source. The Guardian: the verse “cannot … be construed as a repudiation.”
  • Against borders as such. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls borders “imaginary” as grounds for enmity, and in the same breath asks that frontiers be “clearly fixed” by world agreement. “No borders” as a slogan is not the teaching.
  • One world, one culture. Shoghi Effendi writes that the world order of Bahá’u’lláh “disclaims all attempts at uniformity” and keeps “the system of national autonomy.” A song should not picture the nations dissolving into sameness.
  • Naming a villain. Bahá’ís take no side in political disputes. A song that points at a particular country, leader or party turns a principle into partisanship. Shoghi Effendi’s picture covers “governments and peoples, whether democratic or totalitarian … of the East or of the West.”
  • Disloyalty. The Bahá’í position includes “loyalty to government.” A song should not suggest that world citizenship means withdrawing allegiance from one’s own country.
  • Despair or contempt. The 1985 statement frames nationalism as a mark of humanity’s adolescence on the way to maturity, not as proof that people are wicked; its whole argument is against the belief that humans are “incorrigibly selfish and aggressive.”
  • Mixing translations. The three renderings of the Lawḥ-i-Dunyá saying (boast, pride himself, glory) should each be quoted exactly as given, not blended into one line and attributed to a single source.
