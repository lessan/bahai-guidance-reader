Other Poems and Poetic Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh

Last updated 17 October 2026

Bahá’u’lláh revealed more verse and poetic prose than the long poems that have their own pages in this series. This page covers the shorter ones that have a translation or a description in the corpus: the ode Az Bágh-i-Iláhí, two Tablets that use the nightingale as a figure (The Nightingale and the Owl and The Nightingale of Separation), the Tablet Shikkar-Shikan Shavand, a poem called Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí that is named in the sources but not translated there, and the lyrics (ghazals) in a manuscript collection. A last section records that no translated poem of the Báb was found. The series hub is Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

Poems and Tablets with their own pages:

  • Rashḥ-i-‘Amá (The Clouds of the Realms Above)
  • The Ode of the Dove (Qaṣídiy-i-‘Izz-i-Varqá’íyyih)
  • Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá (The Cupbearer from the Unseen Realm of Eternity)
  • The Mathnaví-i-Mubárak
  • The Tablet of the Bell (Lawḥ-i-Náqús)
  • The Wondrous Maiden (Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb)
  • The Immortal Youth (Lawḥ-i-Ghulámu’l-Khuld)
  • The Tablet of the Holy Mariner (Lawḥ-i-Malláḥu’l-Quds)
  • Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat

Status of the texts. None of the poems on this page has an authorised English translation as a whole. Frank Lewis (a scholar, not an authority) writes that Shoghi Effendi did not translate Bahá’u’lláh’s versified poems; Shoghi Effendi did render one passage of the Ode of the Dove in God Passes By (GPB VII.31). Every full English text below is a provisional translation: it is the translator’s own work and is not the text of the Writings. The one authorised English text on this page is an excerpt from Shikkar-Shikan Shavand (see that section).

Az Bágh-i-Iláhí

What it is. A Persian and Arabic ode of Bahá’u’lláh. In the Persian it opens “از باغ الهی با سدرهٴ ناری آن تازه غلام آمد” (az bágh-i-iláhí bá sidrih-yi-nárí án táziḥ ghulám ámad), and each Persian line is followed by an Arabic line that opens “های های هذا” (háy háy hádhá). The original text is an authenticated typescript on the Bahá’í Reference Library, which Masumian reproduces in full ([source], lines 431 onward). It is catalogued as BH01007. Masumian says that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá transcribed the whole Tablet and added all its short-vowel marks, and that the handwritten copy he used (INBA 19:27–31) ends with the note muqábilih shud, “it has been checked against the original”.

When and where. Adib Taherzadeh, quoted in Masumian’s introduction, writes (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, pp. 218–19):

“an ode revealed not long before the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh. It is one of His most joyous odes, composed in an exalted style. Each Persian verse is followed by one in Arabic”

([source], line 49)

Taherzadeh adds that the companions of Bahá’u’lláh in Baghdád chanted the ode in their gatherings. The Bahaipedia list of the Tablets by period places it in the Baghdád years before 1863 ([source], line 81). The ode is therefore from Baghdád, between 1858 and the Declaration in 1863. No source gives a more exact date.

Translator and form. Adib Masumian, “provisional translation (in other words, not official or authorized)”, dated 24 October 2021, with changes on 24 May 2023 after he compared the INBA copy with the authenticated typescript. He set it in blank verse, in iambic tetrameter, and thanks Khazeh Fananapazir, Abir Majid and Sen McGlinn for suggestions. The translation has 32 numbered stanzas, each ending with the refrain Make haste, make haste!, followed by a closing passage in prose. It has 17 translator’s notes that explain readings of unusual words.

Extract. The first stanza, in provisional translation by Adib Masumian (2021, revised 2023):

Hailing from the godly garden, Invested with the Burning Bush, That fresh and novel Youth is come! Make haste, make haste!

([source], lines 74–77.) Stanza 20 names the Youth and gives a picture of the Day:

This is the Youth born in Shíráz! This is the Lantern in the niche!

([source], lines 261–262.)

What it says. Most stanzas have three lines that begin “This is the …” and name something that has arrived (the Robe, the Draught, the Raiment, the Face, the Lote-Tree, the Wine), then three lines that say who comes and from where. The listing of Syriac, Hebrew and Arabic in stanza 15 is Masumian’s reading of the three dispensations, according to his note: “a reference to the Christian dispensation”, the Mosaic dispensation and the Islamic dispensation. Taherzadeh reads the ode as describing Bahá’u’lláh’s station and the Promised Day of God.

What is not settled. The exact date; several readings of rare words (Masumian gives alternatives in his notes, for example for “Throat so delicate” and “placeless”); and the relation between the authenticated typescript and the INBA copy, which differ in places.

Licence. Masumian’s translation is his own work, posted on his website with no licence statement. Treat it as permission-needed. The Persian original is a Writing of Bahá’u’lláh and may be quoted as such.

The Nightingale and the Owl

What it is. A Persian Tablet in the form of a story with a story inside it. It is prose, not verse, and no source calls it a poem. It is included here because it is a figurative tale about the search for the Beloved. The bahai-library tag gives its Persian name as Gul va Bulbul. Alison Marshall’s introduction calls it “a short story, which reads much like a fairy tale”.

When and where. The bahai-library page gives “Date unknown, written sometime during Bahá’u’lláh’s stay in Edirne, circa 1863-1868” and a date field “1863/1868?”. Marshall adds that it was written “sometime after he broke off relations with his half-brother, Mirza Yahya”, and notes that Adib Taherzadeh suggested (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, pp. 241–44) that the artificial nightingales driven from the divine garden may refer to that separation. Both are scholars’ readings. The Tablet’s own text names Edirne: “Now, in Edirne I have thrown off my veil.”

Translator. Juan Cole, with editing by Alison Marshall. The year of the translation is not given. The page was mirrored on bahai-library.com with permission from whoisbahaullah.com and posted there on 2014-11-19.

The story. The Rose (Bahá’u’lláh) addresses nightingales who say they are natives of Medina and knew the Arabian rose. The Rose answers that He is “neither from Medina nor from Mecca” and that they “recognized walls, rafters and buildings”. He tells them they are like an owl that insisted crows sing better than the nightingale, because it had heard a melody in a garden and seen a crow fly out. The nightingale had been silenced by the hunter. A true nightingale then arrives from the divine garden and tells the false ones to fly away. The last paragraph addresses “human nightingales”.

Extracts, in provisional translation by Juan Cole:

“Nightingales, I am neither from Medina nor from Mecca, neither from Iraq nor Syria.”

(Paragraph [4], [source], line 124.)

“Do not transform a minor illusion into a hundred thousand certainties, nor one syllable you overheard into the entire visible world.”

(Paragraph [11], line 152.)

“make every effort to recognize the friend”

(Paragraph [13], line 160.)

What is not settled. The date within 1863–68, the identity of the “artificial nightingales”, and the Persian title. Cole’s translation is numbered by paragraph, and the paragraph numbers are his.

Licence. permission-needed. The page is a mirror made with the translator’s permission; that permission does not extend to a published lyric.

The Nightingale of Separation (Lawḥ-i-Bulbulu’l-Firáq)

What it is. A short Tablet that begins with a poem of ten lines in Arabic, each ending with the call “you who are filled with yearning”, and continues in Persian prose. It is a letter of farewell to the friends. Cole describes the change of register: “the switch away from the formalistic Arabic prose poetry at the beginning the more intimate, chaste Persian prose thereafter”. The original is printed in Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá, vol. 4 (Tehran, 1968), pp. 363–67, according to the bahai-library page; no original text is in the corpus.

When and where. The sources disagree. Cole writes that Ishráq-Khávarí and Taherzadeh date it to the Baghdád period, but that he thinks “internal evidence clearly points to its having been written after Bahá’u’lláh had left the city of Baghdad”, possibly from Istanbul in autumn 1863, because the text speaks of “the bird of eternity” having “flown from the land of Iraq”. Bahaipedia’s list of Tablets places it among the Baghdád Tablets of 1857–63 ([source], line 62). Cole also notes that Bahá’u’lláh had already made His Riḍván declaration of April–May 1863, and that the Tablet says that in the past scriptures “the bird of Persia shall sing an Arabian melody”.

Translator. Juan Cole, 1998, “Translator’s introduction” and translation. Mirrored on bahai-library.com with permission from his university site.

Extracts, in provisional translation by Juan Cole (1998). The poem opens:

The nightingale of separation, perched upon the branch of the horizon, calls out in grief, you who are filled with yearning;

([source], lines 121–123.) It ends:

No sorrow shall ever compare to this grief in the realm of the unknowable essence, for the wind of separation is blowing, you who are filled with yearning!

(Lines 177–179.) The prose closes with counsel:

“Friends, do not forget the union of souls while you are in the affliction of separation. Lament this departure, but sow the seed of patience in the good earth of the heart, watering it with your tears, so that it will give forth sweet fruit.”

(Line 235 onward.)

What is not settled. The date (Baghdád or after the departure), which prophecy the phrase about the bird of Persia refers to (Cole: “I am unaware that anyone has yet identified the precise prophecy”), and whether “Iraq” here means Persia (Cole reads it as ‘Iráq-i-‘Ajam).

Licence. permission-needed.

Shikkar-Shikan Shavand

What it is. A Persian Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh in prose, revealed in Baghdád. It opens with a couplet of the Persian poet Háfiẓ and contains further quoted verse (Rúmí, and Ibn al-Fáriḍ in Arabic). It is not a versified work, but it is poetic in its imagery, and it is known by the first words of the Háfiẓ couplet, “Shikkar-shikan shavand hamih ṭútíyán-i-Hind”. Bahaipedia renders the title Sweet Scented Being. Bahaipedia and the Wilmette Institute notes give the occasion: a letter to Bahá’u’lláh from Mírzá Ḥusayn-i-Mutavallí, a Bábí who wrote on behalf of the Persian Foreign Minister Mírzá Sa‘íd Khán, warning Him to stop teaching, appearing in public and mentioning the Faith. The Tablet answers that letter and was meant also for Mírzá Sa‘íd Khán, the Persian consul Mírzá Buzurg Khán and others. The Wilmette notes put this about 1860–61 and say the Tablet “is very difficult, if not impossible, to translate into English” ([source]). The Tablet uses the Persian poets’ own verses in a prose letter of resignation and defiance.

Which parts are authorised. A part of the Tablet was published in English by the Universal House of Justice in The Bahá’í World, vol. XVIII (1979–83), Haifa, 1986, p. 11, in a section of excerpts from Fire and Light (Nár va Núr, 1982). All those excerpts except one were translated by Habib Taherzadeh “with the assistance of a Committee at the Bahá’í World Centre”. For this Tablet that excerpt is the authorised translation of the passage that begins “They that yearn for the abode of the Beloved” and ends “the familiar voice of the Friend”, including the Rúmí couplet inside it ([source], lines 51–108).

Shahrokh Monjazeb (2003) translated the whole Tablet. His notes say that the italicised passages are the authorised excerpt and that “All other non-italicized text, except the opening verse of the Háfiz, are translated by Shahrokh Monjazeb and must be regarded as a provisional translation pending the publication of an authorized version by the Bahá’í World Centre.” In the saved copy the italics are not reliably preserved, so the authorised portion is identified here from the Taherzadeh file, not from Monjazeb’s page. Monjazeb’s wording of that portion differs slightly from the authorised one in a few places (for example, he has “nor do they flee from that which is ordained by God” where the authorised text has “nor do they flee away”). The authorised text is the one to use.

Extracts. The opening couplet of Háfiẓ, in the translation that opens Monjazeb’s page (its translator is not named):

Warblers, mellifluous-toned, all the parrots of Ind shall be, Because of this Pársí sugar-cone which to Bengal goes.

([source], lines 48–50.) The authorised excerpt, authorised translation (Habib Taherzadeh with a committee, The Bahá’í World XVIII, p. 11):

They that yearn for the abode of the Beloved, they that circle round the sanctuary of the Desired One, are not apprehensive of trials and adversities, nor do they flee away from that which is ordained by God.

What else but a nightingale can understand a Nightingale’s melody and who else but the intimate friend can recognize the familiar voice of the Friend?

([source], lines 51 and 108.) The longer passage between these two extracts speaks of welcoming death on the path of the Beloved: “It behoveth us to kiss the hand of the would-be assassin and to set out, dancing, on our way to the habitation of the Friend.”

What is not settled. The full authorised text; the translator of the Háfiẓ couplet; and the exact date of the Tablet (Bahaipedia puts it in the Baghdád years).

Licence. Authorised excerpt: ok-with-credit. Monjazeb’s translation of the rest: permission-needed. The bahai-library copy of the Taherzadeh excerpt is marked “public domain” by the library; this page does not rely on that mark, because the text is a publication of the Bahá’í World Centre.

Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí

What the sources say. This is a Persian poem of Bahá’u’lláh, listed under the title Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí in three places in the corpus:

  • Bahaipedia’s list of the Tablets by period puts it in the Sulaymáníyyih years, 1854–56, among the “Dervish Poems” ([source], line 38).
  • Frank Lewis lists it with Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá as one of “two Persian odes”, and gives the title in English as “Come Back and Serve the Chalice” (Báz á va bidih jámí). He says that most of Bahá’u’lláh’s poems date to the Baghdád period and that, apart from three unofficial translations, “Bahá’u’lláh’s poems have not yet been Englished” ([source], line 72).
  • A list of Bahá’u’lláh’s best-known writings in The Bahá’í World includes Báz-Áv-u-Bidih-Jámí (in the OCR of volume 16 the line is damaged).

The sources disagree on the period (Sulaymáníyyih 1854–56 or Baghdád). The title means, in Lewis’s rendering, “Come back and give a cup”.

No translation and no text in the corpus. No English translation of this poem is in `sources/`. The one place that may hold its Persian is the manuscript collection described in the next section. Page 158 of that scan carries a line (OCR) that begins “باز آویده سامی این ساقی عطشانرا زان ساغر باقی ده”, which would be báz á(v)-u-bidih jámí (“come again and give a cup”) followed by “this thirsty cupbearer … give from that everlasting goblet” ([source], line 1825). That is a match with the title, not a confirmed identification, and the OCR line is corrupted. A lyric writer should not use the title, or any English wording of this poem, until a translator has read the text.

The ghazals in the Majmú‘ih-yi-Mubárak

What the source is. [source] is a scan with OCR of volume 30 of the Iranian National Spiritual Assembly’s typed collection of the works of the Supreme Pen. The title page says it was reproduced in a limited number with the Assembly’s permission and that it is not among the approved publications (Persian text, line 25), dated Shahru’l-Jalál 133 BE. It is therefore a working copy, not an authenticated text. The contents list in lines 37–74 gives the sections: the Hidden Words in Arabic and Persian, Aṣl-i-Kullu’l-Khayr, the Bishárát, the Tablet of Wisdom, Shikkar-shikan shavand (index page 70), Hádhih Waraqatu’l-Firdaws, Persian and Arabic prayers, a long section headed Alváḥ-i-Mubárak, then “Mathnaví-i-Mubárak” (index page 157) and “Ghazalíyyát-i-Mubárak” (index page 175). The printed page numbers in the scan do not match the contents list: the poems that follow the Mathnaví run from about printed page 157 to 174.

What the ghazals are. Here the OCR is poor, with verse lines out of order, merged or misread, and the description is limited to what can be seen. Reading by rhyme and refrain, at least five lyrics follow the end of the Mathnaví. They are:

  • The Mathnaví ends with a section headed tamma and goes straight into Sáqí az ghayb (printed page 157): “ساقی از غیب قبای ابرقه برافکن از غبار” (line 1778).
  • A lyric on printed pages 158–159 whose lines include the báz á line quoted above (line 1825).
  • A longer lyric with rhyme in -ídih on pages 160–163, with the cupbearer and the wine of the Kauthar (line 1837 has “گفتم چه تاخیر ای مهستر دوران در ده جامی زان کوثر خشنده”).
  • A lyric on pages 166–167 whose refrain is mírízad, “pours down”: “وجه باقی بین چهره ساقی بین رق زجاجی بین کز کوثر ما میریزد” (line 1922).
  • A lyric on pages 168–171 whose refrain is yá húy-i-ú, “his cry of Yá Hú”.
  • A lyric on pages 172–173 that addresses Love (“ای عشق توئی سلطان در مرکز امکان”).

After the last lyric (printed page 174) there is a scribe’s note giving the date 3 Dhu’l-Ḥijjah 1318 AH. The note belongs to the copy, not to the poems. The page that follows (printed page 175, headed in Arabic Huwa’l-Muqtadir ‘alá má yurád) is a prose passage of counsel on the peace of the world, and the contents list gives that as the last item of the section.

These lyrics are described, not translated. The ghazals use the images of the other Sulaymáníyyih and Baghdád poems: the cupbearer, the wine, the Beloved’s hair and face, Sinai and the Burning Bush, the nightingale and the rose. An English reader needs a translator who can read the Persian typescript directly.

Other poems named but not available

Bahaipedia’s period list names more Sulaymáníyyih poems, with no translation in the corpus: ‘Ishq az sidriy-i-a‘lá ámad, Bi-Jánán ján hamí dar-yáft rah, Mast-and bulbulán, Sahar ámad bi bistar-am yár, But-i-má ámad bá battí-u bádih and Ay bulbulán ([source], lines 36–46). Lewis says that few of Bahá’u’lláh’s poems have been put into English. Nothing more can be said about these here.

The Báb

No translated poem of the Báb was found in the corpus. The searches were:

  • [source] has no hit for “poem”, “poetry”, “ode”, “ghazal” or “qaṣídih”. It consists of excerpts from the Báb’s Tablets, commentaries, the Bayán and prayers; where it says “verses” it means the Báb’s revealed verses, not versified poems.
  • A search of `sources/` for “poem”, “poetry”, “ghazal” or “qasida” near “the Báb” found only these: E. G. Browne’s remark that opinion differs about “the authorship of various Bábí books and poems” ([source], line 848); his description of the poems printed in his volume, which are by Qurratu’l-‘Ayn (Ṭáhirih) and Nabíl, with other Bábí poems he held (such as an ode by Mírzá Aslam of Núr) left out ([source], lines 441–446); and modern poems written about the Báb by Bahá’í poets, which are not translations.
  • [source] has no hit for “poem”, “poet” or “ghazal”.

This means no translated poem of the Báb was found in the corpus, not that the Báb wrote none. Lyrics that quote the Báb should use His authorised prayers and Tablets. Poetry by Ṭáhirih, a Bábí, has its own pages in this series; see the hub.

Themes and images

  • The nightingale, the Rose, the hunter and the owl (The Nightingale and the Owl; The Nightingale of Separation): recognition of the Beloved, and failure to recognise Him by place or tradition.
  • The cupbearer, the cup and the wine (Az Bágh-i-Iláhí, Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí, the ghazals): the gift of the Revelation.
  • Separation, tears and departure (The Nightingale of Separation).
  • Welcoming trials and sacrifice with joy (Shikkar-Shikan Shavand, authorised excerpt).
  • The Day of God arriving (Az Bágh-i-Iláhí): the Youth, the Burning Bush, the Lote-Tree, the Lantern in the niche.

What is not settled

The dates of every poem on this page are open to some degree (see each section). The poems with only a provisional translation have not been read by the Bahá’í World Centre. The Persian of the ghazals exists in this corpus only as OCR of a typed copy. No translation is offered here for any poem not already translated by a named translator.

For songwriting

Notes only, not lyrics.

Shortest exact phrases that can be used.

  • “They that yearn for the abode of the Beloved” (authorised excerpt, The Bahá’í World XVIII, p. 11; ok-with-credit).
  • “the intimate friend can recognize the familiar voice of the Friend” (authorised excerpt; ok-with-credit).
  • “Make haste, make haste!” (Masumian’s refrain; permission-needed).
  • “That fresh and novel Youth is come!” (Masumian; permission-needed).
  • “the nightingale of separation” and “you who are filled with yearning” (Cole 1998; permission-needed).
  • “recognized walls, rafters and buildings” (Cole; permission-needed).

For any phrase marked permission-needed, ask the translator first, or write a new line in the song’s own words based on the idea.

Images. The garden, the Rose, the nightingale, the owl in the dark, the hunter, the cupbearer and the cup, the branch of the horizon, tears, the autumn wind in The Nightingale of Separation, a lamp in a niche.

Possible arc. Two arcs are in the sources. In The Nightingale and the Owl the singer is told that the Beloved has appeared, is refused because of where He came from, and ends by being asked to see Him for Himself. In The Nightingale of Separation the singer mourns a departure and ends with patience and the union of souls.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not present a provisional translation as the text of the Writings.
  • It must not say that the Nightingale and the Owl is about a specific person or event as if that were established. The link to Mírzá Yaḥyá is Taherzadeh’s suggestion, as reported by Marshall.
  • It must not give a date or place for these poems with more certainty than the sources do.
  • It must not put the Háfiẓ, Rúmí or Ibn al-Fáriḍ lines in the mouth of Bahá’u’lláh as His own words; they are quotations inside the Tablet.
  • It must not use the title or any wording of Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí as if it were translated.
