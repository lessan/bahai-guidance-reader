The Day Foretold: The Zoroastrian Promise and Sháh-Bahrám

Last updated 16 October 2026

What this page covers

This page belongs to the series the-day-foretold, which takes the religions one at a time. The other pages are the-day-foretold-hindu, the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-buddhist, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi. The wider topic is in promises-of-the-future.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each religion. For this one he writes: “to the Zoroastrians the promised Sháh-Bahrám” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11).

The page keeps four kinds of source apart:

  1. Zoroastrian scripture, in public-domain translations of the 1880s. These are of two kinds. The Gathas and the Zamyad Yasht (Yasht 19) are Avestan texts. The Bahman Yasht and the Bundahishn are Pahlavi (Middle Persian) books, translated by E. W. West.
  2. The Bahá’í Writings and Shoghi Effendi, which state the Bahá’í understanding of the fulfilment.
  3. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi and a Research Department memorandum to the Universal House of Justice. These are not authoritative interpretation; each is labelled where it appears.
  4. History and scholarship (Susan Maneck, Christopher Buck, Moojan Momen), reported as the views of those authors.

The promise

The Gathas (Old Avestan)

The Gathas are the hymns that Zoroastrian tradition attributes to Zoroaster himself. They are in Old Avestan, the oldest layer of the Avesta (description from Wikipedia, “Yasna”). The translation is by Lawrence Heyworth Mills, 1887 (The Zend-Avesta, Part III, Sacred Books of the East 31). Mills puts words of his own in parentheses. The Gathas speak of Saoshyants, “saviours,” in the plural, and sometimes of one Saoshyant.

Zarathustra asks when they will come:

“When come, Great Giver! they who are the day’s enlighteners, to hold the Righteous Order of the world upright, and forward pressing?” (Mills, Yasna 46:3)

He prays that the Saoshyant may see the reward:

“let Thy Saoshyant see how gifts of recompense may be his own” (Mills, Yasna 48:9)

He also says who the saviours will be:

“Such shall be the Saviours of the Provinces” (Mills, Yasna 48:12)

And he names the path that their teaching makes: “the religious precepts and laws of the Saviours” (Mills, Yasna 34:13).

The Zamyad Yasht (Avestan)

Yasht 19 is also Avestan. Its sections 88–96 describe the kingly Glory that will attend the final Saoshyant, here called Astvat-ereta. The translation is by James Darmesteter, 1883 (The Zend-Avesta, Part II, Sacred Books of the East 23).

“a friend of Ahura Mazda, a son of Vispa-taurvairi, knowing the victorious knowledge” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.92)

“he shall look upon the whole living world with the eye of plenty, and his look shall deliver to immortality the whole of the living creatures” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.94)

The text ends with the defeat of falsehood: “the Word of falsehood smites, but the Word of truth shall smite it” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.96). Section 90 says “the Drug shall perish.” In the Avesta Drug is the Lie.

Vahram the Varjavand (Pahlavi)

The Bahman Yasht (Zand-i Vohuman Yasn) is a Pahlavi book, not part of the Avesta. West translated it in 1880 (Pahlavi Texts, Part I, Sacred Books of the East 5). In chapter III Ohrmazd (Auharmazd) speaks to Zoroaster about the coming of Hushedar, the first of the later figures who renew the religion, and of a prince named Vahram the Varjavand. West gives the passage on the prince’s birth:

“it is his father, a prince of the Kayan race, approaches the women, and a religious prince is born to him; he calls his name Vahram the Varjavand” (West, Bahman Yasht III.14)

“the night when that prince is born, a star falls from the sky” (West, Bahman Yasht III.15)

Later in the chapter Vahram arrives with power:

“he comes forth in full glory” (West, Bahman Yasht III.39)

“he drives away from the world covetousness, want, hatred, wrath, lust, envy, and wickedness.” (West, Bahman Yasht III.39)

“the wolf period goes away, and the sheep period comes on” (West, Bahman Yasht III.40)

West adds a note that “It is Vahram’s business to restore the empire”, while another figure restores the religion. The note is West’s reading.

The same chapter has a sign about the sun. Hushedar “cries to the sun with the swift horse” (West, Bahman Yasht III.45), and section 46 begins “The sun with the swift horse stands still ten”. The saved page ends at that point. The sun standing still is the sign that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá later interprets (see below).

The Saoshyant at the renovation (Pahlavi)

In the Bundahishn, chapter XXX, the final Saoshyant appears in West’s spelling as Soshyans. He and his assistants renew the world, and “give every man the reward and recompense suitable to his deeds” (West, Bundahishn XXX.27).

How the traditions of the promise are arranged

The Gathas speak of Saoshyants. Yasht 19 describes the final Saoshyant. The Pahlavi books add Hushedar, Vahram the Varjavand and later figures, and place them in a sequence of millennia. Shoghi Effendi’s phrase “the promised Sháh-Bahrám” corresponds to the name Vahram (Bahrám) in the Pahlavi tradition. In the texts gathered for this page, no authoritative Bahá’í text states how the Saoshyant of the Gathas and Yasht 19 relates to Sháh-Bahrám.

The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The title

Shoghi Effendi gives the title in God Passes By (GPB VI.11): “to the Zoroastrians the promised Sháh-Bahrám.” In the same chapter he records Bahá’u’lláh’s descent:

“He derived His descent, on the one hand, from Abraham (the Father of the Faithful) through his wife Katurah, and on the other from Zoroaster, as well as from Yazdigird, the last king of the Sásáníyán dynasty.” (God Passes By, GPB VI.12)

The sentence states the descent. It does not say that the descent fulfils the prophecy.

Three thousand years and the sun

Shoghi Effendi writes of Bahá’u’lláh’s Dispensation:

“To His Dispensation the sacred books of the followers of Zoroaster had referred as that in which the sun must needs be brought to a standstill for no less than one whole month. To Him Zoroaster must have alluded when, according to tradition, He foretold that a period of three thousand years of conflict and contention must needs precede the advent of the World-Savior Sháh-Bahrám, Who would triumph over Ahriman and usher in an era of blessedness and peace.” (God Passes By, GPB VI.14)

Here the Guardian cites the sun sign and the three thousand years “according to tradition.”

Bahá’u’lláh’s answer to the question about Sháh Bahrám

The Tablet of the Seven Questions (Lawḥ-i-Haft Pursish) was revealed in answer to Ustád Javán-Mard, a Zoroastrian who became a Bahá’í (see below). It is in The Tabernacle of Unity. The fourth question reads:

“Our Books have announced that Sháh Bahrám will come, invested with manifold signs, to guide the people aright” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.9)

Bahá’u’lláh answers:

“Whatsoever hath been announced in the Books hath been revealed and made clear. From every direction the signs have been manifested. The Omnipotent One is calling, in this day, and announcing the appearance of the Supreme Heaven.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.10)

The Tablet also addresses the priests in the voice of the promise. Shoghi Effendi quotes it in The Promised Day Is Come and writes that Bahá’u’lláh’s voice, “identifying itself with the voice of the promised Sháh-Bahrám,” addressed them (The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 192):

“O high priests! Ears have been given you that they may hearken unto the mystery of Him Who is the Self-Dependent, and eyes that they may behold Him. Wherefore flee ye? The Incomparable Friend is manifest. He speaketh that wherein lieth salvation.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.4)

Tablets to believers of Zoroastrian background

The Tabernacle of Unity also holds a Tablet to a believer of Zoroastrian background. Bahá’u’lláh writes of the Fire, which is the symbol of the Zoroastrian Dispensation:

“This is the mystery enshrined in your Book that was sent down aforetime, a mystery which hath until now remained concealed from the eyes and hearts of men. That primal Fire hath in this Day appeared with a new radiance and with immeasurable heat.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.2)

Shoghi Effendi names the Fire in God Passes By: the “Most Great Spirit” is “symbolized in the Zoroastrian, the Mosaic, the Christian, and Muḥammadan Dispensations by the Sacred Fire, the Burning Bush, the Dove and the Angel Gabriel respectively” (God Passes By, GPB VI.26).

The same Tablet says of priests:

“He indeed is a high priest who hath seen the light and hastened unto the way leading to the Beloved.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.3)

“the glad-tidings We had imparted in the Book have appeared in full effect” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.9)

The Tablet does not name “the Book” in that sentence.

In the Tablet to Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl, which answers questions from Mánikchí Ṣáḥib, Bahá’u’lláh writes of the fire-temples:

“The fire-temples of the world stand as eloquent testimony to this truth. In their time they summoned, with burning zeal, all the inhabitants of the earth to Him Who is the Spirit of purity.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 2.40)

And of the claim that one’s religion is superior to every other, He says that the Prophets are one: “All have proceeded from God, unto Him have they summoned all men, and unto Him have they returned” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 2.45).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on the sun standing still

A Zoroastrian believer wrote to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá about the prophecy. His Tablet in reply is quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh (WOB 6.13):

“Thou hadst written that in the sacred books of the followers of Zoroaster it is written that in the latter days, in three separate Dispensations, the sun must needs be brought to a standstill. In the first Dispensation, it is predicted, the sun will remain motionless for ten days; in the second for twice that time; in the third for no less than one whole month.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá reads the three Dispensations as the Muḥammadan Dispensation, the Dispensation of the Báb and the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh. In His reading the Sun of Truth “stood still” for the first period of ten days, “Each day is reckoned as one century,” and the Báb’s Dispensation “began in the year 1260 A.H. and was brought to a close in the year 1280 A.H.” For the third, “its duration hath been fixed for a period of one whole month, which is the maximum time taken by the sun to pass through a sign of the Zodiac.”

Two letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi discuss how to count the days. A letter of 7 August 1934 to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States and Canada says: “The thirty days in the last Dispensation should not be reckoned numerically, but should be considered as symbolizing the incomparable greatness of the Bahá’í Revelation.” A letter of 16 April 1951 says: “the language of prophecy has always in the past been veiled in meaning.”

A talk on the Day foretold

In a talk at 309 West Seventy-eighth Street, New York, on 5 July 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, the Zend-Avesta is among the books named: “These things are recorded in the Torah, or Old Testament, in the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta, and the books of Buddha.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.1)

Those who recognised it

This section is history and scholarship, not Bahá’í Writings.

Mánikchí Ṣáḥib. The introduction to The Tabernacle of Unity (the translators’ introduction, Bahá’í World Centre) says that Mánikchí Limjí Hataria (1813–1890) was born in India of Zoroastrian parents and was sent in 1854 as an emissary of the Parsees of India to help their co-religionists in Iran. He met Bahá’u’lláh in Baghdad, kept his Zoroastrian faith to the end of his life, and was a lifelong admirer. His questions led to two Tablets, the first sent in 1878. The second, revealed on 1 July 1882, is addressed to Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl, who was then Mánikchí’s personal secretary. The introduction says the Tablet of the Seven Questions was addressed to Ustád Javán-Mard, “a prominent early Bahá’í of Zoroastrian background and former student of Mánikchí Ṣáḥib.”

Yazd. Susan Maneck (Conversion of Religious Minorities to the Bahá’í Faith in Iran), quoting Adib Taherzadeh, reports that until 1882–83 no Zoroastrian in Yazd had accepted the Faith, and that the Bahá’ís did not expect any to. Moojan Momen (Iran: Province of Yazd, encyclopaedia entry) names Ustád Javánmard (d. 1928) and Arbáb Siyávash Sifídvash among the eminent converts, and says that at one stage most members of the Anjuman-i Násirí, the elected body that led the Zoroastrians of Yazd, were Bahá’ís or sympathetic to the Faith. Shoghi Effendi notes that the Parsis of Yazd, newly converted, were among those attacked: “the Jews and the Parsis who had been newly converted to the Faith, and were living, the former in Hamadán, and the latter in Yazd” (God Passes By, GPB XII.11).

Kirmán and India. Christopher Buck (Bahá’u’lláh as Zoroastrian Saviour) writes that a significant number of Zoroastrian converts, mainly from Yazd and Kirmán, acclaimed Bahá’u’lláh as Sháh Bahrám Varjávand, and that later some Írání Zoroastrians in India were also won over. He adds that few converts came from among the Parsis.

What moved them. Maneck, drawing on the convert biographies of Sulaymání, reports several accounts:

  • Mullá Bahrám, one of the first Bahá’ís of Zoroastrian background, told a mullá that the sacrifice of lives for the cause was proof of its truth. He had accepted that proof for Zoroaster and saw it again in the Bahá’í Faith. Maneck notes that the form of his argument follows Shí‘í patterns.
  • A young Zoroastrian, Ardishír, was visiting the Bahá’í Mullá ’Abdu’l-Qání, who served him tea and then drank from the same glass without washing it, setting aside the rule of ritual impurity. The host reminded him of the promise that in the Day of the Promised One the lamb and the wolf would drink from one stream.
  • Kay-Khusraw Ji Ṣáḥib, a Parsi agent, set up a body of elected laymen to manage community affairs that the priests had managed. Maneck presents this reform as part of the setting of the conversions.

Maneck writes that for Bahá’ís of Zoroastrian background Bahá’u’lláh was Shah Bahram, the figure of Zoroastrian hopes for a restoration after the Arab invasions, and that his descent from Yazdigird III was widely used. She adds that Bahá’u’lláh wrote to Zoroastrians in pure Persian without Arabic words.

The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

This section is a description from Wikipedia (articles “Yasna,” “Gathas” and “Avestan”, saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/zoroastrian/`, CC BY-SA 4.0). It is description only.

  • The Avesta is recited as a liturgy. The Yasna has 72 chapters. The middle chapters, in Old Avestan, include 17 chapters made up of the five Gathas. A trained priest recites the whole Yasna in about two hours; in its normal form it is performed in the morning.
  • The Gathas are recited in the daily Yasna liturgy and in the longer Visperad liturgy.
  • Old and Young Avestan texts became fixed liturgical languages and were handed on by rote learning. The descriptions say that correct pronunciation of the liturgy “was (and still is) considered necessary for the prayers to be effective.”
  • The Avestan script has many letters, so that recited texts could be written with precision.

The saved descriptions give no melodic scales, vocal style or instruments. They describe recitation, not song. A songwriter should not take a musical idiom from them.

What the sources do not settle

Authenticity of the Zoroastrian scriptures. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 22 June 1943, says: “We cannot be sure of the authenticity, word for word, of any of the past Holy Scriptures except the Qur’án … what we can be sure of is that when Bahá’u’lláh or the Master stated that Zoroaster foretold a Promised One’s coming, it is correct.” In a memorandum dated 27 March 1999 to the Universal House of Justice, the Research Department (not a letter of the House of Justice) quotes that letter and writes: “we do not consider authoritative the Zoroastrian scripture to which we have access today.” The memorandum adds that, according to scholars of the subject, “only the Gathas, 17 hymns which have been preserved in the ancient language spoken by Zoroaster, are attributable to Him.” These statements concern which texts can be relied on. The statements of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá are what the letter says can be relied upon.

The “Dinkird” quotation. John Ferraby’s All Things Made New (1957, p. 171; 1987 edition, p. 174) quotes, citing only “Dinkird”: “When a thousand two hundred and some years have passed from the inception of the religion of the Arabian and the overthrow of the Kingdom of Irán and the degradation of the followers of My religion, a descendant of the Iranian kings will be raised up as a prophet.” The Research Department memorandum says it “has neither the time nor the resources” to find the passage in the Denkard and calls this “a project for future Bahá’í scholars.” It notes that two passages in Muhammad Nátiq’s Al-Munáẓarátu’d-Díníyyah (Cairo, 1342 AH), cited to the Denkard, could together give a similar text. Ferraby’s book is commentary, and the source of the quotation is not identified. This page does not use it.

Scholarship on the prophecies. Christopher Buck writes that the Sháh Bahrám texts “cannot be regarded as genuine prophecies without qualification. The texts were composed after many of the events had transpired.” He also writes that the fulfilment “was not literal at the level of text,” because the texts expected the restoration of the Sasanian empire and the Zoroastrian religion. These are Buck’s views as a scholar. Buck also reports that in a tract for Zoroastrians of Kirmán and Yazd, Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl used passages from the Desatír, which Iranian Zoroastrians then accepted as authoritative, and that Buck regards the text’s authenticity as doubtful.

Saoshyant and Sháh-Bahrám. The Gathas and Yasht 19 speak of the Saoshyant. The Pahlavi books speak of Vahram the Varjavand. The texts collected here do not say whether the Writings equate the two, or how the Saoshyant is fulfilled. Shoghi Effendi’s title is Sháh-Bahrám.

The count of days. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablet gives day-to-century and day-to-year equivalences for the first two periods, and the letter of 7 August 1934 says the thirty days should not be counted numerically.

Two Manifestations. A pilgrim’s note (Honnold, Glimpses of Early Pilgrimages, p. 61) records ‘Abdu’l-Bahá as saying that the Zoroastrians await “two Manifestations,” the Báb and the Blessed Perfection. This is a pilgrim’s record of a spoken answer, not a published text of the Writings. The page does not set it beside the Sháh-Bahrám title.

Zoroaster’s date. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 30 July 1941, says: “There is no exact date in the teachings regarding the beginning of His Dispensation.”

How Zoroastrians read the promise. The sources here are Bahá’í and Bahá’í-related scholarship. They do not report how Zoroastrian scholars or priests read these texts, and nothing here shows that Zoroastrian institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.

Concrete images

  • The sacred fire, and the Tablet’s “primal Fire” that appears “with immeasurable heat.”
  • A star that falls on the night a prince is born.
  • The sun told to stand still.
  • Wolf and sheep at one stream. The first image is in the Pahlavi text; the second is a story Maneck reports from a convert biography.
  • A glass of tea shared across a rule of ritual impurity (Maneck’s report of the story of Ardishír).
  • Fire-temples that “summoned, with burning zeal.”

A possible arc

A long conflict (the three thousand years in GPB VI.14), the sign in the sky, the sun standing still, the fire appearing, and then peace described as a change of ages (“the sheep period comes on”).

What a lyric must not claim

  • That the Zoroastrian priesthood or its scholars accept the Bahá’í reading, or that the Zoroastrian books name Bahá’u’lláh.
  • That the Saoshyant is Bahá’u’lláh, or that Yasht 19 and the Gathas speak of Sháh-Bahrám. The texts gathered here do not say it.
  • That the Pahlavi books were written by Zoroaster, or that the sun literally stopped.
  • A date or a count of years for the sun sign. Use the sign without the sum.
  • The “Dinkird” quotation.
  • A general charge against priests. The address to the “high priests” was spoken to those of Bahá’u’lláh’s time, and the same Tablet says a priest who “hath seen the light” is “a source of illumination to the whole world.”
