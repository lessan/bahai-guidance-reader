The Day Foretold: The Zoroastrian Promise and Sháh-Bahrám

Last updated 17 October 2026

What this page covers

This page belongs to the series the-day-foretold, which takes the religions one at a time. The other pages are the-day-foretold-hindu, the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-buddhist, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi. The wider topic is in promises-of-the-future. For the Day that “can be missed”, and for what the Writings say about the priests who stood between people and the Day, see greatness-of-this-day-the-day-can-be-missed.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each religion. For this one he writes: “to the Zoroastrians the promised Sháh-Bahrám” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11).

The page keeps five kinds of source apart:

  1. Zoroastrian scripture, in public-domain translations of the 1880s. These are of two kinds. The Gathas and the Zamyad Yasht (Yasht 19) are Avestan texts. The Bahman Yasht and the Bundahishn are Pahlavi (Middle Persian) books, translated by E. W. West. A short section also names the Desatir, a text that Zoroastrians in Iran and India knew in the nineteenth century (see below for what the sources say about it).
  2. The Bahá’í Writings and Shoghi Effendi, which state the Bahá’í understanding of the fulfilment.
  3. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi and a Research Department memorandum to the Universal House of Justice. These are not authoritative interpretation; each is labelled where it appears.
  4. History: Adib Taherzadeh, Hasan Balyuzi, Susan Maneck (whose 1983 thesis is catalogued under the earlier name Stiles) and Moojan Momen, reported as the accounts of those authors.
  5. Commentary and scholarship by Bahá’í authors (Christopher Buck, Farshid Kazemi, Ali Kuli Khan, Karl Weaver), reported as their readings, and one Zoroastrian priest’s account (Dastur Dhalla).

The promise

The Gathas (Old Avestan)

The Gathas are the hymns that Zoroastrian tradition attributes to Zoroaster himself. They are in Old Avestan, the oldest layer of the Avesta (description from Wikipedia, “Yasna”). The translation is by Lawrence Heyworth Mills, 1887 (The Zend-Avesta, Part III, Sacred Books of the East 31). Mills puts added words in parentheses. The Gathas speak of Saoshyants, “saviours,” in the plural, and sometimes of one Saoshyant.

Zarathustra asks when they will come:

“When come, Great Giver! they who are the day’s enlighteners, to hold the Righteous Order of the world upright, and forward pressing?” (Mills, Yasna 46:3)

He prays that the Saoshyant may see the reward:

“let Thy Saoshyant see how gifts of recompense may be his own” (Mills, Yasna 48:9)

He also says who the saviours will be:

“Such shall be the Saviours of the Provinces” (Mills, Yasna 48:12)

And he names the path that their teaching makes: “the religious precepts and laws of the Saviours” (Mills, Yasna 34:13).

The Zamyad Yasht (Avestan)

Yasht 19 is also Avestan. Its sections 88–96 describe the kingly Glory that will attend the final Saoshyant, here called Astvat-ereta. The translation is by James Darmesteter, 1883 (The Zend-Avesta, Part II, Sacred Books of the East 23).

The Glory goes with him when the world is restored and the dead rise:

“when the dead will rise, when life and immortality will come, and the world will be restored at its wish” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.89)

His own description:

“a friend of Ahura Mazda, a son of Vispa-taurvairi, knowing the victorious knowledge” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.92)

“he shall look upon the whole living world with the eye of plenty, and his look shall deliver to immortality the whole of the living creatures” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.94)

He is not alone. The text names his friends: “the friends of Astvat-ereta, who are fiend-smiting, well-thinking, well-speaking, well-doing, following the good law” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.95).

The text ends with the defeat of falsehood: “the Word of falsehood smites, but the Word of truth shall smite it” (Darmesteter, Yasht 19.96). Section 90 says “the Drug shall perish.” In the Avesta Drug is the Lie.

Vahram the Varjavand and Peshyotanu (Pahlavi)

The Bahman Yasht (Zand-i Vohuman Yasn) is a Pahlavi book, not part of the Avesta. West translated it in 1880 (Pahlavi Texts, Part I, Sacred Books of the East 5). In chapter III Ohrmazd (Auharmazd) speaks to Zoroaster about the coming of Hushedar, the first of the later figures who renew the religion, of a prince named Vahram the Varjavand, and of Peshyotanu. West gives the passage on the prince’s birth:

“it is his father, a prince of the Kayan race, approaches the women, and a religious prince is born to him; he calls his name Vahram the Varjavand” (West, Bahman Yasht III.14)

“the night when that prince is born, a star falls from the sky” (West, Bahman Yasht III.15)

The text gives two kinds of task, and two figures to do them. Peshyotanu restores the religion. Ohrmazd sends the angels Neryosang and Srosh to call him forth, and in section 41 Peshyotanu speaks:

“let the throne of the religion and sovereignty have a good restorer” (West, Bahman Yasht III.41)

Vahram restores Iran. Later in the chapter he arrives with power:

“he comes forth in full glory” (West, Bahman Yasht III.39)

“he drives away from the world covetousness, want, hatred, wrath, lust, envy, and wickedness.” (West, Bahman Yasht III.39)

“the wolf period goes away, and the sheep period comes on” (West, Bahman Yasht III.40)

West adds a note that “It is Vahram’s business to restore the empire”, while another figure restores the religion. The note is West’s reading. A second note of West’s on section 32 is about the angel Vahram, who goes to help Peshyotanu: “The fact that the angel Vahram goes in his spiritual form to the assistance of Peshyotanu, rather militates against the idea that he also goes in the form of Vahram the Varjavand.” West also notes that the prince “may possibly be an incarnation of the angel Bahram”, with some reminiscences of a Persian general of the same name.

The same chapter has a sign about the sun. Hushedar “cries to the sun with the swift horse” (West, Bahman Yasht III.45), and section 46 begins “The sun with the swift horse stands still ten”. The saved page ends at that point. The sun standing still is the sign that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá later interprets (see below).

The Saoshyant at the renovation (Pahlavi)

In the Bundahishn, chapter XXX, the final Saoshyant appears in West’s spelling as Soshyans. After he comes, the dead are raised:

“in the fifty-seven years of Soshyans they prepare all the dead, and all men stand up” (West, Bundahishn XXX.7)

He and his assistants renew the world, and “give every man the reward and recompense suitable to his deeds” (West, Bundahishn XXX.27).

How the figures are arranged

The Gathas speak of Saoshyants. Yasht 19 describes the final Saoshyant. The Pahlavi books add Hushedar, Hushedar-mah, Vahram the Varjavand and Peshyotanu, and place them in a sequence of millennia. In sections 13–46 of Bahman Yasht chapter III, the part saved for this page, the Saoshyant does not appear, and Vahram and Peshyotanu are two figures with two tasks.

Christopher Buck, writing as a scholar and not from the Writings, says that “later Zoroastrian tradition has associated” the final Saoshyant “with the advent of Sháh Bahrám Varjávand.” Buck cites Mary Boyce for the picture that the hope of ordinary people was fixed on the one Saoshyant, who will be “mightily helped, it is believed, by Vahrám”, the angel (yazad) of Victory. Buck also counts five saviours in the Zand í Vahman Yasn: Hushedar, Peshyotanu and Saoshyans, with the Sasanian kings Shapur and Bahrám V (Varjávand). No Zoroastrian text and no Bahá’í authoritative text gathered for this page states that the Saoshyant of the Gathas and Yasht 19 and Sháh-Bahrám are one figure. Shoghi Effendi’s phrase “the promised Sháh-Bahrám” corresponds to the name Vahram (Bahrám) in the Pahlavi tradition.

The Desatir, as Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl used it

The Desatir is a book of sacred writings said to come from ancient Persian prophets. This page uses Mulla Firuz’s English translation of 1818. In the Book of Sasan the First, the commentary says that after Alexander’s conquest of Iran, Sasan the son of Darab went to India, and that God said: “And now I will raise up one of thy relatives of the Kyani race, a man upright in word and deed, that he may assume the government of the kingdom”. The verses that follow (75–77 in this translation) promise that one of Sasan’s people will be raised up and that prophecy will not be taken from his children again.

Susan Maneck writes that the Desatir belongs to a school that flourished in Mughal India in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and that “no mention of it can be found in pre-Mughal literature.” Maneck’s account is in an email discussion compiled on bahai-library.com (2011). Christopher Buck calls it “a late pious fraud”, and reports that Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl used it in a tract for the Zoroastrians of Kirmán and Yazd because Iranian Zoroastrians accepted it as authoritative. This page names the Desatir for that reason and does not use it as scripture.

The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The title and the descent

Shoghi Effendi gives the title in God Passes By (GPB VI.11): “to the Zoroastrians the promised Sháh-Bahrám.” In the same chapter he records Bahá’u’lláh’s descent:

“He derived His descent, on the one hand, from Abraham (the Father of the Faithful) through his wife Katurah, and on the other from Zoroaster, as well as from Yazdigird, the last king of the Sásáníyán dynasty.” (God Passes By, GPB VI.12)

The sentence states the descent. It does not say that the descent fulfils the prophecy. Adib Taherzadeh adds that Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl “through extensive historical research has verified” the descent from Zoroaster and the Sasanian kings (The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1, p. 8). The seventh of the seven questions in Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablet to Ustád Javán-Mard “regardeth the name, lineage, and ancestry of the Holy One”, and Bahá’u’lláh refers the questioner to what Abu’l-Faḍl “hath written in this regard, based on the Sacred Scriptures” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.14).

Three thousand years and the sun

Shoghi Effendi writes of Bahá’u’lláh’s Dispensation:

“To His Dispensation the sacred books of the followers of Zoroaster had referred as that in which the sun must needs be brought to a standstill for no less than one whole month. To Him Zoroaster must have alluded when, according to tradition, He foretold that a period of three thousand years of conflict and contention must needs precede the advent of the World-Savior Sháh-Bahrám, Who would triumph over Ahriman and usher in an era of blessedness and peace.” (God Passes By, GPB VI.14)

Here the Guardian cites the sun sign and the three thousand years “according to tradition.”

Bahá’u’lláh’s answer to the question about Sháh Bahrám

The Tablet of the Seven Questions (Lawḥ-i-Haft Pursish) was revealed in answer to Ustád Javán-Mard, a Zoroastrian who became a Bahá’í (see below). It is in The Tabernacle of Unity. The fourth question reads:

“Our Books have announced that Sháh Bahrám will come, invested with manifold signs, to guide the people aright” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.9)

Bahá’u’lláh answers:

“Whatsoever hath been announced in the Books hath been revealed and made clear. From every direction the signs have been manifested. The Omnipotent One is calling, in this day, and announcing the appearance of the Supreme Heaven.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.10)

The answer goes on: “The world hath been illumined with the splendours of His revelation, yet how few are the eyes that can behold it!” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.10). The same paragraph asks that God “bestow a penetrating insight upon His servants”.

The first question, about the tongue and direction of worship, receives an address to the priests, which Bahá’u’lláh tells the questioner to speak:

“O high priests! Ears have been given you that they may hearken unto the mystery of Him Who is the Self-Dependent, and eyes that they may behold Him. Wherefore flee ye? The Incomparable Friend is manifest. He speaketh that wherein lieth salvation.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.4)

Shoghi Effendi quotes this Tablet in The Promised Day Is Come. He writes that Bahá’u’lláh’s voice, “identifying itself with the voice of the promised Sháh-Bahrám,” addressed the priestly caste, “holding sacerdotal supremacy over the followers of the Faith of Zoroaster” (The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 192). He also writes that Bahá’u’lláh, “replying to a Zoroastrian who had inquired regarding the promised Sháh-Bahrám,” has written the words of TU 3.10 quoted above (PDC 192).

The fifth question, about the Bridge of Ṣiráṭ, Paradise and Hell, is answered in the same Tablet. It says that “The Day of God’s Revelation is the Day of the most great Resurrection” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 3.11). For that theme see greatness-of-this-day-the-resurrection.

The Fire

The Zoroastrian tradition keeps a sacred fire. Bahá’u’lláh writes of the Fire in several Tablets to believers of Zoroastrian background in The Tabernacle of Unity. The Tablet numbered 5 in the volume is the Lawḥ-i-Dustán-i-Yazdání, the Tablet to the Zoroastrians (bahai-library.com cross-references it to this authorised translation). It begins with heat and motion, and then says:

“This is the mystery enshrined in your Book that was sent down aforetime, a mystery which hath until now remained concealed from the eyes and hearts of men. That primal Fire hath in this Day appeared with a new radiance and with immeasurable heat. This divine Fire burneth of itself, with neither fuel nor fume, that it might draw away such excess moisture and cold as are the cause of torpor and weariness, of lethargy and despondency, and lead the entire creation to the court of the presence of the All-Merciful.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.2)

Another Tablet in the same group speaks in the same terms:

“The fire that consumeth all veils hath been kindled by My hand; quench it not with the waters of ignorance.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 4.2)

“Open your inner eye, that ye may behold the celestial Fire, and listen with the ear of inner understanding, that ye may hear the delightsome words of the Beloved.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 4.3)

Shoghi Effendi names the Fire in God Passes By: the “Most Great Spirit” is “symbolized in the Zoroastrian, the Mosaic, the Christian, and Muḥammadan Dispensations by the Sacred Fire, the Burning Bush, the Dove and the Angel Gabriel respectively” (God Passes By, GPB VI.26). He also writes that the circumstances in which Bahá’u’lláh received the first intimations of His mission recall, and surpass in poignancy, “the soul-shaking experience of Moses when confronted by the Burning Bush”, and that of “Zoroaster when awakened to His mission by a succession of seven visions” (God Passes By, GPB VI.8).

The Tablet to the Zoroastrians speaks also of the priests, and of the Book:

“In your Book it is stated that the high priests will, on that day, lead men far astray, and will prevent them from drawing nigh unto Him. He indeed is a high priest who hath seen the light and hastened unto the way leading to the Beloved. Such a man is a benevolent priest and a source of illumination to the whole world.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.3)

“Any priest who leadeth thee away from this Fire, which is the reality of the Light and the mystery of divine Revelation, is indeed thine enemy.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.4)

“the glad-tidings We had imparted in the Book have appeared in full effect” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.9)

The Tablet does not name “the Book” in these sentences, and the sources collected here do not identify the text from which the saying about the high priests comes. The provisional translation of the Persian by Juan Cole (1997) adds a bracketed gloss, “your book [the Avesta]”. It renders the saying: “The Zoroastrian priests in that day shall keep the people distant and prevent them from drawing near.” This is a provisional translation by Juan Cole, and the gloss is Cole’s.

The Tablet closes with a call to the priests to look again:

“Say: O high priests! The Hand of Omnipotence is stretched forth from behind the clouds; behold ye it with new eyes. The tokens of His majesty and greatness are unveiled; gaze ye on them with pure eyes.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.11)

And with a call to act: “The day of deeds hath come: Now is not the time for words.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 5.5)

Bahá’u’lláh to Mánikchí Ṣáḥib: the Prophets are one

The introduction to The Tabernacle of Unity says that Bahá’u’lláh’s first Tablet to Mánikchí Ṣáḥib (sent in 1878) and a second, longer Tablet addressed to Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl (revealed on 1 July 1882) answer his questions about religion. Three passages bear on the promise.

Mánikchí Ṣáḥib asked how to judge between the Prophets of Mahábád together with Zoroaster, who “were twenty-eight in number”, and the later Prophets who each brought a new law. Bahá’u’lláh answers that the diverse statements are “to be seen as concordant letters, that is, letters that form a single word” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 2.4). On the claim that one’s own religion excels every other, He says that the Prophets are one: “All have proceeded from God, unto Him have they summoned all men, and unto Him have they returned” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 2.45).

On the question whether Hindus and Zoroastrians may welcome outsiders, He writes of the fire-temples:

“The fire-temples of the world stand as eloquent testimony to this truth. In their time they summoned, with burning zeal, all the inhabitants of the earth to Him Who is the Spirit of purity.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 2.40)

And in the first Tablet He writes of the Light itself:

“At a time when darkness had encompassed the world, the ocean of divine favour surged and His Light was made manifest, that the doings of men might be laid bare. This, verily, is that Light which hath been foretold in the heavenly scriptures.” (The Tabernacle of Unity, TU 1.12)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on the sun standing still

A Zoroastrian believer wrote to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá about the prophecy. His Tablet in reply is quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh (WOB 6.13):

“Thou hadst written that in the sacred books of the followers of Zoroaster it is written that in the latter days, in three separate Dispensations, the sun must needs be brought to a standstill. In the first Dispensation, it is predicted, the sun will remain motionless for ten days; in the second for twice that time; in the third for no less than one whole month.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá reads the three Dispensations as the Muḥammadan Dispensation, the Dispensation of the Báb and the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh. In His reading the Sun of Truth “stood still” for the first period of ten days, “Each day is reckoned as one century,” and “The Muḥammadan Dispensation must have, therefore, lasted no less than one thousand years.” The Báb’s Dispensation “began in the year 1260 A.H. and was brought to a close in the year 1280 A.H.” For the third:

“As to the third Dispensation—the Revelation proclaimed by Bahá’u’lláh—inasmuch as the Sun of Truth when attaining that station shineth in the plenitude of its meridian splendor its duration hath been fixed for a period of one whole month, which is the maximum time taken by the sun to pass through a sign of the Zodiac. From this thou canst imagine the magnitude of the Bahá’í cycle—a cycle that must extend over a period of at least five hundred thousand years.” (WOB 6.13, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá)

Two letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi discuss how to count the days. A letter of 7 August 1934 to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States and Canada says the first ten days are each a century, “making thus a total of one thousand lunar years”, and that the twenty days of the Bábí Dispensation are each “only one lunar year”. It then says: “The thirty days in the last Dispensation should not be reckoned numerically, but should be considered as symbolizing the incomparable greatness of the Bahá’í Revelation.” It adds that the thirty days represent “the highest, though not the final stage in the spiritual evolution of mankind.” A letter of 16 April 1951 says: “the language of prophecy has always in the past been veiled in meaning.” In the same letter the writer asks: “Could anybody find a more logical interpretation of this allusion in the Zoroastrian literature than that given by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá”.

Two Manifestations

‘Abdu’l-Bahá says in Some Answered Questions that all peoples await two Manifestations, and He includes the Zoroastrians:

“All the peoples of the world are awaiting two Manifestations, Who must be contemporaneous. This is what they all have been promised.” (Some Answered Questions, SAQ 10.8)

“The same holds true of the Zoroastrians and others, but to belabour this matter would prolong our discourse.” (Some Answered Questions, SAQ 10.8)

A pilgrim’s note (Honnold, Glimpses of Early Pilgrimages, p. 61) records ‘Abdu’l-Bahá as saying that the Zoroastrians await “two Manifestations” in the Zend Avesta. A pilgrim’s record is not a published text of the Writings; Some Answered Questions is.

Zoroaster’s station

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (10 November 1939) says that Buddha and Zoroaster were “both of Whom were divinely appointed and fully independent Manifestations of God”. Another (13 July 1938) lists Zoroaster among the Prophets before the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh. In a talk ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says that the people of religion are of two kinds, “Some worship the sun, and some adore the dawning points from which the sun rises”, and that “the Zoroastrians” turn to “that of Zoroaster” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 93.11).

A talk on the Day foretold

In a talk at 309 West Seventy-eighth Street, New York, on 5 July 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, the Zend-Avesta is among the books named: “These things are recorded in the Torah, or Old Testament, in the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta, and the books of Buddha.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.1)

How Bahá’í authors and scholars have read the prophecies

This section is commentary and scholarship. Nothing here is authoritative interpretation. Where the Writings make the same reading, the section above gives the Writings. Quotations from the authors are short and inside the sentence.

Christopher Buck, “Bahá’u’lláh as Zoroastrian Saviour” (1998)

Buck gave the paper at the American Academy of Religion in 1998. It sets modern scholarship on the Pahlavi apocalypses beside the Bahá’í claim. Buck’s main points:

  • The Pahlavi texts were composed after the events they foretell. Buck writes that the Sháh Bahrám texts “cannot be regarded as genuine prophecies without qualification. The texts were composed after many of the events had transpired.” Buck dates some of these apocalypses to the 9th–10th centuries, after the Arab conquest, and describes them as lamentations over the fall of the Sasanian empire and of the priestly order. Buck quotes Czeglédy on the vaticinatio ex eventu form.
  • Sháh Bahrám is a composite. Buck draws on Cereti, Hultgård, Frye and Boyce to say that the figure echoes the general Bahrám Cóbín (589 CE), Bahrám Gór, and possibly a son of Yazdegard III who went to China. The texts picture him returning from the east with an army, and Buck says that this is a hope for a restored empire and restored priesthood.
  • The fulfilment was not literal. Buck writes that “Bahá’u’lláh accomplished none of the prodigies that Zoroastrian texts required, nor did Bahá’u’lláh overthrow the oppressors.” Instead of restoring Sasanian Zoroastrianism He brought a new religion, which “did represent itself as the renewal of the old Zoroastrian religion.” Buck concludes that the prediction is, in literal terms, “a failed prophecy”, and that the Zoroastrians who accepted Bahá’u’lláh “were obliged to abandon particular aspirations for universal ones”. This is Buck’s conclusion as a scholar. It is not the Bahá’í understanding, which rests on the statements in the section above.
  • The hope was real. Buck cites Edward Granville Browne, who in 1887 met a dastur “very full of a rare old book called the Jamasp-Name”. The dastur described it as containing “the announcement of the return of Sháh Bahrám, the Zoroastrian Messiah, to re-establish ‘the Good Religion.’” Buck’s own words on the matter: “the messianic hope of Zoroastrians was as genuine as it was long-standing.”
  • The sun sign is accurately reported. Buck writes that what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Zoroastrian correspondent put to Him “is quite accurate”. Buck quotes, from Dhalla’s account of the Pahlavi tradition: “the sun will stand in the midst of the sky in the time of Oshedar Bami [Hushédar Bámí] for 10 days and in the time of Oshedar Mah [Hushédar Máh] for 20 days and in the time of Soshyosh [the final Soshyant or saviour] for 30 days.” In that tradition, then, the thirty days belong to the time of Soshyosh, the last Saoshyant. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablet places the thirty days in the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh. Shoghi Effendi’s title for Bahá’u’lláh is Sháh-Bahrám. No authoritative text says what follows from the two placements together.
  • A reading of the Tablet to the priests. Buck reads TU 3.4 as a call to the priests to fulfil the religion’s highest ideals “by abandoning it”, and adds that every Bahá’í doctrinally “becomes a Zoroastrian to the extent that he or she accepts the authenticity of the ancient Persian prophet.” That is Buck’s interpretation. The Tablet itself says the priests should recognise the Friend and help Him.

Farshid Kazemi, “Celestial Fire” (Lights of ‘Irfán 14, 2013; first presented 2009)

Kazemi starts from the Fire in the Tablets to Zoroastrians. Kazemi writes that Bahá’í scholarship on the Zoroastrian apocalypse “largely” focuses on Sháh Bahram, and that the expectation of the divine Fire (atar) in the Gathas and later sources has “gone unnoticed”. Kazemi’s claim is that in Tablets that include TU 4 and 5 Bahá’u’lláh identifies Himself as the Mazdean Fire that the scriptures foretold, and that the Saoshyant, “possessed of the fiery Khvarnah”, is the figure who will renew the world at the end of time.

Kazemi reads TU 5.2 on that ground: the “mystery” there “does not refer to Sháh Bahrám, but to the divine Fire”, because the expectation of Sháh Bahrám “was neither a mystery nor a secret” but widespread in nineteenth-century Iran. This is Kazemi’s reading. The Tablet says “the mystery enshrined in your Book” and does not itself name Sháh Bahrám or the Saoshyant. The Tablet of the Seven Questions answers a question about Sháh Bahrám directly (TU 3.9–3.10), and both Tablets are authoritative; the page records Kazemi’s distinction as his own.

Ali Kuli Khan, a talk on Zoroaster (30 August 1945)

The file of Khan’s essay “Zoroaster: The Prophet of Ancient Iran and His Book, Zend-Avesta” is a poor scan, so this page paraphrases it. A note at the end says it was delivered at a Bahá’í centre in Massachusetts on the evening of 30 August 1945. Khan writes that the Zoroaster of the Avesta appeared 3000 years before the rise of the Báb, and that Bahá’ís base this on a prophecy of Zoroaster about four equal sections of 12,000 years. The number agrees with Shoghi Effendi’s “three thousand years” (GPB VI.14), but the Guardian cites it as tradition, and the calculation is Khan’s own.

Karl Weaver, “Explanation of a Zoroastrian Prophecy” (2017)

Weaver sets ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s text beside astronomy. For the third period he proposes that one “day” is one full cycle of the precession of the equinoxes (about 25,772 years), so that thirty days give 773,160 years, which is “substantially more than” the figure of at least five hundred thousand years in the Tablet. This is Weaver’s calculation. The letter of 7 August 1934 says the thirty days “should not be reckoned numerically”. The page does not use the sum.

Susan Maneck on how the prophecies were read

In a 1990 article Maneck writes that Jews and Zoroastrians who became Bahá’ís “had, before their conversion, diligently searched through their respective scriptures for signs of the advent of the promised one”, and that eschatology “provided one of the primary bridges between the Bahá’ís and those of other communities.” Maneck’s account of the conversions is in the next section.

A Zoroastrian priest: Dastur Dhalla

Dastur Maneckji Nusserwanji Dhalla, whom Maneck calls “the eminent Zoroastrian theologian”, devotes a chapter of the autobiography to Bahá’í conversions (Dastur Dhalla: The Saga of a Soul, translated 1975; chapter 68). This is the only Zoroastrian account in the sources gathered here, and it is quoted so that the page does not speak for Zoroastrians. Dhalla describes the Sháh Bahrám prophecies as found “in some of the unauthentic Pazand and Pahlavi books written after we lost our kingdom”. Dhalla does not accept the Bahá’í reading of them, treats the conversions as a loss to the Zoroastrian community, and writes that efforts should be made to bring converts back. Dhalla also reports that on a visit to Iran in 1920 Dhalla found in Qazvin “that all Iranians credited as Zoroastrians had already become Bahá’ís.” This page reports Dhalla’s view as Dhalla’s view. Dhalla’s figure, cited by Maneck, is about 4000 Zoroastrian converts in Iran and 1000 in India, and Maneck says that such figures, “based largely on the impressions of outside observers”, are open to question.

Those who recognised it

This section is history. It is drawn from Taherzadeh, Balyuzi, Maneck and Momen, and from the introduction to The Tabernacle of Unity. The one text from the Writings is ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s story of the man in Káshán, quoted by Taherzadeh.

Mánikchí Ṣáḥib. The introduction to The Tabernacle of Unity says that Mánikchí Limjí Hataria (1813–1890) was born in India of Zoroastrian parents and was sent in 1854 as an emissary of the Parsees of India to help their co-religionists in Iran. He met Bahá’u’lláh in Baghdad. He “was attracted to the teachings of the new religion and, moved by the sacrifice of its early martyrs, became a lifelong admirer”, but kept his Zoroastrian faith to the end of his life. Taherzadeh says: “Although Manikchi did not become a Baha’i, he remained a sympathetic friend.” Years after the meeting he asked Bahá’u’lláh a series of questions. These led to two Tablets, the first sent in 1878 and revealed in pure Persian at his request. The second, revealed on 1 July 1882, is addressed to Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl, who was then Mánikchí’s personal secretary. According to Taherzadeh, Mánikchí also obtained a royal decree that removed a religious tax from the Zoroastrians and set up the Council of Zoroastrians in Yazd.

Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl. Maneck’s 1983 thesis says that around 1876 Abu’l-Faḍl was dismissed from a teaching post in Tehran when his conversion became known. Mánikchí, who knew his skill in pure Persian, hired him to teach Persian literature in a new school and to act as his secretary. Many of his Zoroastrian students became devoted to him and were among the first Zoroastrian Bahá’ís. In Balyuzi’s Eminent Bahá’ís in the Time of Bahá’u’lláh, Abu’l-Faḍl describes how he reached the conclusion that Bahá’u’lláh’s ancestry went back to the Sasanian kings. He had noted the Desatir, and then something further:

“And in other books of the Parsis I had observed amongst the tidings which they carry the definite statement that this bounty shall be realized after the passage of twelve hundred and some more years from the time of the inception of the Faith of Islam” (Balyuzi, Eminent Bahá’ís, quoting Abu’l-Faḍl)

Abu’l-Faḍl does not name those books. This is the nearest published statement of a “twelve hundred and some years” claim from a Bahá’í of the period, and it is discussed again below under the “Dinkird” quotation.

Káshán: the first to believe. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s account, in A Traveller’s Narrative (p. 34) and quoted by Taherzadeh, tells of the Bábí period. A Bábí in Káshán had his property plundered, was stripped, beaten and paraded through the streets on an ass to the sound of drums and trumpets. A Zoroastrian sitting apart in a caravanserai heard the noise, went out, learned the cause, and “that very day entered the society of the Babis”, saying:

“This very ill-usage and public humiliation is a proof of truth and the very best of arguments. Had it not been thus it might have been that a thousand years would have passed ere one like me became informed.” (Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 3, pp. 268–269, quoting ‘Abdu’l-Bahá)

Taherzadeh adds that the historian Mu’ínu’s-Salṭanih names the man as Suhráb-i-Púr-Kávús, and says that not much is known about him. The first Zoroastrian to believe in Bahá’u’lláh’s time is reputed to be Kay-Khusraw-i-Khudádád, a native of Yazd who traded in Káshán and became a believer after seeing a believer tortured and put to death. Maneck says he kept a textile store there. Momen agrees: “The first conversion occurred in Káshán”.

Mullá Bahrám and Yazd. Mullá Bahrám Akhtar-Khávarí (d. 1349/1930) was a Zoroastrian of Yazd who worked for Kay-Khusraw in Káshán. Maneck (1983) says that in 1879 Kay-Khusraw, upset by news that two Bahá’ís had been killed in Isfahán, told him about the Faith and stressed how it fulfilled the prophecies of the past. Mullá Bahrám went back to Yazd, and Bahá’ís there introduced him to the teacher Ḥájí Muḥammad-Ṭáhir-i-Málmírí, who was hiding in the house of Ustád ‘Alí-Askar. Ḥájí Muḥammad-Ṭáhir wrote of the meeting. His memoir, quoted by Taherzadeh (vol. 2, pp. 103–104):

“There is a Zoroastrian youth by the name of Bahram [later known as Mulla Bahram] who comes to the door periodically to sell beetroot to us. He is a very nice young man.”

He recalls that “Up to that time no one from among the Zoroastrians [in Yazd] had accepted the Faith.” The Bahá’ís “could not even imagine that these people would embrace the Faith, because they were not involved in the early history and events associated with the Manifestations of God”. After the talk, Mullá Bahrám “came the next day, and after a few days he acknowledged the truth of the Cause of Bahá’u’lláh”, and “often wept aloud”. He brought a second Zoroastrian, Áqá Rustam-i-Khursand, with him. Momen says that Mullá Bahrám, “since he had a thorough knowledge of Zoroastrian prophecies”, worked with Ḥájí Qalandar to reach the Zoroastrians of Yazd and the villages around. Maneck’s thesis adds that at a meeting Ḥájí Qalandar showed from the prophecies that Bahá’u’lláh had fulfilled them one by one, and read a Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh in pure Persian that “greatly moved Mulla Bahram”.

The date differs between authors. Taherzadeh’s quoted passage gives no year. Maneck’s 1990 article inserts “[1882-83]” after “Up to that time” in the same quotation, so Maneck dates the meeting there, and dates the first telling in Káshán to 1879. Momen says that Mullá Bahrám “became a Bahá’í in about 1298/1881”. The three dates concern different events (first hearing in Káshán, the meeting in Yazd, the acceptance) and the sources do not settle which one each author means.

Mullá Bahrám’s own account of why he accepted, from the biographer Sulaymání and quoted by Maneck, treats Zoroaster’s story as proof of a pattern: souls were sacrificed in the path of his cause. He writes: “These same proofs I had accepted for Zoroastrianism I saw demonstrated with my own eyes in this blessed Cause. For holy souls to sacrifice their very lives is the greatest act in the world”. Maneck notes that the form of this argument follows Shí‘í patterns. Maneck’s thesis, from the biography by Sulaymání, adds that he dreamed of two men in green turbans the night before two Bahá’í poets, fleeing persecution in Isfahán, reached his village, and that a break with his wife (who stayed hostile to the Bahá’ís) ended only after ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote him a letter that began by saying that astronomers call the star Bahrám (Mars) quarrelsome and hot-tempered. The thesis text is an imperfect scan, so the page gives only the outline.

Ustád Javán-Mard. Ustád Javánmard (d. 1347/1928) was one of Abu’l-Faḍl’s students. Taherzadeh says that Bahá’u’lláh gave him the name Shír-Mard, “Lion of a man”, and that he was secretary of the Council of Zoroastrians in Yazd. Maneck says he became principal of the first Zoroastrian school in Yazd, and stayed a prominent member of his community. He wrote to Bahá’u’lláh with questions on ritual, relations with other religions, eschatology and Bahá’u’lláh’s genealogy. The Tablet of the Seven Questions answers him, and Maneck says that Bahá’u’lláh referred him on the genealogy to Abu’l-Faḍl’s work tracing the descent to Yazdigird III. Taherzadeh says that Shír-Mard was the first Zoroastrian Bahá’í buried in the earth, not exposed in the dakhma, the open tower where Zoroastrians place their dead. Other Zoroastrian-background Bahá’ís made a Bahá’í cemetery, which was attacked, and some graves were desecrated.

Siyávash Sifídvash. Maneck’s thesis, using his autobiography, describes Arbáb Siyávash Sifídvash of Yazd. As a child he heard that the advent of the Saoshyant was near. His father, a merchant in Tehran, had watched the executions of the Bábís in 1852. Siyávash was given a copy of the Kitáb-i-Íqán in Iraq and gave it back when Qur’án quotations began. (Maneck notes that the Íqán makes no mention of Zoroaster or Zoroastrian prophecies.) The Báb was a descendant of Muḥammad, and that made him reluctant. In 1895 he and three other Zoroastrian merchants traded in Qum, where the only people willing to associate with them were Bahá’ís. When his father died, a Jewish Bahá’í buried him. According to Maneck, he was most impressed by Bahá’u’lláh’s Sasanian genealogy. After reading the Writings, including the Tablet of the Seven Questions, he and his three companions became Bahá’ís in Qum.

Hospitality as proof. Maneck’s 1990 article tells of a young Zoroastrian, Ardishír, who visited the Bahá’í Mullá ‘Abdu’l-Qání. The host served him tea, then “deliberately ignoring the strictures of ritual uncleanliness, drank out of the same glass after him without washing it.” He said:

“You must have heard how, in the days of the advent of the Promised Lord, the lamb and the wolf will drink from the same stream and graze in the same meadow. Do you still doubt that we are living in that Day?” (Sulaymání, quoted by Maneck)

Maneck says that the kindness of the Bahá’ís, in contrast to the Muslims, impressed the Zoroastrians. In a 1990 article Maneck adds that the use of pure Persian in Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablets to Zoroastrians, and the genealogy, helped to present the Faith “as the culmination of all religious traditions.” Hájí Mírzá Haydar-‘Alí, quoted in Maneck’s thesis, found in Káshán friends of “Jewish, Christian, Zoroastrian and Muslim” backgrounds, and “one could not tell them apart.”

The Council and the Anjuman. Taherzadeh writes that the Council of Zoroastrians in Yazd had at one time nineteen councillors, six of whom became Bahá’ís. Maneck, drawing on Mullá Bahrám’s memoirs, says that of 23 members originally elected to the Anjuman, the majority were Bahá’ís or very sympathetic, and that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá congratulated the community by letter. Momen says that “at one stage” most members of the Anjuman-i Násirí were Bahá’ís or sympathisers. The counts differ and may describe different periods.

Opposition. Taherzadeh writes that the high priests were angered by the “mere act of conversion”, which had not happened since the early days of Islam, and by converts who “openly proclaimed to the community that the Shah-Bahram, the Promised One of the Zoroastrians, had been manifested in the person of Bahá’u’lláh.” A distinguished teacher and Council member, Master Khudábakhsh, “although not officially a Baha’i”, was shot dead for his sympathy. Maneck says the chief priest used the persecutions of 1891 as a pretext to draw up a list of Zoroastrian Bahá’ís. Shoghi Effendi notes the attacks on “the Jews and the Parsis who had been newly converted to the Faith, and were living, the former in Hamadán, and the latter in Yazd” (God Passes By, GPB XII.11).

Kirmán and India. Christopher Buck writes that a significant number of Zoroastrian converts, mainly from Yazd and Kirmán, acclaimed Bahá’u’lláh as Sháh Bahrám Varjávand, and that later some Írání Zoroastrians in India were also won over. Buck adds that few converts came from among the Parsis.

The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

This section is a description from Wikipedia (articles “Yasna,” “Gathas” and “Avestan”, saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/zoroastrian/`, CC BY-SA 4.0). It is description only.

  • The Avesta is recited as a liturgy. The Yasna has 72 chapters. The middle chapters, in Old Avestan, include 17 chapters made up of the five Gathas. A trained priest recites the whole Yasna in about two hours; in its normal form it is performed in the morning.
  • The Gathas are recited in the daily Yasna liturgy and in the longer Visperad liturgy.
  • Old and Young Avestan texts became fixed liturgical languages and were handed on by rote learning. The descriptions say that correct pronunciation of the liturgy “was (and still is) considered necessary for the prayers to be effective.”
  • The Avestan script has many letters, so that recited texts could be written with precision.

The saved descriptions give no melodic scales, vocal style or instruments. They describe recitation, not song. A songwriter should not take a musical idiom from them.

What the sources do not settle

Authenticity of the Zoroastrian scriptures. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 22 June 1943, says: “We cannot be sure of the authenticity, word for word, of any of the past Holy Scriptures except the Qur’án … what we can be sure of is that when Bahá’u’lláh or the Master stated that Zoroaster foretold a Promised One’s coming, it is correct.” A letter of 30 July 1941 says that “the Avesta is not to be regarded as the authentic compilation of the writings of the Prophet.” In a memorandum dated 27 March 1999 to the Universal House of Justice, the Research Department (not a letter of the House of Justice) quotes the 1943 letter and writes: “we do not consider authoritative the Zoroastrian scripture to which we have access today.” The memorandum adds that, according to scholars of the subject, “only the Gathas, 17 hymns which have been preserved in the ancient language spoken by Zoroaster, are attributable to Him.” These statements concern which texts can be relied on. The statements of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá are what the 1943 letter says can be relied upon.

The “Dinkird” quotation (Ferraby). John Ferraby’s All Things Made New (1957, p. 171; 1975, p. 171; 1987, p. 174) quotes, citing only “Dinkird”: “When a thousand two hundred and some years have passed from the inception of the religion of the Arabian and the overthrow of the Kingdom of Irán and the degradation of the followers of My religion, a descendant of the Iranian kings will be raised up as a prophet.” What the sources now show:

  • It is not identified in the Denkard. The Research Department memorandum says it “has neither the time nor the resources” to find the passage in the Denkard and calls this “a project for future Bahá’í scholars.” The inquirer who raised the question had looked in a web version of the 1896 translation and found nothing like it. The memorandum notes that two passages in Muhammad Nátiq’s Al-Munáẓarátu’d-Díníyyah (Cairo, 1342 AH), both cited to the Denkard with no further reference, “if combined, appear to provide a similar text.” The photocopy of that page is an image and is not in the corpus.
  • The same wording appears elsewhere only as a copy. A pre-publication edition of Ruhi Book 9 in the corpus lists the passage under “Dinkird”, with the same words. It adds no new source.
  • The Desatir is the nearest parallel, and it does not contain the figure. In Mulla Firuz’s 1818 translation, the Book of Sasan the First promises that one of Sasan’s people will be raised up (commentary and verses 75–77, above). It has no “twelve hundred” figure, no sentence on the overthrow of the Kingdom of Irán, and no prophet “from the Iranian kings”.
  • Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl is the earliest Bahá’í use found of both ideas. Balyuzi quotes him citing the Desatir verses, and then, separately, “other books of the Parsis” for the claim of “twelve hundred and some more years” after the inception of Islam, “before 1300 years have come to pass from the birth of Islam”. He does not name those books. Buck mentions the Jamasp-Name as a Zoroastrian book of prophecies known in the nineteenth century, and the Research Department does not name it. The page does not claim that the Ferraby wording comes from it.
  • Net. The origin of Ferraby’s wording is not shown. A Persian-language Bahá’í source based on Parsi books is one possibility, and it is only a possibility. Ferraby’s book is commentary. This page does not use the quotation as scripture or as a Zoroastrian prophecy.

Saoshyant and Sháh-Bahrám. What the texts read show: in sections 13–46 of Bahman Yasht chapter III the Saoshyant is not named, and Vahram the Varjavand and Peshyotanu are separate figures with separate tasks. Buck, citing Boyce, reports that later Zoroastrian tradition links the Saoshyant’s advent with Sháh Bahrám and that Vahram helps the Saoshyant. No Zoroastrian or Bahá’í authoritative text found equates the two. Shoghi Effendi’s title is Sháh-Bahrám. Kazemi reads the Fire and the Saoshyant as what the Tablets point to and treats Sháh Bahrám as a separate expectation; Buck and others focus on Sháh Bahrám. What is not settled: how the Writings relate the Saoshyant to Bahá’u’lláh, and whether the sources not yet read (the rest of Bahman Yasht chapter III, chapter VII, Bundahishn chapter 33, and Maneck’s book The Death of Ahriman, 1997, not in the corpus) link the two.

The count of days. Settled in part. The letter of 7 August 1934 explains the first two periods (ten days as ten centuries, twenty days as twenty years, matching ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s dates of 1260 and 1280 AH for the Báb’s Dispensation) and says the thirty days “should not be reckoned numerically”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Himself draws only the conclusion of “at least five hundred thousand years” for the Bahá’í cycle. Weaver’s sum of 773,160 years is Weaver’s own and goes beyond both.

Where the sign sits in the Zoroastrian tradition. Buck, quoting Dhalla, places the thirty days in the time of Soshyosh. The Writings place them in the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh and call Bahá’u’lláh Sháh-Bahrám. The sources do not say whether ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reading was meant to address this.

Two Manifestations. Settled that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names the Zoroastrians among the peoples who await two Manifestations (SAQ 10.8), with a pilgrim’s note in agreement. Not settled: which Zoroastrian figure or title belongs to the Báb and which to Bahá’u’lláh. The page does not set the two Manifestations beside the Sháh-Bahrám title.

What “your Book” and the saying about the high priests are. The Tablet to the Zoroastrians (TU 5.2–5.3) cites “your Book” for the mystery of the Fire and for a saying about the high priests. Cole glosses “the Avesta”. No passage of the Avesta or the Pahlavi books gathered here has been matched to the saying.

Zoroaster’s date. A letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 30 July 1941, says: “There is no exact date in the teachings regarding the beginning of His Dispensation.” Buck reports a scholarly “near consensus” of 1200–1000 BCE; Khan puts Zoroaster 3000 years before the Báb. These do not agree, and the Writings give no date.

Dates of the first conversions in Yazd. Maneck (1879 for the first telling, 1882–83 for the meeting in Yazd) and Momen (about 1881 for the conversion) differ, as above. Taherzadeh’s passage has no date.

How Zoroastrians read the promise. The sources here are Bahá’í and Bahá’í-related scholarship, with one Zoroastrian priest’s account (Dhalla), which is critical of the Bahá’í reading and of the conversions. They do not report how Zoroastrian scholars or priests in general read these texts today, and nothing here shows that Zoroastrian institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.

Concrete images

  • The sacred fire, and the Tablet’s ”primal Fire“ that appears ”with a new radiance and with immeasurable heat“ (TU 5.2); a fire that ”burneth of itself, with neither fuel nor fume“ (TU 5.2).
  • A star that falls on the night a prince is born (Bahman Yasht III.15).
  • The sun told to stand still (Bahman Yasht III.45; WOB 6.13).
  • Wolf period and sheep period (Bahman Yasht III.40). The wolf and the lamb at one stream is a separate image, from a conversion story in Maneck.
  • A young man selling beetroot at the door of a house in Yazd, where a Bahá’í teacher is hiding, and the talk the next day (Ḥájí Muḥammad-Ṭáhir’s memoir in Taherzadeh, vol. 2, pp. 103–104).
  • A glass of tea shared across a rule of ritual impurity (Maneck’s report of Ardishír and Mullá ‘Abdu’l-Qání).
  • A man sitting apart in a caravanserai in Káshán who runs out at the noise of a Bábí paraded on an ass, and says that a thousand years might have passed before he heard (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, quoted by Taherzadeh, vol. 3, pp. 268–269).
  • Fire-temples that ”summoned, with burning zeal“ (TU 2.40).
  • A star named Bahrám: ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s letter to Mullá Bahrám opens with the star Bahrám (Mars) as quarrelsome and hot-tempered (Maneck’s thesis; paraphrase of a poor scan).
  • The Hand of Omnipotence stretched out from behind the clouds (TU 5.11).

Two possible arcs

  1. Conflict to dawn. A long conflict (the three thousand years in GPB VI.14), the sign in the sky (the star), the sun standing still, the Fire appearing ”with a new radiance“, the call to the priests to look ”with new eyes“ (TU 5.11), and then peace described as a change of ages (”the sheep period comes on“).
  2. A single household. A young man at a door with beetroot; the question the family had never thought to ask; a talk the next day; weeping aloud; a glass of tea shared; a prophecy known from childhood and heard again. This arc uses only the historical reports and keeps the scripture for the refrain.

What a lyric must not claim

  • That the Zoroastrian priesthood or its scholars accept the Bahá’í reading, or that the Zoroastrian books name Bahá’u’lláh. Dhalla’s chapter shows that a leading priest of the twentieth century did not.
  • That the Saoshyant is Bahá’u’lláh, or that Yasht 19 and the Gathas speak of Sháh-Bahrám. The texts gathered here do not say it, and Kazemi’s reading about the Fire is Kazemi’s own.
  • That the Pahlavi books were written by Zoroaster, or that the sun literally stopped.
  • A date or a count of years for the sun sign beyond what the Tablet and the 1934 letter state. Use the sign without the sum.
  • The ”Dinkird“ quotation, or a ”twelve hundred years“ prophecy as Zoroastrian scripture.
  • That Mánikchí Ṣáḥib became a Bahá’í. He did not (introduction to The Tabernacle of Unity; Taherzadeh).
  • A general charge against priests. The address to the ”high priests“ was spoken to those of Bahá’u’lláh’s time, and the same Tablet says a priest who ”hath seen the light“ is ”a source of illumination to the whole world.“
  • That the conversions were large in Parsi India. Buck says there were few Parsi converts.
