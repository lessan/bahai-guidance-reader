Baghdad and the Bábí Gatherings: The Community Around Bahá’u’lláh, 1856–1863

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page covers

Between His return from Sulaymáníyyih in 1856 and His departure for the Garden of Riḍván in April 1863, Bahá’u’lláh lived in Baghdad as the person around whom the scattered Bábí community re-formed. During those years the exiles who gathered around Him were described, by an eyewitness quoted by Shoghi Effendi, as intoxicated with His presence. They were poor, they chanted His Tablets through the night, and they did not yet know that He would declare His mission. In the same years He went each day to a coffee house in the old city and talked with whoever came.

This page collects what the sources say about three things:

  1. The community and its state: who came, how they lived, what the Guardian and Nabíl say of their joy and their poverty.
  2. The gatherings and the Tablets: the chanting, the feasts, the odes, and the works revealed in those years.
  3. The coffee house: what is recorded about Bahá’u’lláh’s daily visits, who was there, and how the people of Baghdad regarded Him.

It closes briefly with the departure to the Garden of Riḍván, which ended the period.

It is also the research base for a song. A separate set of notes for that purpose records which details are well attested and which rest on one source.

Where the community stood in 1856

Shoghi Effendi presents Bahá’u’lláh’s return from Sulaymáníyyih as a turning point for the Faith as a whole, because for the first time since the Báb’s early ministry it had a fixed place to turn to:

“The return of Bahá’u’lláh from Sulaymáníyyih to Baghdád marks a turning point of the utmost significance in the history of the first Bahá’í century.”

The Guardian says the community had been close to ending. The Báb had spent half of His ministry confined near the Persian border, and after His martyrdom the community was in confusion: the figure nominally at its head, Mírzá Yaḥyá, stayed in hiding. In the Guardian’s words the Faith “had, in consequence of the successive and formidable blows it had received, reached the verge of extinction”, and Bahá’u’lláh’s retirement to Kurdistán “seemed to have set the seal on its complete dissolution.”

On His return the pattern reversed. Kurds from Sulaymáníyyih followed Him to Baghdad “with the name of ‘Darvísh Muḥammad’ on their lips, and the ‘house of Mírzá Músá the Bábí’ as their goal.” The house was in the Karkh quarter on the western bank of the Tigris. Shoghi Effendi describes it as “an extremely modest residence”, and says that within a few years it had become “the focal center of a great number of seekers, visitors and pilgrims, including Kurds, Persians, Arabs and Turks, and derived from the Muslim, the Jewish and Christian Faiths.” It also became a refuge: those wronged by the Persian government’s representative went there to seek redress.

Who came

The Guardian names the Bábís who made the journey to Baghdad to attain His presence. The list includes four cousins of the Báb and His maternal uncle, Ḥájí Mírzá Siyyid Muḥammad; Varaqatu’r-Riḍván, a granddaughter of Fatḥ-‘Alí Sháh; Mullá Muḥammad-i-Qá’iní, Nabíl-i-Akbar; Mullá Ṣádiq-i-Khurásání, Ismu’lláhu’l-Aṣdaq; Mullá Báqir, one of the Letters of the Living; Siyyid Asadu’lláh, Dayyán; Siyyid Javád-i-Karbilá’í; the two brothers later called the King and the Beloved of Martyrs; Siyyid Ismá‘íl-i-Zavári’í; Mírzá Áqáy-i-Munír; and Mullá Zaynu’l-‘Ábidín, “who had ranked as a highly esteemed mujtahid.” Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam, “His Poet-Laureate, His chronicler and His indefatigable disciple”, had already joined the exiles and was making journeys to Persia on the Cause’s behalf.

People of Baghdad’s own establishment came too. The Guardian lists the Muftí, Ibn-i-Álúsí, and the Shaykhs ‘Abdu’s-Salám and ‘Abdu’l-Qádir, who, “having obtained completely satisfying answers to their several queries, enrolled themselves among the band of His earliest admirers.” Government officials, including ‘Abdu’lláh Páshá, and Persian princes of the royal blood, including the Fakhru’d-Dawlih and the Shujá‘u’d-Dawlih, joined “the ever-widening circle of His associates and acquaintances.”

A change of conduct, not only of mood

The Guardian insists that the community’s joy came with a change in how it behaved. Bahá’u’lláh held to the Báb’s laws but taught “a standard which, while not incompatible with its tenets, was ethically superior to the loftiest principles which the Bábí Dispensation had established.” The Guardian lists among its features the “dissociation of the Bábí Faith from every form of political activity,” the “emphasis placed on the principle of non-violence,” the “ban imposed on all forms of sedition, on back-biting, retaliation, and dispute,” and the stress laid on “godliness, kindliness, humility and piety, on honesty and truthfulness, chastity and fidelity.”

Bahá’u’lláh Himself described the effect of those years in words the Guardian quotes:

“We exhorted all men, and particularly this people, through Our wise counsels and loving admonitions, and forbade them to engage in sedition, quarrels, disputes or conflict. As a result of this, and by the grace of God, waywardness and folly were changed into piety and understanding, and weapons of war converted into instruments of peace.”

Nabíl, quoted in the same chapter, describes how seriously some of the companions took the standard:

“Each one,” he relates, “had entered into a pact with one of his fellow-disciples, in which they agreed to admonish one another, and, if necessary, chastise one another with a number of blows on the soles of the feet, proportioning the number of strokes to the gravity of the offense against the lofty standards they had sworn to observe.”

According to Nabíl, an offender would not eat or drink “until the offender had suffered the punishment he had solicited.” This is Nabíl’s report of a private practice among some companions. It is not reported as a rule Bahá’u’lláh set, and the page does not offer it as a model.

The state of the believers

“So intoxicated”

The central testimony is Nabíl’s, quoted by Shoghi Effendi at the point where he turns from the community’s conduct to its devotion. The Guardian introduces it by saying that the transformation of the companions’ outlook “was equalled by the burning devotion which His love had kindled in their souls”, and that “a passionate zeal and fervor, that rivalled the enthusiasm that had glowed so fiercely in the breasts of the Báb’s disciples in their moments of greatest exaltation, had now seized the hearts of the exiles of Baghdád and galvanized their entire beings.” Then Nabíl:

“So inebriated,” Nabíl, describing the fecundity of this tremendously dynamic spiritual revival, has written, “so carried away was every one by the sweet savors of the Morn of Divine Revelation that, methinks, out of every thorn sprang forth heaps of blossoms, and every seed yielded innumerable harvests.”

And a little later in the chapter:

“So intoxicated were those who had quaffed from the cup of Bahá’u’lláh’s presence,” is yet another testimony from the pen of Nabíl, who was himself an eye-witness of most of these stirring episodes, “that in their eyes the palaces of kings appeared more ephemeral than a spider’s web.… The celebrations and festivities that were theirs were such as the kings of the earth had never dreamt of.”

The dates

Directly after, the Guardian quotes the passage on how little they had:

“Many a night,” continues Nabíl, depicting the lives of those self-oblivious companions, “no less than ten persons subsisted on no more than a pennyworth of dates. No one knew to whom actually belonged the shoes, the cloaks, or the robes that were to be found in their houses. Whoever went to the bazaar could claim that the shoes upon his feet were his own, and each one who entered the presence of Bahá’u’lláh could affirm that the cloak and robe he then wore belonged to him. Their own names they had forgotten, their hearts were emptied of aught else except adoration for their Beloved.… O, for the joy of those days, and the gladness and wonder of those hours!”

Taherzadeh (vol. 1, p. 209) quotes the same Nabíl passage. Taherzadeh describes these companions as “God-intoxicated” and “a new race of men”, and writes that “They circled around Him as the moth does around a candle.” The Guardian’s text does not use these phrases, and they are Taherzadeh’s own.

Nabíl adds a scene of Bahá’u’lláh Himself among the poor. Nabíl and two others lived in a room with no furniture, and Bahá’u’lláh came in one day:

“Its emptiness pleases Me. In My estimation it is preferable to many a spacious palace, inasmuch as the beloved of God are occupied in it with the remembrance of the Incomparable Friend, with hearts that are wholly emptied of the dross of this world.”

The Guardian follows it with Bahá’u’lláh’s own account of His circumstances in ‘Iráq. “There was a time in ‘Iráq,” He Himself affirms, in one of His Tablets, “when the Ancient Beauty … had no change of linen. The one shirt He possessed would be washed, dried and worn again.”

What the Guardian lists

In one long sentence the Guardian lists what the period was made of:

“The joyous feasts which these companions, despite their extremely modest earnings, continually offered in honor of their Beloved; the gatherings, lasting far into the night, in which they loudly celebrated, with prayers, poetry and song, the praises of the Báb, of Quddús and of Bahá’u’lláh; the fasts they observed; the vigils they kept; the dreams and visions which fired their souls, and which they recounted to each other with feelings of unbounded enthusiasm; the eagerness with which those who served Bahá’u’lláh performed His errands, waited upon His needs, and carried heavy skins of water for His ablutions and other domestic purposes; the acts of imprudence which, in moments of rapture, they occasionally committed; the expressions of wonder and admiration which their words and acts evoked in a populace that had seldom witnessed such demonstrations of religious transport and personal devotion—these, and many others, will forever remain associated with the history of that immortal period…”

The reception room

Nabíl’s description of the room in which Bahá’u’lláh received visitors is also in the Guardian’s chapter. The room was “dilapidated”, “low-roofed”, and had “but a single couch, fashioned from the branches of palms”, yet “it drew to itself, even as a loadstone, the hearts of the princes.” One of them, the Shujá‘u’d-Dawlih, wanted to build a copy in his house at Káẓimayn. Bahá’u’lláh, told of this, is reported to have said with a smile that he might reproduce the room, “this low-roofed room made of mud and straw with its diminutive garden”, but asked: “What of his ability to open onto it the spiritual doors leading to the hidden worlds of God?” Another prince, Zaynu’l-‘Ábidín Khán, the Fakhru’d-Dawlih, said of the room: “were all the sorrows of the world to be crowded into my heart they would, I feel, all vanish, when in the presence of Bahá’u’lláh. It is as if I had entered Paradise itself.”

The gatherings and the chanting

Nights of chanting

Taherzadeh, summarising Nabíl, says that as 1863 approached, a new Tablet or ode was revealed almost every day, and that the companions would “gather at night in a small room, light numerous camphorated candles and chant aloud these joyous odes. Oblivious of this world and wholly immersed in the realms of the spirit, they would suddenly discover that night had become day.” (Taherzadeh, vol. 1, ch. 12.) The Guardian quotes Nabíl on the same scene, in his chapter on the Declaration. Nabíl names the chanter: “Many a night,” he writes, “would Mírzá Áqá Ján gather them together in his room, close the door, light numerous camphorated candles, and chant aloud to them the newly revealed odes and Tablets in his possession. Wholly oblivious of this contingent world, completely immersed in the realms of the spirit, forgetful of the necessity for food, sleep or drink, they would suddenly discover that night had become day, and that the sun was approaching its zenith.”

Mírzá Áqá Ján was Bahá’u’lláh’s amanuensis. Taherzadeh lists the odes revealed in this period (spelled here as he gives them) as Subhana-Rabbiya’l-A‘la, Ghulamu’l-Khuld, Hur-i-‘Ujab, Az-Bagh-i-Ilahi and Halih-Halih-Ya-Bisharat, and says they are “very difficult to describe and may well prove impossible to translate.” Of Az-Bagh-i-Ilahi he writes that its chanting “creates an atmosphere of ecstasy and joy”, and that the Baghdad companions, who chanted it in their gatherings, “were carried away into the realms of spirit”. These are Taherzadeh’s statements about the odes, not the Guardian’s.

The feast with the rose-water

Taherzadeh retells a night that Nabíl, in his unpublished narrative, considered one of the most memorable of his life. The account is secondary (Taherzadeh reporting Nabíl’s manuscript) and the only source for the scene in the corpus.

A feast was held in Bahá’u’lláh’s house before the Declaration. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, then eighteen, was the host. Believers from Baghdad and Karbilá were present, among them Ḥájí Siyyid Javád-i-Karbilá’í, Shaykh Sulṭán and Sayyáḥ. After the meal they chanted Tablets, including the ode Az-Bagh-i-Ilahi. One verse condemns the unfaithful among the companions. When it was chanted, everyone looked at Siyyid Muḥammad-i-Iṣfahání, who rose and danced “a dance of rapture in an attempt to dispel their suspicion”. Then:

Then without warning the door opened and Bahá’u’lláh entered majestically, holding in His hand a small glass vessel of rose-water. He greeted them with the salutation ‘Allah’u’Akbar’, and bade them not to arise or disrupt their meeting. He had felt the spirituality of that gathering, He said, and so had come to anoint them with rose-water. This He graciously did, going to every person in the room, after which He left.

Taherzadeh closes with Nabíl’s verdict: “No one was able to sleep that night, so intoxicated were they with the wine of His presence. ‘The like of that night’, Nabíl writes, ‘the eye of creation had not seen.’” Taherzadeh adds, in a note, that in those days it was a gracious act for a host to anoint his guests with rose-water.

What was revealed

The Guardian describes the amount of revelation in these years with a figure from Nabíl:

“So prolific was this period, that during the first two years after His return from His retirement, according to the testimony of Nabíl, who was at that time living in Baghdád, the unrecorded verses that streamed from His lips averaged, in a single day and night, the equivalent of the Qur’án!”

Much of it did not survive. Mírzá Áqá Ján told Nabíl that “hundreds of thousands of verses, mostly written by His own hand, were obliterated and cast into the river,” by Bahá’u’lláh’s order, with the explanation: “None is to be found at this time worthy to hear these melodies.” A Shírází named Muḥammad Karím, who had watched the Báb write, said of the verses he saw Bahá’u’lláh produce: “I bear witness that the verses revealed by Bahá’u’lláh were superior, in the rapidity with which they were penned, in the ease with which they flowed, in their lucidity, their profundity and sweetness to those which I, myself saw pour from the pen of the Báb when in His presence.”

The Guardian names the works that survive from the period:

  • The Kitáb-i-Íqán, “revealed within the space of two days and two nights, in the closing years of that period (1278 A.H.–1862 A.D.)”, in answer to the Báb’s maternal uncle, Ḥájí Mírzá Siyyid Muḥammad. Taherzadeh adds that Mírzá Áqá Ján described the circumstances in a Tablet to Shaykh ‘Abdu’l-Majíd-i-Shírází.
  • The Hidden Words, “with which Bahá’u’lláh was inspired, as He paced, wrapped in His meditations, the banks of the Tigris”, revealed in 1274 A.H. and first called the “Hidden Book of Fáṭimih”. Bahá’u’lláh’s own opening says: “This is that which hath descended from the Realm of Glory, uttered by the tongue of power and might”. Taherzadeh dates it “about A.D. 1858”.
  • The Seven Valleys, “His greatest mystical composition”, in answer to Shaykh Muḥyi’d-Dín, the Qáḍí of Khániqayn.
  • The Four Valleys, an epistle to Shaykh ‘Abdu’r-Raḥmán-i-Karkúkí, together with the Tablet of the Holy Mariner, the Lawḥ-i-Ḥúríyyih, the Súriy-i-Ṣabr and others.

The Guardian says these works “contributed, each in its own way, to swell the ‘rivers of everlasting life’ which poured forth from the ‘Abode of Peace’ and lent a mighty impetus to the expansion of the Báb’s Faith in both Persia and ‘Iráq, quickening the souls and transforming the character of its adherents.”

The Hidden Words open the Arabic part with a counsel for the heart, which the companions of that period would have heard:

“O SON OF SPIRIT! My first counsel is this: Possess a pure, kindly and radiant heart, that thine may be a sovereignty ancient, imperishable and everlasting.” (Hidden Words, Arabic no. 1)

And, in the same wine imagery that Nabíl uses of the companions:

“O SON OF DUST! Turn not away thine eyes from the matchless wine of the immortal Beloved, and open them not to foul and mortal dregs.” (Hidden Words, Persian no. 62)

The page does not claim that the companions had any of these specific verses in mind. It places them here only because they were revealed in the same years and in the same city.

Some of the people

Two accounts stand out in the Guardian’s chapter and show the pitch of devotion. Another is reported by Taherzadeh. Each is told here with the sources named.

Dhabíḥ and the turban. The Guardian chose this one instance “from such a treasury of precious memories”: Siyyid Ismá‘íl of Zavárih, “formerly a noted divine, taciturn, meditative and wholly severed from every earthly tie”, swept the approaches to Bahá’u’lláh’s house at dawn. “Unwinding his green turban, the ensign of his holy lineage, from his head, he would, at the hour of dawn, gather up, with infinite patience, the rubble which the footsteps of his Beloved had trodden”, and, “scorning to cast his burden for the feet of others to tread upon”, carried it to the river and threw it in. After forty days without sleep or food he took his own life on the river bank, on the road to Káẓimayn (1275 A.H.). Bahá’u’lláh is reported by the Guardian to have said of him: “No blood … has, till now, been poured upon the earth as pure as the blood he shed.” Bahá’u’lláh then ordered the refugees living in Baghdad to return to their native land, because others were ready to follow. This is the most extreme example of the period, and it is not a model. Taherzadeh adds a note that the act “cannot be compared with ordinary suicide” and that suicide “is strongly condemned in the Faith of Bahá’u’lláh and is clearly against His Teachings.”

Taherzadeh also tells (vol. 1, pp. 102–3, citing the Kitáb-i-Badí‘) how Dhabíḥ was changed. At a host’s home, offered a tray of fruit and sweets, he asked instead for “a portion of spiritual food”, heard Bahá’u’lláh speak, and “remained in a state of spiritual intoxication”. Salmání, who reached Baghdad in 1862 and so tells this at second hand, has a version in which the man is “Siyyid Ismá‘íl of Ardistán” and, when tea was ordered, asked: “Lord, do Thou bid them bring me the wine of the spirit.” The attributions differ (Zavárih in the Guardian and Taherzadeh; Ardistán in Salmání), and it is probably the same person, but the sources here do not settle it.

Nabíl’s repentance. Taherzadeh (vol. 1, pp. 202–3) says that Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam, in the confusion after the Báb’s martyrdom, had claimed to be the Promised One. He came to Baghdad, met Bahá’u’lláh, and “prostrated himself at His feet and begged forgiveness for his presumption.” As a sign of humility, “he cut his beard, which in those days was the symbol of a man’s dignity, made a brush and with it swept the approaches to the house of Bahá’u’lláh.” This is Taherzadeh’s account only. The Guardian says that many who had claimed that title “were for the most part instinctively led to seek His presence, confess their error and supplicate His forgiveness.”

The “delightful Afnán”. Taherzadeh (vol. 1, p. 198) says that Ḥájí Mírzá Muḥammad-Taqí, a cousin of the Báb, read the Kitáb-i-Íqán, travelled to Baghdad, and “radiated such heavenly joy” walking in the streets that the believers called him “the ‘delightful Afnan’”. Taherzadeh is the only source.

The coffee house

What the sources say

Three sources describe Bahá’u’lláh’s visits to a coffee house in Baghdad. They are independent of the Guardian, who does not mention the coffee house but describes the same openness in general terms (see below).

1. Bahá’u’lláh’s own remark, as recorded by Ḥájí Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí. Taherzadeh (vol. 4, pp. 4–5) records words Bahá’u’lláh spoke to Ḥájí Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí in the Mazra‘ih period, about 1877–79. It is not a Tablet. It is a believer’s record of a conversation, written down from memory. In it, Bahá’u’lláh contrasts Baghdad with His later confinement:

During the days of Baghdad We ourself used to visit the coffee house and meet with everyone. We associated with people whether they were in the community or outside, whether acquaintances or strangers, whether they came from far or near.

We considered those who were distant from us to be near and the strangers as acquaintances.

He adds that in Baghdad “to all appearances We were not a prisoner”, that in Adrianople He met some people, and that in ‘Akká He met no one. Now ‘Abdu’l-Bahá bore that work as “a mighty shield facing the world and its peoples”, and “to deal and associate with these people is the most arduous task of all.” The remark is therefore evidence that Bahá’u’lláh Himself regarded the coffee-house years as a time of meeting everyone, and that He thought of it as work.

2. Taherzadeh’s account of the daily round. In vol. 3 (pp. 250–51), Taherzadeh describes the day:

After having breakfast in the inner section of His house, He went to the outer apartment set aside for the reception of visitors. The friends used to gather in that room where they attained His presence for about a half to one hour. During this time He used to either sit or pace up and down the room. He then proceeded to an oriental inn (coffee house) in the old part of the city, accompanied by at least two believers. The inn was owned by a certain Siyyid Habib, an Arab, who was not a Babi, but a great admirer of Bahá’u’lláh and one who showed extraordinary reverence towards Him. Many people, both high and low, attained His presence in this inn. Bahá’u’lláh often used to speak to them about the Faith of the Báb and expound some verities of the Cause of God.

After the meeting, which “usually lasted over an hour”, He returned home, received the believers in the afternoon, went back to the inn, and “returned home about the time of sunset.” Some evenings the believers came to His house. “Eminent personalities, civil and religious dignitaries, Persian princes, and others” came to Him for guidance, but “Bahá’u’lláh never went to their houses although sometimes He visited the homes of the believers.” Taherzadeh does not name his source in this passage. The order of the day matches Salmání, below, so he probably drew on a similar account, but that is an inference.

3. Salmání’s eyewitness description. Ustád Muḥammad-‘Alí Salmání, the barber, reached Baghdad about a year before the departure (that is, in 1862) and writes that Bahá’u’lláh was not yet making an explicit declaration. He gives the same sequence, with more detail. After morning tea in the inner apartments, Bahá’u’lláh went to a single clean room on the men’s side of the house, put up by a builder, Ustád Ismá‘íl Banná of Káshán, where He “would walk about and pace up and down and the friends would visit Him” for half an hour or an hour. Then:

After that, He would proceed to a coffeehouse. There was in Baghdad a Siyyid Habíb the Arab, who was the kad-khudá [borough-head] of Old Baghdad. This man had a coffeehouse which Bahá’u’lláh would frequent, and as a general thing this is where the people would come to be with Him. Áqá Najaf-‘Alí and Áqá Muhammad-Ibráhím, who were permanent servitors of Bahá’u’lláh, would also be present. Sometimes, I too would go along.

And of what happened there:

Here Bahá’u’lláh would partake of coffee every day, and the water pipe would be prepared for Him. This was His own pipe--from Bihbihán--and He would smoke a very little, and would converse with the people. His purpose in going to this coffeehouse was to spread the Faith. It was an excellent establishment. Siyyid Habíb was not a believer, but he was a fine man--and very unassuming.

After an hour or an hour and a half He returned home until afternoon, went back to the coffee house and stayed until sundown, then went to His quarters; the friends “would usually remain together until two hours after sunset”.

On who came and how the city saw Him, Salmání writes: “All the great of Baghdad, and the ulama, and the magistrates, would present themselves here at this coffeehouse with extreme deference. Bahá’u’lláh, however, would never go to their homes. The inhabitants of Baghdad (that is, the Sunnís) would speak, one and all, of the utter perfection of Bahá’u’lláh. Many a time they would refer difficult questions to Him and request Him to solve them. With Námiq Páshá He did not associate.”

Points of detail and where the sources differ

  • Siyyid Ḥabíb’s role. Taherzadeh calls him the owner of the inn. Salmání says he was the borough-head of Old Baghdad who had a coffee house which Bahá’u’lláh frequented. Both say he was an Arab and not a Bábí.
  • Companions. Taherzadeh says “at least two believers” went with Him. Salmání names two servants (Áqá Najaf-‘Alí, who was later killed in Ṭihrán, and Áqá Muḥammad-Ibráhím of Nayríz) and says he sometimes went too.
  • How many times a day. Both say He went twice: a morning visit of about an hour, and an afternoon visit until sunset.
  • Subjects. Taherzadeh says He spoke of the Faith of the Báb. Salmání says He went there to spread the Faith. Neither gives a conversation. The corpus holds no account of a particular exchange in the coffee house.
  • Which coffee house. The sources speak of one place, Siyyid Ḥabíb’s. The plural “coffee houses” in the request for this page has no support; there is one named establishment.
  • When. Salmání’s eyewitness covers the last year. Taherzadeh gives no date and says the pattern held before the Declaration. Bahá’u’lláh’s own remark covers “the days of Baghdad” in general.

The wider picture of His public life

The Guardian does not mention the coffee house, but describes how openly Bahá’u’lláh lived among the city. He lists the people of the city who left testimony of Him: those who watched Him “as He moved through the lanes and streets of the city, or paced the banks of the river”; “the worshippers who watched Him pray in their mosques”; “the mendicant, the sick, the aged, and the unfortunate whom He succored, healed, supported and comforted”; “the visitors, from the haughtiest prince to the meanest beggar, who crossed His threshold and sat at His feet.” He calls these “numerous and striking” anecdotes and chooses one to tell (Dhabíḥ).

The Guardian also records a plot against Him in the city. The Persian consul-general, Mírzá Buzurg Khán, tried to stir the poorer population to insult Him in public, hoping for a retaliation that could be used against Him. It failed because Bahá’u’lláh “continued to walk unescorted, both by day and by night, through the streets of the city”, and, “Well aware of their motives, He would approach them, rally them on their intentions, joke with them, and leave them covered with confusion and firmly resolved to abandon whatever schemes they had in mind.”

On what the city’s rulers thought, the Guardian reports that the British consul-general, Colonel Sir Arnold Burrowes Kemball, offered Him British citizenship and transfer to India, which He declined; that the governor, Námiq Páshá, “impressed by the many signs of esteem and veneration in which He was held”, called on Him in person, and delayed for three months, through five successive commands from the Grand Vizir, before telling Him the Turkish government wanted Him in the capital; and that Sulṭán ‘Abdu’l-Majíd “consistently refused” Persian requests to hand Him over or expel Him. The Guardian says that “On no previous occasion, since the inception of the Faith, not even during the days when the Báb in Iṣfahán, in Tabríz and in Chihríq was acclaimed by the ovations of an enthusiastic populace, had any of its exponents risen to such high eminence in the public mind.”

Taherzadeh makes the same point in vol. 1 (p. 259): “For no less than eight years the supreme Manifestation of God lived freely among the inhabitants of Baghdad”, and that multitudes “longed to attain His presence, to hear His words, or even catch a glimpse of Him as He walked in the streets or paced along the bank of the Tigris rapt in meditation.” He does not mention the coffee house in that passage.

The end of the Baghdad years

The conditions that made this period possible ended in the spring of 1863. The Guardian’s account runs as follows.

Naw-Rúz, at the Mazra‘iy-i-Vashshásh. On the fifth day of Naw-Rúz Bahá’u’lláh was celebrating the festival in a field on the outskirts of Baghdad when He revealed the Tablet of the Holy Mariner, “whose gloomy prognostications had aroused the grave apprehensions of His Companions”. An emissary of Námiq Páshá arrived the same day asking for an interview. Salmání, who was there (Bahá’u’lláh had told him at the bath, “Tomorrow you are to be my guest at Vashshásh”), says: “that very day a Tablet was revealed called the Tablet of the Holy Mariner.” Taherzadeh, citing Nabíl, adds that Mírzá Áqá Ján came out of the tent and chanted the Tablet to the assembled believers, and that Nabíl wrote: “Oceans of sorrow surged in the hearts of the listeners.” As the tents were being folded, Bahá’u’lláh is reported to have remarked that they “may be likened to the trappings of this world, which no sooner are they spread out than the time cometh for them to be rolled up.”

The news. The Guardian quotes an eyewitness on the community’s reaction: “That day … witnessed a commotion associated with the turmoil of the Day of Resurrection. Methinks, the very gates and walls of the city wept aloud at their imminent separation from the Abhá Beloved. The first night mention was made of His intended departure His loved ones, one and all, renounced both sleep and food.… Many had resolved that in the event of their being deprived of the bounty of accompanying Him, they would, without hesitation, kill themselves.… Gradually, however, through the words which He addressed them, and through His exhortations and His loving-kindness, they were calmed and resigned themselves to His good-pleasure.” For every one of them, “whether Arab or Persian, man or woman, child or adult”, He revealed during those days a separate Tablet in His own hand.

The departure. On Wednesday, 22 April 1863, the Guardian writes, “A concourse of people of both sexes and of every age … thronged the approaches of His house”. Giving alms to the poor on His way, He crossed the Tigris by ferry to the Najíbíyyih Garden. Before embarking He spoke to the companions: “With you it now rests to watch lest your deeds and conduct dim the flame of love that gloweth within the breasts of its inhabitants.” The muezzin was calling the afternoon prayer as He entered the garden.

The garden. He stayed there twelve days, which the Bahá’í calendar commemorates as the Festival of Riḍván and which the Guardian treats as the time of the Declaration of His mission. The Guardian writes that “Through that solemn act the ‘delay,’ of no less than a decade … was at long last terminated.” Nabíl’s description of those days, which the Guardian calls “one of the very few authentic records we possess”, is of roses and nightingales: every morning, the gardeners piled so many roses in the tent that “His companions … would be unable to see each other across it”, and Bahá’u’lláh gave them to His companions to carry to His friends in the city. One night, Nabíl saw Him walking the moonlit avenues while the nightingales sang so loudly that only those nearby could hear Him. Bahá’u’lláh said: “Consider these nightingales. So great is their love for these roses, that sleepless from dusk till dawn, they warble their melodies and commune with burning passion with the object of their adoration. How then can those who claim to be afire with the rose-like beauty of the Beloved choose to sleep?”

The Guardian quotes Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablet calling it the “Day of supreme felicity” and the “Most Great Festival”. The Declaration, and what Bahá’u’lláh said in the garden, are outside the scope of this page. The Guardian says of the Declaration that the words Bahá’u’lláh uttered and the manner of it “are shrouded in an obscurity which future historians will find it difficult to penetrate.” Salmání says that during the twelve days, morning and afternoon, Bahá’u’lláh “would speak of the Báb’s Cause and declare His own”, and that He spoke of the Báb as the Qá’im, “with certain intimations” declaring His own mission.

What the sources do not settle

  • The coffee house is documented by two secondary or recollective sources and one remark. The sentence quoted from Bahá’u’lláh comes through Ḥájí Mírzá Ḥaydar-‘Alí, as recorded by Taherzadeh. Salmání is a single eyewitness writing decades later. The Guardian does not mention the coffee house.
  • No conversation in the coffee house is recorded. We know who owned or kept it, who attended, what Bahá’u’lláh did (talk, drink coffee, smoke a little), and how often. We do not know what was said in a particular exchange.
  • The pennyworth of dates and the shared clothing are Nabíl’s report, not a Tablet, and the Guardian quotes them without further comment. They describe some of the companions. The page does not say how widely they applied.
  • The rose-water feast is reported only by Taherzadeh from Nabíl’s unpublished manuscript. It has no parallel in the Guardian’s text.
  • The identity and origin of Siyyid Ismá‘íl (Zavárih or Ardistán) differs between the Guardian and Salmání.
  • Length of residence. The Guardian says “a decade”; Taherzadeh says “no less than eight years” of living freely among the people; Námiq Páshá is quoted by Salmání as saying “twelve years”. These are different ways of counting and a reader should not treat the figure as exact.
  • Dates of individual Tablets (the Hidden Words, the Seven Valleys, the Four Valleys) are given loosely, or not at all, in the sources used here.
