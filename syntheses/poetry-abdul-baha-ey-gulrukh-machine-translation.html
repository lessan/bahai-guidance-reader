About the text

  • Title and author: Known by its opening line Ey gulrukh-i-abhá-yi-man (`ای گلرخ ابهای من`, ”O my Most Glorious rose-cheeked One“), attributed in the source header to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá.
  • Source and edition: Saved at [source] from `https://adibmasumian.com/translations/ab04431/` (retrieved 2026-10-17), citing Muntakhabátí az Makátíb-i-Ḥaḍrat-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá, vol. 2, pp. 17–18 (selection no. 27).
  • Form and language: A Persian strophic poem (murabba‘) of 9 four-line stanzas (36 lines in all), composed in the octosyllabic metre rajaz-i-murabba‘-i-sálim (`--u- / --u-`, mustaf‘ilun mustaf‘ilun). In each stanza the first three lines share an internal stanzaic rhyme while the fourth line returns to the overarching rhyme `-ú-yi-tu` (`روی تو`, `کوی تو`, `سوی تو`, `بوی تو`, `بوی تو`, `گیسوی تو`, `دلجوی تو`, `موی تو`, `جوی تو`, ”Thy countenance / Thy quarter / toward Thee / Thy fragrance / Thy tresses / Thy heart-alluring face / Thy hair / Thy stream“).
  • Content of the poem: The speaker addresses the Divine Beloved as ”my Most Glorious (Abhá) rose-cheeked One“, ”my Lord the Most Exalted (Rabbíya’l-A‘lá)“, and ”my Lote-Tree of Sinai (Sidriy-i-Síná)“, offering his life as a sacrifice for the Beloved’s countenance (stanza 1). He describes his lamentation out of longing for the Beloved’s quarter (stanza 2), declares the Beloved’s threshold and the dust of the path to be his Qiblih and the flame on the Sinai of Truth (stanza 3), contrasts the withering of hearts in separation with the Messiah-like life-giving breath of the Beloved’s fragrance (stanza 4), celebrates how a single waft of that fragrance has filled the horizons with ambergris and the musk of Cathay (stanza 5), asks the Beloved to behold the lovers wounded with grief, estranged from all kindred, and captive in the ringlet of His tresses (stanza 6), speaks of gazing toward the Beloved even while present in the company of the friends with a distraught heart (stanza 7), asks Him to look upon his weeping eye, seared heart, and burning sigh in longing for a single lock of His hair (stanza 8), and closes by invoking `بها` (Bahá) in three consecutive lines—presenting himself as the dust of the threshold, athirst for His water, burning in fever and restlessness for a single drop from His stream (stanza 9).
  • Historical context: Date, recipient, and occasion of composition are not established in the source file’s header.

Literal translation

Stanza 1

  1. ای گلرخ ابهای من

O my Most Glorious (Abhá) rose-cheeked One,

  1. ای ربّی الاعلای من

O my ”my Lord, the Most High“,

  1. ای سدرۀ سينای من

O my Lote-Tree of Sinai,

  1. جانم فدای روی تو

May my soul be a sacrifice for Thy face!

Stanza 2

  1. ای دلبر طنّاز من

O my enchanting Heart-Ravisher,

  1. ای همدم و هم راز من

O my Companion and Sharer of my secrets,

  1. اين ناله و آواز من

This lamentation and cry of mine

  1. از حسرت اين کوی تو

[Is] from yearning for this quarter of Thine.

Stanza 3

This threshold of Thine is my Qiblih;

  1. بر خاک ره قبلۀ من است

  1. در طور حقّ شعلۀ من است

Upon the Sinai of Truth is my flame;

  1. روی دلم زان سوی تو

The face of my heart is therefore toward Thee.

Stanza 4

  1. روح مسيحا بوی تو

Stanza 5

Uncertain readings

1–2`ای گلرخ ابهای من / ای ربّی الاعلای من``ابها` (Abhá, ”Most Glorious“) and `ربّی الاعلی` (Rabbíya’l-A‘lá, ”my Lord, the Most High“) function simultaneously as descriptive Arabic superlatives embedded in Persian iḍáfih constructions and as the distinctive titles of Bahá’u’lláh and the Báb (and of the Supreme Lord in the Arabic Tablet of the Holy Mariner).(1) Render the titles into English (”Most Glorious“, ”my Lord, the Most High“) while noting the proper titles in the Terms and Literal sections (chosen); (2) Leave Abhá and Rabbíya’l-A‘lá untranslated.
5`ای دلبر طنّاز من`In classical Persian mystical lyric, `طنّاز` (ṭannáz) denotes a beloved who is playfully teasing, winsome, coquettish, or enchanting—an idiom that in literal English (”coquettish“ / ”mocking“) can sound jarring in a sacred register.(1) ”enchanting“ (literal) / ”winsome“ (readable) (chosen to preserve the sense of captivating grace in an elevated register); (2) ”alluring“; (3) ”playful“.
8`از حسرت اين کوی تو`The demonstrative `اين` (”this“) before `کوی تو` (”Thy quarter/street“) is slightly unexpected when the speaker is lamenting in separation (`حسرت` / `فرقت`), where `آن کوی تو` (”that quarter of Thine“) is more common, though `اين` can express vivid mental proximity or proximity to the Shrine/threshold mentioned in line 9 (`اين درگهت`).(1) ”this quarter of Thine“ (literal, following the printed text) / ”Thy sacred quarter“ (readable); (2) emending `اين` to `آن` (”that quarter of Thine“).
9–10`اين درگهت قبلۀ من است / بر خاک ره قبلۀ من است`Lines 9 and 10 both repeat the exact rhyme-word `قبلۀ من است` (”is my Qiblih“), whereas line 11 has a different rhyme-word `شعلۀ من است` (”is my flame“). In Persian prosody, repeating `قبله` in lines 9 and 10 alongside `شعله` in line 11 is irregular unless `قبلۀ` in line 10 is a scribal or typographical assimilation for `جبهۀ من است` (”my brow/forehead is upon the dust of the path“, which fits `بر خاک ره` idiomatic prostration) or `کعبۀ من است` (”is my Ka‘bih“).(1) Translate `قبلۀ من است` as printed in both lines (”is my Qiblih“), preserving strict fidelity to the source text (chosen); (2) Read line 10 as a likely scribal slip for `بر خاک ره جبهۀ من است` (”Upon the dust of the path is my brow“) or `کعبۀ من است` (”is my Ka‘bih“).
12`روی دلم زان سوی تو``زان` (z-án, contraction of `از آن`) can mean ”therefore / for that reason“ (`زان [رو] سوی تو`, ”the face of my heart is therefore toward Thee“) or ”from that [direction] toward Thee“ / ”toward that direction of Thine“.(1) ”The face of my heart is therefore toward Thee“ (chosen, linking line 12 as the conclusion of lines 9–11); (2) ”The face of my heart is turned toward that quarter of Thine“.
15–16`از فرقتت آزرده است / روح مسيحا بوی تو`Syntactic ambiguity between lines 15 and 16: (a) line 15 (`از فرقتت آزرده است`) continues the predicate of `دل‌ها` and `جان‌ها` in lines 13–14, while line 16 is an independent nominal sentence (”The Spirit/breath of the Messiah is Thy fragrance“, contrasting separation’s blight with the reviving power of the Beloved’s scent); or (b) `روح مسيحا بوی تو` (”the spirit that hath the Messiah-breath of Thy fragrance“) is the subject of `از فرقتت آزرده است`.(1) Take line 15 with lines 13–14 and line 16 as a nominal clause: ”The Spirit of the Messiah is Thy fragrance“ (chosen); (2) ”Even the spirit perfumed with Thy Messiah-like breath is afflicted by separation from Thee“.
19–20`چون نَکْهَتِ گلزار شد / يک شمّه‌ای از بوی تو``چون` may be read either as a temporal conjunction (”When/since a single waft of Thy scent became like the fragrance of the rose-garden, [the horizons rained ambergris...]“) or as a preposition of comparison (”A single waft of Thy scent became like the fragrance of the rose-garden“).Both senses converge; rendered with both nuances in the literal and readable versions.
22–23`بيگانه از هر خويش بين / بنگر اسير خويش بين`Wordplay (tajnís-i-támm) on `خويش` (khvísh): in line 22, contrasted with `بيگانه` (”stranger“), it means ”kinsman / relative“ (or compound `خويش‌بين`, ”self-regarding one“); in line 23, `اسير خويش` means ”captive to Thyself“ (or ”captive to ourselves“).(1) Line 22 ”estranged from every kinsman“, line 23 ”captive to Thyself“ (chosen as primary); (2) Line 22 ”estranged from every self-regarding soul“.
33–36`اين خاک درگاهت بها / اين تشنه آبت بها / اندر تب و تابت بها / يک قطره‌ای از جوی تو`Grammatical and literary ambiguity in the closing stanza’s thrice-repeated `بها` (Bahá) and elliptical final line: (a) `بها` is a vocative without yá/ey (”[O] Bahá!“), addressing Bahá’u’lláh, while the speaker (‘Abdu’l-Bahá) points to himself as ”this dust of Thy threshold“ and ”this one athirst for Thy water“, begging for ”a single drop from Thy stream“; (b) `بها` functions as a poetic takhalluṣ / predicate (”This dust of Thy threshold is Bahá; this one athirst for Thy water is Bahá; in fever and burning for Thee is Bahá—[seeking] a single drop from Thy stream“); or (c) `بها` in one or more lines plays on the common noun bahá (”worth/price“).(1) Vocative ”O Bahá!“ with elliptical petition in line 36 (chosen, aligning with the attribution of the poem to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and the opening invocation of Abhá in line 1); (2) Predicate/takhalluṣ reading (”This dust of Thy threshold is Bahá...“).
