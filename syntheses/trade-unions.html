Trade Unions and the Bahá’í Teachings: Past, Present, and Future

Last updated 26 September 2026

Executive summary

A Bahá’í may join a trade union, hold office in one, and decide by conscience whether to take part in a lawful strike. The guidance on this is settled and consistent from 1951 to 1985. Membership is permitted as long as the union is not affiliated with a political party and does not require the member to join one (letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 2 February 1951; on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 4 January 1979 and 23 June 1985). Holding union office is permitted on the same condition. On strikes, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “in general disapproved,” but when the law recognises a strike called by a properly constituted authority such as a union, “the Bahá’í teaching neither requires nor forbids an individual to participate,” and Assemblies are told not to make further rules (1956, 1969, 1985).

The teachings go further than permission. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá diagnosed the conflict between owners and workers as having two causes, the greed of owners and the excessive demands of workers, and one root: an economic order that lets “a handful of people” accumulate “vast fortunes” while “the greater number remain naked, destitute, and helpless” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 78). His remedy was law, not force: rights of both parties “officially fixed,” a daily wage, a share for workers of “a fourth or fifth of the profits,” pensions from the firm’s revenues, and state intervention because “an impairment to one is a loss to all.” In 1937 Shoghi Effendi’s secretary summarised the method: the conflict between labour and capital “can best be solved through the peaceful and constructive methods of cooperation and of consultation.”

My position. Unions are a legitimate, and at present often necessary, means of protecting workers, and a Bahá’í can belong to one in good conscience. They are not the Bahá’í model of labour relations. That model is partnership: workers who share in profits and ownership, rights fixed in just law, and disputes settled by consultation and adjudication rather than by trials of strength. Much of the world has moved partway toward it since 1912, through minimum-wage law, labour courts, pensions, profit-sharing schemes, employee ownership and codetermination. The next pressure on work, from artificial intelligence, platform management and the 2.1 billion workers in informal employment, will test whether the gains of labour are shared or concentrated. The most useful contribution a Bahá’í can make is to bring consultation into workplaces and to build the arrangements, legal and technical, that give workers a fixed and fair share in what they produce. For a reader with software, AI or data skills, the highest-leverage work is on transparent, contestable algorithmic management and on tools for worker ownership and profit-sharing.

The question a Bahá’í brings to it

A Bahá’í who is offered a union card usually has three questions. Is a union a political organisation, which Bahá’ís may not join? If I join, may I take office? If the union calls a strike, may I walk out, and must I?

The first question arises because Bahá’ís are asked to keep out of partisan politics. Shoghi Effendi asked the believers to “refrain from associating themselves, whether by word or by deed, with the political pursuits of their respective nations, with the policies of their governments and the schemes and programs of parties and factions” (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, 21 March 1932). Many unions have historic ties to political parties. In Britain in the 1950s, union members paid a “political levy” that funded the Labour Party unless they contracted out of it.

The third question arises because ‘Abdu’l-Bahá spoke against strikes plainly and more than once. A Bahá’í who reads Some Answered Questions will find that workers “should not make excessive demands, be recalcitrant, ask for more than they deserve, or go on strike.”

The guidance answers all three questions. The history of how it came to be given helps a reader apply it well.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on labour and capital, 1904–1912

Some Answered Questions: the diagnosis

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s fullest treatment is chapter 78 of Some Answered Questions, the table talks recorded in ‘Akká between 1904 and 1906, which He reviewed Himself. The chapter is titled “Strikes” and begins by answering a question put to Him:

“YOU HAVE ASKED about strikes. Great difficulties have arisen and will continue to arise from this issue. The origin of these difficulties is twofold: One is the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners, and the other is the gratuitous demands, the greed, and the intransigence of the workers. One must therefore seek to address both.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 78, ¶1

He does not take a side. He then names the structural cause beneath both: “the root cause of these difficulties lies in the law of nature that governs present-day civilization, for it results in a handful of people accumulating vast fortunes that far exceed their needs, while the greater number remain naked, destitute, and helpless.” He rejects absolute equality as unworkable and asks instead for “moderation,” which He defines as laws that “prevent the unwarranted concentration of wealth in the hands of the few and satisfy the essential needs of the many.”

The remedy He gives is specific:

“For instance, the factory owners reap a fortune every day, but the wage the poor workers are paid cannot even meet their daily needs: This is most unfair, and assuredly no just man can accept it. Therefore, laws and regulations should be enacted which would grant the workers both a daily wage and a share in a fourth or fifth of the profits of the factory in accordance with its means, or which would have the workers equitably share in some other way in the profits with the owners. For the capital and the management come from the latter and the toil and labour from the former.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 78, ¶5

The chapter adds pensions for workers who are injured, incapacitated or old, paid “from the revenues of the factory itself.” Only after setting out these obligations on owners does He turn to the workers, and the sentence against strikes comes as part of a bargain:

“For their part, the workers should not make excessive demands, be recalcitrant, ask for more than they deserve, or go on strike. They should obey and comply and make no demands for exorbitant wages. Rather, the mutual and equitable rights of both parties should be officially fixed and established according to the laws of justice and compassion, and any party that violates them should be condemned after a fair hearing and be subject to a definitive verdict enforced by the executive branch, so that all affairs may be appropriately ordered and all problems adequately resolved.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 78, ¶9

The worker gives up the strike in a system where his wage, his share of profits and his pension are fixed in law and enforced by courts. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá then defends the state’s right to intervene against the objection that employment is a private contract: “since the commercial, industrial, and agricultural affairs, and even the general business of the nation, are all intimately linked together: An impairment to one is a loss to all” (¶10). That sentence is close to the old labour slogan “an injury to one is an injury to all,” applied to both parties.

America and Canada, 1912

In 1912 ‘Abdu’l-Bahá addressed the labour question in public in North America. The texts of these talks come from note-takers and press reports, not from texts He reviewed, so they carry less weight than Some Answered Questions. They are consistent with it.

At the Hotel Victoria in Boston on 23 July 1912, answering questions, He said:

“Under the laws which are to govern the world, the socialists may justly demand human rights but without resort to force and violence. … Today the method of demand is the strike and resort to force, which is manifestly wrong and destructive of human foundations. Rightful privilege and demand must be set forth in laws and regulations.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 23 July 1912 (notes by Edna McKinney)

In the same talk He described the Bahá’í method as change “not through sedition and appeal to physical force—not through warfare, but welfare.”

On 3 September 1912 He spoke at Coronation Hall in Montreal to an audience the Promulgation describes as “Socialists and Labor leaders.” H. M. Balyuzi, quoting the Montreal Gazette, records that the gathering was “associated with Jewish strikers.” To this audience He said that the labour question “will not be solved by strikes for wages,” and proposed that governments agree a law in which “the rights of the working people are to be strongly preserved. Also the rights of the capitalists are to be protected.” He gave a worked example of worker ownership:

“every factory that has ten thousand shares will give two thousand shares of these ten thousand to its employees and will write the shares in their names, so that they may have them, and the rest will belong to the capitalists. Then at the end of the month or year whatever they may earn after the expenses and wages are paid, according to the number of shares, should be divided among both. In reality, so far great injustice has befallen the common people. Laws must be made because it is impossible for the laborers to be satisfied with the present system. They will strike every month and every year.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, 3 September 1912, Montreal

The last sentence is a prediction, not an endorsement: without just law, workers will strike repeatedly, and “finally, the capitalists will lose.”

Earlier that year, on 26 April 1912 in Washington, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá met Samuel Gompers, president of the American Federation of Labor. Agnes Parsons recorded the exchange in her diary: Gompers said, “I try to do my duty,” and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá replied, “If each one tried to do his duty there would be no labor question” (Parsons diary, Bahá’í News no. 516, March 1974). A pilgrim-note record from the same week, with no authority beyond that of a note, has Him answering a question about using “the socialist & labor union movements” to spread the Faith: “All nations must become socialist in spirit. People must not be forced by circumstances to help the poor. They must give & willingly.”

Read together, the 1912 material repeats one position. The workers’ grievance is just. The strike is the wrong instrument because it is a contest of force. The solution is law that fixes both sides’ rights, profit-sharing that makes the worker a partner, and a change of heart in the rich.

The first Western Bahá’í writers on unions, 1917–1939

Western Bahá’ís took up this material quickly and wrote about unions directly. Their articles are the opinions of individual believers, but they show how the teaching was first understood.

Alfred E. Lunt, a Boston lawyer, addressed the Bahá’í Congress in New York on 29 April 1919. He wrote the earliest Bahá’í analysis of unions in the corpus:

“The meaning is not to abolish the wage system, but to supplement it. The bargaining power of enormous aggregations of workers, i. e., trade unions, is at present necessarily invoked to combat a stubborn employer, or one who is himself helpless in the grasp of the economic law of competition … But this bargaining power of the labor union sometimes openly ignores the justice of the common interest.” — Alfred E. Lunt, Star of the West, 10:16 (31 December 1919)

Lunt accepted that unions were “at present necessarily invoked” and objected to them as “an instrument of force and compulsion.” The same year he wrote to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá about profit-sharing and received a Tablet, translated by Shoghi Rabbani on 22 July 1919, which answered that “undoubtedly the workers must secure their daily salary, and in addition, must have shares with the employers” (Star of the West, 10:18).

Other writers in Star of the West followed the same line. Mary Hanford Ford (1917) wrote that “the workers in any institution will presently be regarded as partners.” George Orr Latimer (1925) held up the A. Nash Company of Cincinnati, a clothing firm that “adopted a plan of profit-sharing in 1920 by the unanimous vote of the workers,” as a model, and criticised unionism as having “become a self-centered and self-seeking power.” Ruhi Afnan (1925) described strikes and lock-outs as tactics “detrimental to both parties” whose object is “to force the opposite party to agree to their terms.” None of the firms these writers praised was a Bahá’í enterprise.

Two events from the 1930s show the community’s contact with organised labour. In December 1931 Bahá’í News printed a report from the Bahá’í Bureau in Geneva on the International Labour Conference, noting that the employees’ delegates “proposed dropping the old competitive system and bringing about co-operation between employer and employee” (Bahá’í News no. 57). In 1939 Frank Hurst of Bradford, described as “an early worker in the Trade Union Movement in Britain,” became a Bahá’í after more than twenty years as a sympathiser. A letter written on Shoghi Effendi’s behalf on 31 May 1939 called his confirmation “specially gratifying” (Unfolding Destiny).

Shoghi Effendi: membership, strikes and class

The method: cooperation and consultation

The earliest direct guidance on strikes came in 1937. A believer who supervised labourers in a factory, and expected a strike, asked what the Bahá’í attitude should be. The reply, written on the Guardian’s behalf on 30 June 1937, is:

“With regard to your question concerning the Bahá’í attitude towards labour problems: these cannot assuredly be solved, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá tells us, through the sheer force of physical violence. Non-co-operation too, even though not accompanied by acts of violence, is ineffective. The conflict between labour and capital can best be solved through the peaceful and constructive methods of co-operation and of consultation.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 30 June 1937, Social Action, extract [220]

The fuller text, quoted by the House of Justice in 1973, continues: “The Bahá’ís, therefore, are advised to avoid, as much as they can, getting mixed in labour strikes and troubles, and particularly to desist from all acts of physical violence which indeed run counter to the very spirit of the Cause” (letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice to the National Spiritual Assembly of Luxembourg, 4 April 1973, in Lights of Guidance no. 1404).

The words “as much as they can” matter. This is advice to a supervisor facing a possible strike, not a prohibition.

Membership: a matter for National Assemblies

In 1951 the Guardian’s secretary answered the membership question:

“Regarding your question about trade unions: The Guardian considers that this is a matter for each National Spiritual Assembly to advise the believers on. As long as the trade unions are not members of any particular political party, there does not seem to be any objection to the Bahá’ís belonging to them.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 2 February 1951, quoted in a letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 23 June 1985 (Lights of Guidance no. 2121)

Strikes: left to conscience

In 1956 the National Spiritual Assembly of the British Isles wrote to the Guardian with its own understanding of strikes. British law recognised strikes “as legal when called by properly constituted authorities such as a Trade Union,” and the Assembly’s view was that “the Bahá’í teaching, in spite of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s express disapproval of strikes, neither requires nor forbids an individual to strike but leaves him free to decide for himself in the particular circumstances of his case what is the proper course of action.” The reply, dated 11 July 1956, was brief:

“As regard strikes, the Guardian feels that your own understanding of the matter as expressed in your letter is quite correct, and he does not see the necessity of adding anything to it. We should avoid becoming rigid and laying down any more rules and regulations of conduct.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi to the National Spiritual Assembly of the British Isles, 11 July 1956 (Lights of Guidance no. 2121)

The next year the British National Assembly published its guidance in the Bahá’í Journal. Union membership “is in no way contrary to the teachings of the Faith, but Baha’is should not participate in the political activities of trade unions. They should make a point of ‘contracting out’ of the political levy … Holding trade union office is also permissible for Baha’is, providing the office does not require participation in politics” (Bahá’í Journal (UK) no. 131, May 1957). This is a national Assembly’s application, not universal guidance, but it is the clearest early statement of how the principle works in practice.

Class and ideology

While leaving individual Bahá’ís free on membership and strikes, Shoghi Effendi placed the Faith outside the ideology of class struggle. Bahá’ís, he wrote in 1936, “conceive” their Faith “to be essentially non-political, supra-national in character, rigidly non-partisan,” and “such a Faith knows no division of class or of party” (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, 11 March 1936). Of the future world commonwealth he wrote that “the inordinate distinction between classes will be obliterated,” and in 1931 described a world community in which “the interdependence of Capital and Labor” would be “definitely recognized.”

Letters written on his behalf filled in the economic position. Private ownership is “this vital sacred right of the individual,” and “the Cause neither accepts the theories of the Capitalistic economics in full, nor can it agree with the Marxists and Communists in their repudiation of the principle of private ownership” (10 June 1939, Social Action extract [221]). Wages “should be unequal,” because abilities differ, but “extremes of wealth and poverty should, however, be totally abolished,” and “those whose brains have contributed to the creation and improvement of the means of production must be fairly rewarded, though these means may be owned and controlled by others” (26 December 1935, extract [219]). On 19 November 1945 his secretary wrote to Dr. Glenn Shook: “Profit-sharing is recommended as a solution to one form of economic problems. There is nothing in the teachings against some kind of capitalism; its present form, though, would require adjustments to be made” (Bahá’í News no. 210, August 1948).

The position that results is neither capitalist nor socialist. Workers and owners are partners with different contributions. Their conflict is real but is not the engine of history, and it is to be resolved by law, profit-sharing and consultation.

The Universal House of Justice, 1969 to the present

1969: official strikes and the individual conscience

In 1969 the House of Justice restated the 1956 position to a National Spiritual Assembly, probably the British one, and defined its scope:

“As you know, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in general disapproved of strikes. Briefly stated, the Bahá’í attitude is that when the law recognises strikes as legal, as when called by a properly constituted authority such as a trade union, the Bahá’í teaching neither requires nor forbids an individual to participate in the strike but leaves him free to decide for himself what is the proper course of action in the particular circumstance. This ruling, we feel, applies by its nature only to so-called official strikes and when Bahá’ís are members of a trade union. However, as essentially this question is a matter of conscience your Assembly should be very careful, in the words written on behalf of the Guardian in the above-mentioned letter to your Assembly, to ‘avoid becoming rigid and laying down any more rules and regulations of conduct’.” — The Universal House of Justice to a National Spiritual Assembly, 9 August 1969 (NSA of Australia, LSA Handbook, §16.2.17)

The freedom of conscience applies to strikes that the law recognises and that a union properly calls. It does not extend to wildcat action, violence or unlawful strikes.

1979: union office

In 1979 the National Spiritual Assembly of Alaska asked about the Alaska Public Employees Association. The reply, written on behalf of the House of Justice on 4 January 1979, settled the question of office: “As long as this and other associations … are not affiliated with any political party and are not involved in political activities there is no objection to Bahá’ís belonging to them nor to their holding office in them” (Lights of Guidance no. 1403).

1985: the summary

On 23 June 1985, answering an individual, the House of Justice’s secretariat quoted the 1951 and 1956 letters and drew two conclusions:

“1. A Bahá’í can become a member of a trade union as long as he is not required to also join a political party. 2. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in general disapproved of strikes. The Bahá’í attitude is that when the law recognizes strikes as legal, as when called by a properly constituted authority such as a trade union, the Bahá’í teaching neither requires nor forbids an individual to participate in the strike but leaves him free to decide for himself what is the proper course of action in the particular circumstances.” — Letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice to an individual, 23 June 1985 (Lights of Guidance no. 2121)

This is the most recent statement of the membership and strike guidance found in the corpus.

2008 to 2022: justice in economic life

Later letters do not return to unions by name, but they widen the frame. In 2008 the House of Justice’s secretariat distinguished “between partisan political activity and the discourse and action intended to bring about constructive social change. While the former is proscribed, the latter is enjoined” (23 December 2008). Improving wages and conditions through a non-partisan union falls in the second category.

In 2010 the House of Justice wrote to the Bahá’ís of Iran about the legitimate acquisition of wealth and returned to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s point about profit-sharing:

“An employer and employee, for example, are bound by the laws and conventions that regulate their work, and each is expected to carry out his or her responsibilities with honesty and integrity. At another level, however, if the deeper implications of justice are to be realized, the other two preconditions to the legitimate acquisition of wealth mentioned above must be taken into account, and prevailing norms reassessed in their light. Here, the relationship between minimum wage and the cost of living merits careful evaluation—this, especially in light of the contribution workers make to a company’s success and their entitlement, as noted by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, to a fair share of the profits.” — The Universal House of Justice, 2 April 2010, to the Believers in the Cradle of the Faith (Social Action, extract [225])

This is the strongest recent statement on labour in the corpus. It treats the worker’s share of profits as an “entitlement,” not a favour, and names the gap between the minimum wage and the cost of living as a question of justice.

The letter of 1 March 2017 on economic life addresses the individual believer: “Every choice a Bahá’í makes—as employee or employer, producer or consumer, borrower or lender, benefactor or beneficiary—leaves a trace.” It asks the friends to “make their own individual and collective contributions to economic justice and social progress wherever they reside.” In 2022 the House of Justice encouraged the Bahá’ís of the Democratic Republic of the Congo to “help populations address economic injustice through unified action and without resort to conflict” (1 November 2022). Collective action by workers is not excluded. Conflict as a method is.

Reading the guidance as one whole

On a first reading, the guidance can look inconsistent. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá said workers should not strike. The Guardian and the House of Justice say a Bahá’í may strike. The Bahá’í approach to apparent contradictions between authoritative sources is to look for the concept that connects them, not to rank one above the other. Here the connecting concept is in ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s own text.

In Some Answered Questions the renunciation of the strike is paired with a system in which the rights of both parties are “officially fixed and established according to the laws of justice and compassion” and enforced after “a fair hearing.” In Boston the instruction was that “rightful privilege and demand must be set forth in laws and regulations.” The objection is to force used outside law. The later guidance limits the freedom of conscience to exactly the case where the law itself recognises the strike as a legal procedure called by “a properly constituted authority.” In that case the worker who strikes is using a procedure the law provides. The unlawful or violent strike, the kind ‘Abdu’l-Bahá described, remains outside what a Bahá’í should do.

This is my reading, not an authoritative interpretation. It explains why the Guardian could agree with the British Assembly “in spite of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s express disapproval of strikes” without contradicting the Master, and why the House of Justice repeats the disapproval in the same sentence that grants the freedom. The disapproval states the ideal. The freedom recognises that Bahá’ís live and work in the present order, where the law often fixes workers’ rights only partly and the lawful strike is one of the means that law provides.

Three further points follow from the guidance as a whole:

  • The test for a union is partisanship, not militancy. The 1951, 1979 and 1985 letters ask one question: is the union a member of, or affiliated with, a political party, and does membership require joining one? The British application in 1957 shows how to handle a union that funds a party: stay a member, and contract out of the political payment.
  • Office is allowed; political office within the union is not. A shop steward, branch secretary or negotiator is within the guidance. A post whose duties include campaigning for a party or candidate is not.
  • Assemblies should not add rules. The Guardian and the House of Justice both tell Assemblies to “avoid becoming rigid.” A Bahá’í who strikes lawfully and a Bahá’í who crosses a lawful picket line have both acted within the guidance. Neither should be criticised for it.

Where unions have been used against Bahá’ís: Iran

The Bahá’ís of Iran have met trade and guild bodies mainly as instruments of exclusion. On 9 November 1982 the Council of Labour Unions of Kirman wrote to the Bahá’í owner of a hairdressing shop that, “to avoid defiling Muslims by your touching them with your wet hands,” her business should be closed (The Bahá’í World, vol. XVIII). In 1996, after the 83rd International Labour Conference, the ILO’s Committee on the Application of Standards urged Iran to adopt a non-discriminatory policy, “particularly with regard to posts within the judiciary system, election to Islamic Labor Councils and access to university education.” In late 2008, according to the Bahá’í International Community, the Chamber of Commerce of Semnan and “39 member trade unions” decided to stop issuing business licences to Bahá’ís and to revoke existing ones; at least 15 Bahá’í-owned shops and workshops in the city were then sealed (BIC news, 14 June 2012).

This history reverses the usual image of unions as defenders of the weak. It is also why the Bahá’í International Community values the ILO’s structure. The ILO is a tripartite body of governments, workers and employers, so, as Diane Ala’i of the BIC put it in 2013, its concern for the Iranian Bahá’ís “represents the opinion not just of governments but also of workers and employers.”

The present: unions in 2026

The figures in this section come from current web sources, not from the Bahá’í corpus. They describe the state of the field. They are not authority for any Bahá’í principle.

Membership has halved in a generation. Across OECD countries, union density fell from 30% of employees in 1985 to 15% in 2023/24. It is 41.3% in the public sector and 10.1% in the private sector. Density is above 60% in Sweden and Denmark and 90% in Iceland, where unions administer unemployment insurance, and below 8% in Colombia, Estonia and Hungary (OECD, September 2025). In the United States the rate was 10.0% in 2025, 14.7 million members, against 20.1% in 1983; in the private sector it was 5.9% (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, February 2026). In the United Kingdom it was 22.4% in 2025, slightly up from 22.0% in 2024 (Department for Business and Trade, May 2026).

Collective bargaining covers a third of OECD workers. Coverage fell from 47% in 1985 to 33.6% in 2023/24. It remains high where bargaining happens at sector level, even where few workers are members (OECD, 2025).

Public support is high even where membership is low. Gallup found in 2025 that 68% of American adults approve of unions, the fifth year in a row in the 67–71% range, a level last seen in the early 1960s. In 2023, 33 major US work stoppages involved 458,900 workers, including the United Auto Workers strike against the three Detroit carmakers, whose settlement included a 25% wage increase over about four and a half years.

The law is moving in both directions. The UK Employment Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 18 December 2025. It repealed the 2023 minimum-service-levels law, made dismissal for lawful industrial action automatically unfair and shortened strike notice. In the United States the National Labor Relations Board lacked a quorum for most of 2025 after the removal of a Board member, until new members were sworn in on 7 January 2026.

Most of the world’s workers are outside unions altogether. The ILO counts 2.1 billion workers in informal employment and nearly 300 million in extreme working poverty, under $3 a day (ILO, Employment and Social Trends 2026). For these workers the question is not whether to join a union but whether any of their rights are fixed in law at all.

Unions are now bargaining over technology. The Writers Guild of America ended a 148-day strike in September 2023 with a contract stating that AI “cannot be considered a writer” and that writers cannot be required to use it. SAG-AFTRA won a requirement of informed consent and payment for digital replicas of performers. In December 2023 the AFL-CIO and Microsoft agreed to share information on AI and to give workers a voice in its design.

Two observations follow. First, the adversarial union and the partnership arrangements are not rivals in practice. France, Germany and the Nordic countries have both strong unions and the most developed partnership institutions. Unions have often been the parties that negotiated profit-sharing and codetermination into law. Second, where the partnership arrangements are strong, the strike is rarer. This is consistent with ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s prediction that without just law workers “will strike every month and every year,” and with His view that once rights are fixed the strike loses its reason.

The partisan test still matters in 2026. Many unions remain affiliated with parties or make political donations. In the United Kingdom, a union that spends money on party politics must do so from a separate political fund, and members are not required to pay into it; the British National Assembly advised Bahá’ís in 1957 not to. In the United States unions are not formally affiliated with parties, but many endorse candidates and fund campaigns. A Bahá’í member can take part in bargaining, grievances and workplace organising while declining to take part in endorsements and campaigns.

The future of work, and what the teachings indicate

The pressures now visible

Three changes are likely to shape labour relations over the coming decades.

Artificial intelligence. The IMF estimated in January 2024 that AI will affect almost 40% of jobs worldwide and about 60% in advanced economies, and that it “will likely worsen overall inequality” without policy action. The ILO’s refined 2025 index finds that one job in four is potentially exposed to generative AI, with 3.3% of employment in the highest-exposure category, and that transformation of jobs is more likely than their elimination. The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030. The central question is who receives the gains in productivity.

Algorithmic management and platform work. On 12 June 2026 the International Labour Conference adopted Convention No. 193 on decent work in the platform economy, the first international labour standard on the subject. According to secondary summaries, it covers freedom of association, pay transparency, social security, and human review before a worker’s pay is withheld or account deactivated. The EU Platform Work Directive, which presumes platform workers to be employees in many cases and regulates algorithmic management, must be transposed into national law by 2 December 2026.

The informal economy. For the 2.1 billion informal workers, the most basic element of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s design, rights fixed in law, is missing.

What the teachings indicate

The texts do not describe a future Bahá’í labour code, and Shoghi Effendi’s secretary was clear that “the Teachings of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá do not provide specific and detailed solutions to all such economic questions” (10 June 1939). They do give principles that bear directly on these pressures.

Machines will not end work. A letter written on Shoghi Effendi’s behalf on 26 December 1935 states: “Whatever the progress of the machinery may be, man will have always to toil in order to earn his living. … The progress of machinery has not made effort unnecessary. It has given it a new form, a new outlet.” Bahá’u’lláh raised work “performed in the spirit of service” to the rank of worship (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶33). A future that makes most people idle recipients would conflict with both.

The gains of production are to be shared with those who produce. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reason for profit-sharing was that “the capital and the management come from the latter and the toil and labour from the former.” When AI raises output per worker, the principle points toward sharing that gain with workers, through profit-sharing, ownership and wages, rather than letting it accrue only to capital. The 2010 letter’s reference to workers’ “entitlement … to a fair share of the profits” applies to AI-driven profits as much as to factory profits.

Disputes should be settled by fixed rights and fair hearings. ILO Convention 193’s requirement of human review before a worker is deactivated is an application of the principle that a party should be “condemned” only “after a fair hearing.” Transparency about how an algorithm sets pay is a precondition of rights being “officially fixed.”

The long-run direction is partnership, not permanent conflict. Shoghi Effendi described a world order in which “the interdependence of Capital and Labor” is “definitely recognized” and the “inordinate distinction between classes” has been “obliterated.” Some Bahá’í scholars have concluded that unions would have no place in such an order. John Huddleston wrote in World Order in 1975 that in a Bahá’í society “there would be no place for factions such as political parties and pressure groups or for lockouts, strikes, owners’ associations, and trade unions.” That is his opinion, not guidance. It is also possible that bodies representing workers would continue in a different form, as participants in consultation rather than combatants, much as works councils and worker board members function now. The texts do not settle this.

My position, and what to do with it

The Bahá’í teaching does not oppose unions. It regards them as a stage. In a world where workers’ rights are only partly fixed in law, a union is one of the few means workers have to secure what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá said they are owed: a living wage, a share of profits, protection in injury and old age. The early Western Bahá’í writers saw this in 1919: bargaining power “is at present necessarily invoked.” The guidance lets a Bahá’í take a full part in that work. What the teaching adds is a direction. The aim is not a permanent balance of power between two camps but an order in which the camps become partners. A Bahá’í in a union should work toward that.

In practice, that means the following.

For any Bahá’í worker or manager:

  • Join a union if it serves you and your colleagues, and check that membership does not require joining a party. Where the union funds a party, opt out of the political payment if the law allows it.
  • Serve as a representative or officer if asked, and decline duties that involve party campaigning.
  • Decide on lawful strikes by conscience, after consulting with those affected. Do not take part in violence or unlawful action. Do not judge fellow believers who decide differently.
  • In negotiations, use the methods of consultation: frank statement of facts, the search for the just outcome rather than victory, and respect for the other side. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s diagnosis gives a Bahá’í a way to speak to both sides, since it names greed on both.
  • If you are an employer or manager, act on the 2010 letter: compare the wages you pay with the cost of living, and consider profit-sharing or employee ownership. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s proposal of one-fifth to one-quarter of profits is a concrete benchmark.

For a reader with software, AI or data skills, the highest-leverage contribution is on the systems that will decide how the gains of AI are divided and how workers are managed:

  • Build or improve algorithmic-management systems that meet the standard of a “fair hearing”: explainable pay calculations, human review before deactivation, and an appeal route that a worker can use. ILO Convention 193 and the EU directive will create demand for exactly this.
  • Build tools for profit-sharing and employee ownership, such as cap-table and distribution software for small firms and cooperatives, which currently find these schemes costly to administer.
  • Contribute to data and research on how AI productivity gains are distributed between wages, profits and prices. Accurate measurement is a precondition for any just settlement.
  • Where you have influence over AI deployment in your own workplace, argue for consulting the affected workers before deployment, as the WGA and AFL-CIO agreements require.

For Bahá’í communities, the 2017 letter’s call to contribute to “economic justice and social progress” applies to working life. Study circles and reflection meetings can include the economic teachings in Some Answered Questions ch. 78. Social action projects can include vocational training and cooperatives, as Bahá’í development programmes in Haiti, India and Africa have done since the 1980s.

Open questions and honest limits

  • The most specific union guidance reaches us through compilations. The 1951, 1956, 1969, 1979 and 1985 letters are quoted here from Lights of Guidance (Helen Hornby, 1997) and the Australian National Spiritual Assembly’s LSA Handbook, not from primary publications on bahai.org. The 1937 letter is also in the Research Department’s Social Action compilation. The wording is consistent across the compilations, but readers should check with their National Assembly for the current application.
  • The corpus contains no union guidance after 1985. I found no later letter from the House of Justice on unions or strikes. There may be letters that are not in the corpus.
  • The 1912 talks are not authenticated texts. The Boston and Montreal talks come from notes and press reports. The Gompers exchange comes from a diary, and the Washington answer on “socialist & labor union movements” from a pilgrim note. I have used them only where they agree with Some Answered Questions.
  • The recipient of the 1969 letter is not named in the sources. Its reference to the Guardian’s 1956 letter “to your National Spiritual Assembly” indicates the British Assembly.
  • The reconciliation of the strike guidance is my reading. The section “Reading the guidance as one whole” is an interpretation by the author of this page, not an authoritative interpretation.
  • Web figures will age. The figures on union density, AI exposure, platform work and profit-sharing are current to September 2026. The French profit-sharing total and the UK employee-ownership count come from secondary reports of official data. The provisions of ILO Convention 193 are described from secondary summaries.
  • The future of unions in a Bahá’í order is not settled by the texts. Huddleston’s view that there would be no unions is one scholar’s opinion.
  • A related date discrepancy. The ownership passage (“this vital sacred right of the individual”) is dated 10 June 1939 in Social Action. The page wealth-and-poverty dates the same passage 22 April 1939. The Social Action date is used here.
