Free Will, Predestination and Fate: Some Answered Questions 35, 68 and 70

Last updated 11 October 2026

What this page covers

Three short chapters of Some Answered Questions carry most of what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says, in a single book, about whether human beings are free. Chapter 35 asks whether an action God already knows, and which is written in the “Guarded Tablet”, can be resisted. Chapter 68 asks whether fate is irrevocable and, if so, why anyone should try to avoid it. Chapter 70 asks whether a person is free or compelled. Taken together the answers make five claims:

  1. Knowing is not causing. God’s knowledge of an act, and a prophet’s announcement of it, do not bring the act about. God’s knowledge is also “freed from the distinctions of past, present, and future” (ch. 35).
  2. Fate has two kinds. Some outcomes are fixed by the nature of a thing and cannot be changed; others may or may not occur, and prudence can avoid them (ch. 68).
  3. The moral choice belongs to us. Choosing between good and evil, justice and injustice, is free, and we are accountable for it. Sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune and material loss are not chosen, and we are not accountable for them (ch. 70).
  4. The power to choose is lent. Every human act, good or bad, is carried out with a power that God continuously supplies. The direction of the act is the person’s own (ch. 70, the ship and the governor).
  5. Differences in rank are not injustice. A lower station has no grievance against the potter; each being must progress within its own degree (ch. 70, with chs. 32, 57 and 63).

The page is also the research base for a video episode on these chapters, with a host, a sceptical philosophy lecturer as guest, and a narrator reading the passages. So it does four things in turn: it walks through each chapter and every analogy in it; it gathers the same themes from the rest of the Writings and the authoritative guidance; it describes the Islamic, Christian and modern philosophical debates these chapters entered; and it sets out the objections a careful sceptic would raise, with what the texts say and what they leave open.

Quotations of the Writings and of the letters of Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice are taken verbatim from the texts held in this knowledge base. The intellectual history in the section on the wider debate comes from outside scholarship and web sources. It describes the field. It is never cited as authority for a Bahá’í teaching.

The setting of the three chapters

Some Answered Questions records answers ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave to Laura Clifford Barney in ‘Akká between 1904 and 1906, while He was still a prisoner of the Ottoman authorities. The foreword to the 2014 revised translation describes how they were recorded:

“Many of the conversations took place at the lunch table. Arrangements were made for one of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s sons-in-law, or for one of His three secretaries of that time, to take down in Persian the text of His replies. From the resulting collection of notes a selection was made; ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá then corrected these notes twice in His own hand, sometimes substantially revising them in the process as well as carefully reviewing the final wording.”

Two facts follow for anyone discussing these chapters. First, the text is not a transcript of table talk: ‘Abdu’l-Bahá reviewed and corrected it, which is why it carries the authority of His own writing. Second, the conversational form remains. Chapter 70 opens with a remark that only makes sense at a meal: “God willing, another day we will explain this matter at length from the beginning of our lunch.” The book does not contain that longer explanation, and nothing in this corpus shows whether it was ever given.

The foreword also gives a reading rule that matters here, because the subject is spread across the book:

“the same topic would sometimes be addressed from different perspectives in separate conversations, with the result that the concepts required to fully understand a given subject may be spread across different chapters, or the contents of a subsequent chapter may form the basis for the understanding of an earlier one.”

Chapter 35 is the last chapter of Part 2, which deals with Christian doctrines; it follows chapters on the return of the prophets and on Peter and the papacy, and its examples are Christian (the prophecies of Christ, His martyrdom). Chapters 68 and 70 sit in Part 4, “On the Origin, Powers, and Conditions of Man”, after the chapters on the soul and its immortality. Chapter 69, on the influence of the stars, sits between them. Chapter 70 itself sends the reader elsewhere: its footnote on the potter passage points to chapters 32, 62 and 63.

Chapter 35: “Free Will and Predestination”

The question and the “Guarded Tablet”

“QUESTION: WHEN AN action which someone will perform becomes the object of God’s knowledge and is recorded in the “Guarded Tablet” of destiny, is it possible to resist it?” (¶1)

The question uses the vocabulary of Islamic theology. The “Guarded Tablet” (Arabic al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ, often rendered “Preserved Tablet”) comes from the Qur’án, which says of itself: “Nay, it is a glorious Qur’án, in a Preserved Tablet” (85:21–22). In later Islamic thought the Tablet became the heavenly record on which God’s decree for all things is written before they happen. A related verse, Qur’án 13:39, adds that God “effaces what He wills and confirms, and with Him is the Mother of the Book”. The questioner’s worry is the classic one: if my act is already written, is my choosing anything more than reading out the script?

Bahá’u’lláh uses the same term throughout His Writings, in several senses. Sometimes it is the record of the decree: “Blessed is He Who, in a well-guarded Tablet, hath prescribed a fixed measure unto all things” (Summons of the Lord of Hosts, Súriy-i-Haykal, ¶4). Sometimes it is where God’s knowledge and His freedom to revise meet, as in His words to Czar Alexander II, discussed below: “What He pleaseth will God abrogate or confirm, and with Him is the knowledge of all things in a Guarded Tablet” (¶158). And once He identifies the Tablet with the Manifestation Himself: “By God! The Preserved Tablet hath appeared; it walketh amongst His servants and calleth out: “This is the Day promised unto you in the Holy Books of old.”” (Additional Tablets and Extracts from Tablets Revealed by Bahá’u’lláh). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s answer in chapter 35 does not deny that God knows and records. It denies the inference from knowing and recording to compelling.

¶2. Knowledge is not a cause

“Answer: The knowledge of a thing is not the cause of its occurrence; for the essential knowledge of God encompasses the realities of all things both before and after they come to exist, but it is not the cause of their existence. This is an expression of the perfection of God.” (¶2)

This is the thesis of the chapter. Two points in it are easy to miss. God’s knowledge is complete: it covers things “both before and after they come to exist”. There is no suggestion that the future is hidden from God. And the knowledge is called a perfection, which means the answer is not a concession. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not save freedom by shrinking God’s knowledge.

¶3. Prophecy, and the sunrise in seven hours

“As to the pronouncements which, through divine revelation, have issued from the Prophets regarding the advent of the Promised One of the Torah, these likewise were not the cause of Christ’s appearance. But the hidden mysteries of the days to come were revealed to the Prophets, who thus became acquainted with future events and who proclaimed them in turn. This knowledge and proclamation were not the cause of the occurrence of these events. For instance, tonight everyone knows that in seven hours the sun will rise, but this common knowledge does not cause the appearance and rising of the sun.” (¶3)

The argument moves from God’s knowledge to the prophets’ knowledge, which is easier to picture because it is announced in time. The sunrise example then moves to ordinary human knowledge, which everyone has. The point the example makes is narrow: knowing that something will happen does not make it happen. It does not claim that the sunrise is free. The sunrise is about as fixed as any event can be; it was chosen because the knowledge of it is certain and shared, and still nobody thinks the knowing pushes the sun up.

¶4. Knowledge without past, present or future

“Likewise, God’s knowledge in the contingent world does not produce the forms of things. Rather, that knowledge is freed from the distinctions of past, present, and future, and is identical with the realization of all things without being the cause of that realization.” (¶4)

This is the paragraph a philosopher will notice first. It adds a second idea to the first. God’s knowledge does not just fail to cause; it is not located in time at all, so it is not “fore”-knowledge in the strict sense. It does not sit at a point before the act and reach forward. The phrase “identical with the realization of all things” is compressed and the text does not expand it. A cautious reading: God’s knowing a thing and that thing’s actually coming to be are not two separate events with one causing the other. The section on Boethius below shows how close this is to a solution given in the sixth century.

¶5. The record in Scripture and the martyrdom of Christ

“In the same way, the record and mention of a thing in the Scriptures is not the cause of its existence. The Prophets of God were informed through divine revelation that certain events would come to pass. For instance, through divine revelation they came to know that Christ would be martyred, which they in turn proclaimed. Now, did their knowledge and awareness cause the martyrdom of Christ? No: This knowledge is a sign of their perfection and not the cause of His martyrdom.” (¶5)

Paragraph 5 answers the “recorded” half of the question, as paragraph 2 answered the “known” half. The chosen example is pointed. The martyrdom of Christ was carried out by people who are blamed for it. If prophecy compelled it, the blame would make no sense. The chapter leaves the reader to draw that conclusion about the actors; it states only that the prophecy was not the cause.

¶6. The eclipse: “merely an analogy and not an exact image”

“Through astronomical calculations, the mathematicians determine that at a certain time a solar or lunar eclipse will occur. Surely this prediction is not the cause of the eclipse. This of course is merely an analogy and not an exact image.” (¶6)

The caveat at the end is the most useful sentence in the chapter for a careful discussion. An astronomer predicts an eclipse by calculating from present causes: the positions and motions of the bodies now fix where they will be later. If God’s knowledge of human acts worked like that, it would be knowledge of a determined future, and the question in ¶1 would get the answer “no”. The caveat signals that the eclipse illustrates only the one point, that predicting is not causing, and should not be taken as a model of how God knows. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá uses the same kind of caveat in chapter 40, where He compares the Manifestation’s knowledge with man’s knowledge of the lower kingdoms: “This is only an example and not an exact analogy.” In both places He marks the limit of His own illustration.

What chapter 35 does not say is also worth stating. It does not say how God knows free acts, and it does not use the word “free” after the title. It answers the question asked, whether knowledge and record compel, and leaves the positive account of human agency to chapter 70.

Chapter 68: “Two Kinds of Fate”

The question

“QUESTION: IS FATE, which is mentioned in the Holy Books, an irrevocable thing? If so, what use or benefit will come from seeking to avoid it?” (¶1)

The second half of the question is the practical one. If outcomes are fixed, caution, medicine and prayer look pointless. The answer divides fate in two.

¶2. The irrevocable: the lamp’s oil

“Answer: Fate is of two kinds: One is irrevocable and the other is conditional, or, as it is said, impending. Irrevocable fate is that which cannot be changed or altered, while conditional fate is that which may or may not occur. Thus, the irrevocable fate for this lamp is that its oil will be burnt and consumed. Its eventual extinction is therefore certain, and it is impossible to change or alter this outcome, for such is its irrevocable fate. Likewise, a power has been created in the body of man whose depletion and exhaustion leads inevitably to the disintegration of the body. It is even as the oil in this lamp: After it has been burnt and consumed, the lamp will assuredly be extinguished.” (¶2)

The paragraph makes three points. “As it is said” marks “impending” as a term already in use; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is taking up a distinction His listeners knew. The irrevocable fate is located inside the thing, in its own nature: a lamp has a finite supply of oil, and a body has a finite store of the power that keeps it alive. And the example is plain mortality. Irrevocable fate in this chapter is not a detailed script for each life; the one example given is the limit that a finite nature sets.

¶3. The conditional: the wind

“But conditional fate may be likened to this: While some oil yet remains, a strong wind blows and extinguishes the lamp. This fate is conditional. It is expedient to avoid this fate, to guard oneself against it, and to be cautious and prudent. But the irrevocable fate, which is like the depletion of the oil of the lamp, cannot be changed, altered, or delayed. It is bound to occur, and the lamp will undoubtedly be extinguished.” (¶3)

The conditional fate comes from outside the thing: a wind, an accident, a disease that could have been avoided. The answer to the practical question is direct: “It is expedient to avoid this fate, to guard oneself against it, and to be cautious and prudent.” Prudence is not a lack of trust; it is the right response to the kind of fate that “may or may not occur”.

The same teaching in Bahá’u’lláh

‘Abdu’l-Bahá did not originate the distinction. Bahá’u’lláh states it in a Tablet consoling a bereaved woman, whom He addresses as “the fruit of My Tree and the leaf thereof”:

“Know thou, O fruit of My Tree, that the decrees of the Sovereign Ordainer, as related to fate and predestination, are of two kinds. Both are to be obeyed and accepted. The one is irrevocable, the other is, as termed by men, impending. To the former all must unreservedly submit, inasmuch as it is fixed and settled. God, however, is able to alter or repeal it. As the harm that must result from such a change will be greater than if the decree had remained unaltered, all, therefore, should willingly acquiesce in what God hath willed and confidently abide by the same.

The decree that is impending, however, is such that prayer and entreaty can succeed in averting it.” (Gleanings, LXVIII)

Bahá’u’lláh adds two things that chapter 68 does not state. The irrevocable decree is irrevocable for creatures, not a limit on God: God “is able to alter or repeal it”, and does not, because the change would do more harm. And the means of averting the impending decree include “prayer and entreaty”, alongside the prudence ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names. Read together, the two passages give the conditional decree two remedies: care in action and prayer. The words “as termed by men” match ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s “as it is said”: both treat the name of the second kind as received usage.

A second Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh, in Persian and Arabic and without an authorized English translation, is printed in the compilation Má’idiy-i-Ásmání, volume 1 (chapter 15, pp. 61–63). Its chapter heading reads “در رضای بقضای الهی و قضای محتوم و معلق” (on contentment with the divine decree, and the irrevocable and the suspended decree). What follows is this article’s own working paraphrase of a scanned and machine-read text. It is not an authorized translation and should be checked against the original before it is quoted on air:

  • Bahá’u’lláh notes that, “as is mentioned among the servants”, people speak of two terms (ajal) for every thing, “the first suspended (mu’allaq) and the second irrevocable (maḥtúm)”, and says that what they have said about these two stations does not satisfy, because they lack the root of divine knowledge.
  • He then gives a parable: a burning candle. Its irrevocable term is the end of its tallow and wick. While these last, it burns; when they are used up, its going out is certain, and that moment can be neither advanced nor delayed. But the candle reaches that end only if the “existential terms” created in things do not take it first. Often, before it reaches its end, winds pass over it, or other created causes put it out.
  • He closes with a qualification: in one station every decree that comes to pass was and will be irrevocable, but these distinctions of station and rank are spoken of in view of human limitations and worldly causes, and what is with God and His Will is sanctified from such limits.

If this reading is right, the candle-and-wind analogy of chapter 68 goes back to Bahá’u’lláh, and the last point bears on a question the guest is likely to ask: whether the line between irrevocable and conditional is a fact about the world or a fact about our vantage point. This Tablet appears to say that the distinction is drawn for us, at our level of causes, and that from God’s side the categories do not apply in the same way. That conclusion rests on an unofficial reading and is flagged as such.

Prayer, illness and poverty: the Guardian’s application

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi applies the two kinds of fate to ordinary misfortune:

“As to illness or poverty: Such calamities may be either irrevocable or, and it is often the case, they may be avoided.” (letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 17 July 1937, Lights of Guidance, no. 1750)

“Often” is the operative word. On this reading most illness and poverty belong to the conditional kind, which is the kind the Writings tell us to guard against and pray about.

Chapter 69 between them: the stars and the interconnection of things

Chapter 69 answers whether the stars have a spiritual influence on human souls. It belongs in this discussion because astrology is the oldest form of the belief that a person’s fate is fixed from outside. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá separates two claims. The universe is “like the human body”, its parts “connected one with another” and influencing each other “whether material or spiritual” (¶3). But the old astrology was something else:

“My meaning, however, is not that the deductions that the astrologers of old made from the motions of the stars and planets were true, for these were mere figments of the imagination which had their origin with the Egyptian, Assyrian, and Chaldean priests, or rather stemmed from the idle conjectures of the Hindus and the superstitions of the Greeks, the Romans, and the other star worshippers.” (¶3)

Letters on behalf of Shoghi Effendi draw the practical line. One calls astrology a “pseudo-science” that is “for the most part ‘non-sensical’” (10 July 1939, Lights of Guidance, no. 1747). Another links the subject to free will directly:

“The friends should be encouraged not to waste time on such things as astrology etc., which you mention. They cannot be forbidden to do so. The exercise of our free will to choose to do the right things is much more important.” (letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, Lights of Guidance, no. 1749)

Lights of Guidance dates this letter 30 July 1956 but gives its source as Bahá’í News no. 230 of April 1950; the two cannot both be right, and the date is uncertain.

The chapter’s general claim, that all parts of the universe influence each other, makes the stakes of chapter 70 clearer. Influence is everywhere. What chapter 70 defends is not freedom from influence but a specific freedom inside it.

Chapter 70: “Free Will and Its Limits”

¶1–2. The question, and “most abstruse”

“QUESTION: IS MAN free and unconstrained in all his actions, or is he compelled and constrained?” (¶1)

“Answer: This is one of the most important questions of divinity, and it is most abstruse. God willing, another day we will explain this matter at length from the beginning of our lunch. For now, we will briefly say a few words, as follows.” (¶2)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls the question “most abstruse” and says the answer will be brief. The chapter is a summary, not a treatise. That matters when the guest presses on a gap: some gaps are acknowledged by the speaker himself.

¶3–4. What is chosen and what is compelled

“Certain matters are subject to the free will of man, such as acting with justice and fairness, or injustice and iniquity—in other words, the choice of good or evil actions. It is clear and evident that the will of man figures greatly in these actions. But there are certain matters where man is forced and compelled, such as sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, and material loss: These are not subject to the will of man and he is not accountable for them, for he is compelled to endure them. But he is free in the choice of good and evil actions, and it is of his own accord that he performs them.” (¶3)

“For example, should he so wish, he can pass his days in praise of God, and should he so desire, he can occupy himself with that which is other than Him. He can light the candle of his heart with the flame of the love of God and become a well-wisher of the world, or he can become an enemy of all mankind or set his affections on worldly things; he can choose to be just or iniquitous. All these deeds and actions are under his own control, and he is therefore accountable for them.” (¶4)

The answer does not say “man is free” or “man is compelled”. It divides the field. The domain of freedom is moral: justice or injustice, love of God or attachment to the world, goodwill or enmity. The domain of compulsion is the set of things that happen to us: sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, material loss. Accountability follows the line exactly. We answer for the first list and not for the second.

Two phrases in ¶3 matter. “The will of man figures greatly” says that will is a major factor in moral acts; it does not say will is the only factor. And “of his own accord” places the origin of the choice in the person.

The list of compelled matters overlaps with the conditional fate of chapter 68. Sickness is on the compelled list here, and the Guardian’s letter calls illness often avoidable. The two passages are answering different questions. Chapter 70 says that sickness, when it comes, is not a moral act we are accountable for; chapter 68 says that many sicknesses can be avoided and that we should try. Both can be true together.

¶5. The second question: the potter and the vessels

“But another question arises: Man’s condition is one of utter helplessness and absolute poverty. All might and power belong to God alone, and man’s exaltation and abasement depend on the will and purpose of the Most High. Thus it is said in the Gospel that God is like a potter who makes “one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour”. Now, the dishonoured vessel has no right to reproach the potter, saying, “Why did you not make me a precious cup that would be passed from hand to hand?” The meaning of these words is that souls occupy different stations. That which occupies the lowest station of existence, like the mineral, has no right to object, saying, “O God, why have you denied me the perfections of the plant?” Likewise, the plant has no right to protest that it has been deprived of the perfections of the animal realm. And, similarly, it is not befitting for the animal to complain of the want of human perfections. No, all these things are perfect in their own degree and must pursue the perfections of that degree. As we have said previously, that which is inferior in rank has no right or qualification to aspire to the station and perfections of that which is superior, but must progress within its own degree.” (¶5)

The verse is Romans 9:21 (the footnote in the text). In Christian history it has been one of the main proof-texts for predestination to salvation or damnation: Augustine and Calvin both read it that way. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá reads it differently. The vessels stand for “different stations” of existence, mineral, plant, animal, human, each “perfect in their own degree”. On this reading the potter passage is about rank and capacity, not about who is saved. The footnote sends the reader to chapters 32, 62 and 63, which develop the point. Chapter 32 says that differences of degree among people are not blameworthy, while a lack of faith is:

“Now, the differences among mankind are twofold: One is a difference of degree, and this difference is not blameworthy. The other is a difference with respect to faith and certitude, the absence of which is blameworthy; for the soul must have fallen prey to its own lusts and passions to have been deprived of this bounty and bereft of the attractive power of the love of God.” (ch. 32, ¶7)

Chapter 63 adds that the soul’s progress, in this world and the next, happens within its own degree: “no matter how far the spirit and reality of Peter may progress, it will never reach the degree of the reality of Christ but will progress only within its own inherent limits” (¶2).

So the potter answers a different worry from the one about foreknowledge. The worry here is fairness: if God sets our station, can we complain about it? The answer is that station is a gift, not a verdict, and what we are judged on is our progress within it.

¶6. Dependence on divine assistance: the ship’s steam and its rudder

“Moreover, man’s stillness or motion itself is conditioned upon the aid of God. Should this assistance fail to reach him, he can do neither good nor evil. But when the assistance of the all-bounteous Lord confers existence upon man, he is capable of both good and evil. And should that assistance be cut off, he would become absolutely powerless. That is why the aid and assistance of God are mentioned in the Sacred Scriptures. This condition can be likened to that of a ship that moves by the power of wind or steam. Should this power be cut off, the ship would become entirely unable to move. Nevertheless, in whatever direction the rudder is turned, the power of the steam propels the ship in that direction. If the rudder is turned to the east, the ship moves eastward, and if it is directed to the west, the ship moves west. This motion does not arise from the ship itself, but from the wind or steam.” (¶6)

The ship analogy assigns two things to two sources. The motive power, wind or steam, is God’s; without it the ship cannot move at all, and the person can do “neither good nor evil”. The direction, set by the rudder, is the person’s. The power does not choose the course; it carries the ship whichever way the rudder points.

The analogy answers the problem raised at the start of ¶5, that “all might and power belong to God alone”. If all power is God’s, how can any act be mine? The answer is that power and direction are different things. It also explains a feature of Scripture: “That is why the aid and assistance of God are mentioned in the Sacred Scriptures.” Prayers for strength and promises of confirmation make sense because the power to act is a gift that can be asked for.

¶7. The king and the governor

“In like manner, all the doings of man are sustained by the power of divine assistance, but the choice of good or evil belongs to him alone. It is like when the king appoints an individual as governor of a city, grants him full authority, and shows him that which is just and unjust according to the law. Now, should the governor commit injustice, even though he acts by the power and authority of the king, yet the king would not condone his injustice. And should the governor act with justice, this too would be through the royal authority, and the king would be well pleased and satisfied with his justice.” (¶7)

The ship could suggest that the power is morally neutral, like fuel. The governor analogy adds the moral relationship. The king grants authority and also shows “that which is just and unjust according to the law”. A governor who abuses the authority still acts by it, but the king does not condone the abuse. So the fact that God’s power sustains an evil act does not make God the author of the evil, any more than a king is the author of his governor’s crimes. The analogy also brings in guidance: the governor is not left to guess what justice is.

¶8–9. The summary

“Our meaning is that the choice of good and evil belongs to man, but that under all circumstances he is dependent upon the life-sustaining assistance of Divine Providence. The sovereignty of God is great indeed, and all are held captive in the grasp of His power. The servant can do nothing of his own will alone: God is almighty and all-powerful and bestows His assistance upon all creation.” (¶8)

“This question has been clearly explained and elucidated.” (¶9)

Paragraph 8 holds both claims in one sentence. The choice “belongs to man”; the capacity to carry it out is always dependent. “The servant can do nothing of his own will alone” does not cancel the choice; it says the will alone, without the sustaining power, achieves nothing.

How the three chapters fit together

Each chapter answers a different version of the question “am I free?”, and they do not overlap much.

A short Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá supplies the definitions that tie the three together:

“THOU hadst asked about fate, predestination and will. Fate and predestination consist in the necessary and indispensable relationships which exist in the realities of things. These relationships have been placed in the realities of existent beings through the power of creation and every incident is a consequence of the necessary relationship. For example, God hath created a relation between the sun and the terrestrial globe that the rays of the sun should shine and the soil should yield. These relationships constitute predestination, and the manifestation thereof in the plane of existence is fate. Will is that active force which controlleth these relationships and these incidents. Such is the epitome of the explanation of fate and predestination. I have no time for a detailed explanation. Ponder over this; the reality of fate, predestination and will shall be made manifest.” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, §167)

On this definition, predestination is not a list of events written in advance. It is the set of “necessary and indispensable relationships” built into things: the sun shines, the soil yields. Fate is those relationships working out in the world. The lamp’s oil running out is fate in exactly this sense. The same phrase appears in chapter 40, where ‘Abdu’l-Bahá defines religion: “religion consists in the necessary relationships deriving from the realities of things” (¶8). The world has a lawful structure, and both fate and religion are described as expressions of it. Will, in this Tablet, is “that active force which controlleth these relationships and these incidents”; the Tablet does not say whose will, God’s or man’s or both, and the wording supports more than one reading.

The same themes elsewhere in the Writings and the guidance

Bahá’u’lláh

Foreknowledge and volition. The closest parallel to chapter 35 in Bahá’u’lláh’s Writings is a passage that also answers a question, this time “regarding the creation of man”:

“Know thou that all men have been created in the nature made by God, the Guardian, the Self-Subsisting. Unto each one hath been prescribed a preordained measure, as decreed in God’s mighty and guarded Tablets. All that which ye potentially possess can, however, be manifested only as a result of your own volition. Your own acts testify to this truth.” (Gleanings, LXXVII)

The passage then argues from the breaking of God’s law: “Men, however, have wittingly broken His law. Is such a behavior to be attributed to God, or to their proper selves? Be fair in your judgment. Every good thing is of God, and every evil thing is from yourselves.” And it ends with the same thesis as chapter 35, with the same kind of analogy:

“Every act ye meditate is as clear to Him as is that act when already accomplished. There is none other God besides Him. His is all creation and its empire. All stands revealed before Him; all is recorded in His holy and hidden Tablets. This foreknowledge of God, however, should not be regarded as having caused the actions of men, just as your own previous knowledge that a certain event is to occur, or your desire that it should happen, is not and can never be the reason for its occurrence.” (Gleanings, LXXVII)

Three features matter for the episode. First, this passage combines the two halves of the SAQ teaching: a “preordained measure” for each person (the station of chapter 70’s potter), and potential that is realized “only as a result of your own volition”. Second, it states God’s knowledge of future acts in the strongest terms, “as clear to Him as is that act when already accomplished”, which rules out any reading in which God does not know what we will choose. Third, its analogy adds “or your desire that it should happen”: not only does knowing not cause the event, wanting it does not either. “Every good thing is of God, and every evil thing is from yourselves” echoes Qur’án 4:79 and states the asymmetry that the king-and-governor analogy illustrates.

The Universal House of Justice draws on this passage in a 1985 letter, discussed below, to explain both human responsibility and the presence of evil.

God abrogates and confirms. In the Kitáb-i-Íqán, Bahá’u’lláh describes how, at the appearance of a new Revelation, learned men who reject it lose their station and unlettered people who accept it rise, and He concludes: “Thus, “What He pleaseth will God abrogate or confirm: for with Him is the Source of Revelation.”” (Kitáb-i-Íqán, Part Two, quoting Qur’án 13:39). The same verse appears in His words to Czar Alexander II, which show a decree that depends on the person’s response:

“Wherefore hath God ordained for thee a station which the knowledge of none can comprehend except His knowledge. Beware lest thou barter away this sublime station. Thy Lord, verily, doeth what He willeth. What He pleaseth will God abrogate or confirm, and with Him is the knowledge of all things in a Guarded Tablet.” (Summons of the Lord of Hosts, Súriy-i-Haykal, ¶158)

The station is “ordained”, and the Czar can still “barter away” it. This is a conditional decree stated about a named person.

“The Middle Way.” The Kitáb-i-Aqdas contains a counsel on how to respond to trials:

“Lament not in your hours of trial, neither rejoice therein; seek ye the Middle Way which is the remembrance of Me in your afflictions and reflection over that which may befall you in future.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶43)

The Arabic of “the Middle Way” here is amran bayna al-amrayn, “a matter between the two matters” (verified in the Arabic text of the Aqdas in this corpus). That is the same phrase as the Shí’í formula on free will discussed below. In ¶43 Bahá’u’lláh uses it about one’s inner response to affliction, not about the metaphysics of action, so it should not be cited as a statement on free will. It is still relevant, because a viewer who knows the Islamic background may notice the echo, and because it shows the authorized English rendering of the phrase.

The Báb

The Báb’s Writings hold together the two claims that what befalls a person is decreed and that those who do evil are culpable. Writing to Muḥammad Sháh from prison:

“Whatever reacheth Me is ordained to reach Me; and that which hath come unto Me, to him who giveth will it revert. By the One in Whose hand is My soul, he hath cast no one but himself into prison. For assuredly whatsoever God hath decreed for Me shall come to pass and naught else save that which God hath ordained for us shall ever touch us. Woe betide him from whose hands floweth evil, and blessed the man from whose hands floweth good.” (Selections from the Writings of the Báb, Epistle to Muḥammad Sháh)

The imprisonment is “ordained”, and the jailer has “cast no one but himself into prison”. This is the governor analogy seen from the side of the one wronged. In His address to Sulaymán, a divine of Masqaṭ, the Báb shows the other side: a person can fail the very purpose for which he was created. “Although We had created thee to behold Our countenance, and We did actually alight in thy locality, yet thou didst fail to attain the object of thy creation” (Selections from the Writings of the Báb). God’s purpose in creating a person does not compel that person to fulfil it.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá elsewhere

An explicit “matter between the two matters.” A Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in Arabic, answering several questions and printed in Má’idiy-i-Ásmání, volume 5 (pp. 103–104), takes up “the question of lā jabr wa lā tafwīḍ” (“no compulsion and no delegation”), the Shí’í formula described below. There is no authorized English translation; what follows is this article’s working paraphrase of a machine-read scan, to be checked against the printed text before use on air.

  • The eternal Power moves all things and turns hearts, and man’s share in his acts is his will, inclinations, receptivity and capacity.
  • A man and a tree both move, and God is the mover of both, but the two movements differ: the man’s is by choice, will and inclination, the tree’s by compulsion and without choice. “This is the meaning of no compulsion and no delegation… a matter between the two matters (amr bayn al-amrayn).”
  • There is no compulsion, citing the Qur’án, “We wronged them not, but they wronged themselves” (16:118); and there is no delegation, because God is the mover, “Say: all is from God” (4:78).
  • Then the ship: when a strong wind blows over land and sea it drives the ships east and west, and without it they would never move from their place, so there is no delegation; but if the sailor turns the rudder to the east the ship goes east by the wind’s force, and if to the west, west. He cites Qur’án 17:20, “We aid both these and those from the bounty of thy Lord”, and concludes that there is no compulsion, given man’s will and inclination.
  • He calls this a brief answer and hopes later to set out the full proofs.

The ship analogy, the “no compulsion, no delegation” structure and the promise of a fuller treatment all match chapter 70. If the paraphrase is accurate, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Himself identifies the position of chapter 70 with the Shí’í amr bayn al-amrayn. The scanned text has some apparent machine-reading errors (for example a word in the tree passage that most likely should read “differs”), and the Qur’ánic verse identifications are this article’s.

Volition as what separates man from nature. In His talks in North America, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá repeatedly made the contrast between human will and the compulsion of nature part of His argument for the spiritual reality of man:

“Nature is without volition and acts perforce, whereas man possesses a mighty will. … Man can voluntarily discontinue vices; nature has no power to modify the influence of its instincts.” (Promulgation of Universal Peace, talk at the Baptist Temple, Philadelphia, 9 June 1912)

In another talk He gives the sun as the example of compulsion: “It is controlled—it cannot do otherwise than radiate light—but it is not volitional” (talk at Hotel Plaza, Chicago, 2 May 1912). The sun of chapter 35 appears here in its proper role, as the model of what is not free. The talks in Promulgation were published from notes taken by listeners; they are a valued source but do not carry the authority of a text ‘Abdu’l-Bahá revised, as Some Answered Questions does.

Why freedom matters morally. A Tablet on voluntary sharing states the principle behind the whole teaching: “Man reacheth perfection through good deeds, voluntarily performed, not through good deeds the doing of which was forced upon him.” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, §79). A good act that is compelled does not perfect the one who does it.

Capacity, character and blame. Chapter 57 of Some Answered Questions takes up the objection that people’s capacities differ, so blaming the wicked is unfair. Its answer separates innate capacity, which “is wholly and entirely good”, from acquired capacity, which “can become the cause of evil”:

“It is not on account of their innate capacity and disposition that one reproaches the wicked, but rather on account of that which they themselves have acquired.” (ch. 57, ¶9)

The same chapter gives education an “enormous influence” on character (¶8). The two claims together set up one of the sceptic’s questions below: if acquired character depends on education, how much of it is acquired by the person?

Evil as absence. Chapter 74 argues that “all evil is mere non-existence. Good has a positive existence; evil is merely its absence” (¶7). This bears on the governor analogy: if evil is a lack, then the power God supplies is always a power for something good, and evil is the failure to direct it well.

Grace and attraction. Chapter 32 complicates the picture. It says that faith “is a token of grace and not the result of justice”, and that “The flame of the fire of love, in this world of earth and water, burns by the power of attraction and not through human effort and striving” (¶5). In a talk at Dublin, New Hampshire, on 6 August 1912, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá described the early believers as so attracted to God “that they were dispossessed of volition, deprived of will in His path” (Promulgation, notes by Howard MacNutt). These passages place something central to the spiritual life, the love of God, partly outside the domain of effort. They are taken up among the objections below.

Calamity as a hidden bounty. On the death of a youth, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote to the bereaved that “this predestined decree is considered a bounty”, comparing it to a gardener moving a shrub “from a confined place to a wide open area” (Selections, §169). The irrevocable decree of death is here given a meaning, not just a name.

Shoghi Effendi (letters written on his behalf)

The Guardian’s letters do not develop the metaphysics; they apply it. Besides the letters on illness and astrology quoted above, three are relevant.

On the limits of what anyone, even a Manifestation, can do for another person’s soul:

“There is, unfortunately, no way that one can force his own good upon a man. The element of free will is there and all we believers --and even the Manifestation of God Himself --can do is to offer the truth to mankind. If the people of the world persist, as they seem to be doing, in their blind materialism, they must bear the consequences in a prolongation of their present condition, and even a worsening of it.” (letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 13 October 1947, The Unfolding Destiny of the British Bahá’í Community)

This letter extends free will from the individual to humanity. The world’s suffering is partly the consequence of collective choice, and the “prolongation” of the present condition depends on what people do.

On causal law and divine action, in reply to a question about Einstein’s “Cosmic Religion”:

“According to the Bahá’í conception there is and can be no incompatibility between the idea of causal law and that of an omnipotent and omniscient God, Who, if He deems it fit, may at times interfere with the normal sequence of events in the world, and thus retard or altogether stop the operation of certain laws, whether in the physical universe, or in any other worlds of nature and man.” (letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 6 December 1939, Lights of Guidance, no. 1703)

The letter addresses God’s freedom in relation to natural law, not human freedom. It is useful for the episode because it shows the Bahá’í texts accepting a law-governed world, which is also the premise of chapter 68 and of the “necessary relationships” Tablet.

The Universal House of Justice

Suffering, free will and justice (14 March 1985). A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice to an individual troubled by the suffering of the world gives the fullest institutional treatment of these themes in this corpus. It frames suffering within “God’s great redemptive Major Plan, which is shaping the destiny of mankind according to the operation of the divine Will”, then turns to individual responsibility, quoting Gleanings LXXVII:

“Is it not an evidence of the justice of God that each of us, whether materially comfortable or struggling for physical survival, is assessed in terms of the efforts we have made to seize whatever opportunities existed in our lives, to develop and use our allotted talent, be it large or small?”

“In the same passage from the Gleanings, Bahá’u’lláh also raises the possibility that possessing free will, human beings may well commit evil and “wittingly” break “His law.” By the exercise of his free will, man either affirms his spiritual purpose in life or chooses to perpetuate evil by living below his highest station.” (14 March 1985)

The letter compares the passage to the parable of the talents, which fits the potter of chapter 70: the “allotted talent” differs, and the assessment is of effort within it.

Choosing to recognize the Manifestation (1988, 1992). Two letters of the House of Justice describe the recognition of the Manifestation and obedience to His laws, the “twin duties” of the opening of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, as the highest use of free will:

“To accept the Prophet of God in His time and to abide by His bidding are the two essential, inseparable duties which each soul was created to fulfill. One exercises these twin duties by one’s own choice, an act constituting the highest expression of the free will with which every human being has been endowed by an all-loving Creator.” (29 December 1988, to the Bahá’ís of the United States)

The 26 November 1992 message on the centenary of the Day of the Covenant repeats the point in almost the same words. A related letter on family life applies it to deference: “Deciding to forgo one’s personal wishes in deference to one’s parents—or to the institutions of the Faith, civil authorities, or the law for that matter—represents an expression of free will, not a curtailment of it.” (Family Life compilation, letter written on behalf of the House of Justice).

God’s Plan and ours (8 December 1967). A letter to an individual distinguishes two levels of providence:

“The working out of God’s Major Plan proceeds mysteriously in ways directed by Him alone, but the Minor Plan that He has given us to execute, as our part in His grand design for the redemption of mankind, is clearly delineated.” (8 December 1967)

This is a collective version of the two kinds of fate, though the letter does not use that language. The outcome of the Major Plan is assured; how it unfolds depends in part on whether people carry out the work given to them. The statement The Promise of World Peace (October 1985) puts the conditional side plainly: “Whether peace is to be reached only after unimaginable horrors precipitated by humanity’s stubborn clinging to old patterns of behavior, or is to be embraced now by an act of consultative will, is the choice before all who inhabit the earth.” Reading these as the irrevocable and the conditional at the scale of history is this article’s interpretation, not a statement in the texts.

Inner promptings (16 July 1980). A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice to a believer struggling with homosexual inclinations states a general principle about the purpose of earthly life: “Man’s physical existence on this earth is a period during which the moral exercise of his free will is tried and tested in order to prepare his soul for the other worlds of God”. It goes on to place that believer’s struggle alongside others shared by everyone: “every human being is beset by such inner promptings as pride, greed, selfishness” and other desires that “must be overcome” (Lights of Guidance, no. 1228). For the episode, the relevant point is the general one: inclinations are treated as the material on which free will is tested, not as proof that there is no free will.

Free will and spiritual search. One Common Faith (2005), a statement prepared under the supervision of the House of Justice, applies the principle to teaching: “Because free will is an inherent endowment of the soul, each person who is drawn to explore Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings will need to find his own place in the never-ending continuum of spiritual search.”

The debate these chapters entered

This section describes the history of the question in Islam, in Christianity and in modern philosophy and science, using outside scholarship and web sources listed in the references. It describes the field. It is not authority for any Bahá’í teaching, and where it compares a passage of the Writings with an earlier thinker, the comparison is this article’s observation.

The Islamic debate

The questioner in chapter 35 used Islamic vocabulary, and the positions ‘Abdu’l-Bahá steers between were first named in Islam. The Qur’án contains verses that stress God’s decree (qadar, qaḍā’) and verses that stress human responsibility, and early Muslims divided over how to hold them together. H. M. Balyuzi’s Muhammad and the Course of Islam, which is in this corpus, gives a compact account; W. Montgomery Watt’s Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam (1948) is the classic study in English.

  • The Jabriyya (“compulsionists”, from jabr, compulsion) held that God creates every act and that attributing action to human beings is figurative. Balyuzi reports their image: “When we speak of a river flowing, they said, we are speaking figuratively, because the river, in truth, has no will of its own to flow.” Jahm ibn Ṣafwān (d. 746) is usually named as the leading figure.
  • The Qadariyya asserted human free will against this. Balyuzi names Ma’bad al-Juhanī, executed in 699, and Ghaylān of Damascus. Their opponents circulated a saying, which Balyuzi notes is of very doubtful authenticity, that “The proponents of free will are the Magians of my people”.
  • The Mu’tazila, founded by Wāṣil ibn ‘Aṭā’ in Basra in the eighth century, made God’s justice (’adl) central: a just God cannot punish people for acts He compelled. Balyuzi summarizes their position in words close to the ship of chapter 70: “All power comes from God, but man determines the use that power is put to, because God, although omnipotent and capable of forcing human beings to act, does not do so.” Their opponents accused them of tafwīḍ, “delegation”: making human beings autonomous creators of their own acts.
  • The Ash’arites, after Abu’l-Ḥasan al-Ash’arī (d. 935–36), became the dominant Sunni school. They held that God creates every act, and that the human being “acquires” (kasb) it: the person has a created power that accompanies the act, which makes the act his for purposes of responsibility, but that power has no causal effect of its own. Critics have long asked whether “acquisition” names a real difference or only relabels compulsion. The Māturīdī school gave somewhat more room to human choice.
  • The Imāmī (Twelver) Shí’a adopted a formula attributed to the Imams, most often to Ja’far al-Ṣādiq (d. 765): lā jabr wa lā tafwīḍ bal amr bayn al-amrayn, “no compulsion and no delegation, but a matter between the two matters”. It is recorded in al-Kulaynī’s al-Kāfī (vol. 1) and Ibn Bābawayh’s Kitāb al-Tawḥīd. It denies the Jabrī claim that humans do not act, and the claim attributed to the Mu’tazila that humans act independently of God. One analogy in the Shí’í tradition: if you forbid a man a crime and he commits it anyway, you did not command it by leaving him free.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s position in chapter 70 has the shape of the Shí’í formula: the moral choice is the person’s (no jabr), and the power to act is always God’s (no tafwīḍ). The Arabic Tablet in Má’idiy-i-Ásmání described above, if read correctly, makes the identification explicitly and uses the same ship analogy. So the brief’s question can be answered from a primary source: the echo is intended. The conclusion depends on an unofficial reading of an untranslated text, and should be presented that way.

Two other pieces of the Islamic background bear on chapter 68. The Qur’án speaks of two terms (ajal): “He it is who created you from clay, then decreed a term, and a term named is with Him” (6:2), and later thinkers built on this a distinction between a fixed term and a suspended one. Bahá’u’lláh’s candle Tablet refers to exactly this popular distinction (people, it says, have posited two terms for every thing). And a ḥadīth in al-Tirmidhī’s collection (no. 2139, Book of Qadar) says: “Nothing turns back the Decree except supplication, and nothing increases the life-span except righteousness.” Its chain of transmission is graded ḥasan (acceptable) by some ḥadīth scholars and weak by others. It is the Islamic precedent for the statement in Gleanings LXVIII that “prayer and entreaty” can avert the impending decree.

The Christian debate

Paul and the potter. Romans 9 is the root text. Paul’s potter (9:21) has been read, from Augustine on, as describing God’s free choice of some for salvation. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reading of the same verse as a statement about degrees of existence is a sharp departure from that tradition, and the guest may find it the most surprising move in chapter 70.

Origen (third century), in his commentary on Romans, stated the principle that chapter 35 opens with. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on foreknowledge quotes him: “it will not be because God knows that an event will occur that it happens”. The thing is known because it will happen; it does not happen because it is known.

Augustine and Pelagius. Pelagius (d. after 418) taught that human beings can choose the good by their own natural powers, aided by God’s law and example. Augustine answered that after the Fall the will cannot choose the good without God’s grace, and that God’s grace is given to the elect. Pelagius’s views were condemned at the Council of Carthage in 418. Chapter 70’s ¶6, that without God’s aid man “can do neither good nor evil”, is anti-Pelagian in the sense that no act is possible without God. But ch. 70 also gives the power for both good and evil to all, and leaves the direction to the person, which is closer to what Augustine’s opponents defended than to Augustine’s later views on election.

On foreknowledge, Augustine gave an analogy very close to Gleanings LXXVII and chapter 35. In On Free Choice of the Will (III.4.11) he writes: “Sicut enim tu memoria tua non cogis facta esse quae praeterierunt; sic Deus praescientia sua non cogit facienda quae futura sunt” — “Just as you do not by your memory compel past things to have happened, so God by His foreknowledge does not compel future things to happen” (this article’s translation; Latin verified at augustinus.it). The sentence just before it asks why a just God should not punish “what, foreknowing, He does not compel” (quae fieri non cogit praescius). Bahá’u’lláh’s analogy uses foreknowledge of the future where Augustine uses memory of the past; the logic is the same.

Boethius. The parallel the brief asked about is close. In The Consolation of Philosophy (c. 524), Book V, Prose 6, Boethius answers the problem of foreknowledge in two steps. First, God is eternal, and eternity is “the possession of endless life whole and perfect at a single moment” (H. R. James’s translation; the Latin is interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio). So God’s knowledge is not foreknowledge:

“thou wilt more rightly deem it not foreknowledge as of something future, but knowledge of a moment that never passes.”

Second, knowing a thing does not impose necessity on it, and Boethius illustrates this with a sunrise:

“For even as ye, when at one and the same time ye see a man walking on the earth and the sun rising in the sky, distinguish between the two, though one glance embraces both, and judge the former voluntary, the latter necessary action: so also the Divine vision in its universal range of view does in no wise confuse the characters of the things which are present to its regard, though future in respect of time.”

Set beside chapter 35:

The two passages use the sunrise for slightly different purposes. Boethius uses it to show that one act of knowing can take in a necessary event and a free one without confusing them. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá uses it to show that knowing does not cause. The resemblance in the timelessness claim is strong. This corpus contains nothing to show whether ‘Abdu’l-Bahá knew Boethius, and the idea of God’s timeless knowledge was also familiar in Islamic philosophy, so no line of influence should be claimed.

Aquinas: a contrast. Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae I, q. 14, a. 8) answers “yes” to the question “Whether the knowledge of God is the cause of things”, explaining that God’s knowledge causes things insofar as His will is joined to it, the way an artist’s knowledge causes the work. Aquinas also holds, with Boethius, that God sees future contingent things as present in eternity (q. 14, a. 13). Chapter 35’s flat statement that “The knowledge of a thing is not the cause of its occurrence” reads differently from Aquinas’s article. Whether the two conflict depends on what “knowledge” refers to: ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is speaking of God’s knowledge of particular acts, and ch. 70 attributes the existence and power of the agent to God’s assistance, which is close to what Aquinas means by God’s causal knowledge joined to will. This is a point where a philosopher guest may press, and where the texts need careful handling.

Concurrence. Christian and Islamic scholastics distinguished three views of how God’s power relates to a creature’s act. Occasionalism (associated with the Ash’arites and with Malebranche) says God is the only true cause. Concurrentism (Aquinas, Molina) says God and the creature both cause the act, each in its own way. Mere conservationism (Durandus) says God only keeps the creature and its powers in existence, and the creature acts by itself. The ship of chapter 70, in which God supplies the power continuously and the person supplies the direction, sits between the last two. Alfred Freddoso’s “God’s General Concurrence with Secondary Causes: Why Conservation Is Not Enough” (Philosophical Perspectives 5, 1991) is a standard modern treatment of this map.

Calvin, Arminius and Dort. John Calvin (Institutes, 1559, III.21–24) taught “double predestination”: God’s eternal decree determines who is saved and who is damned. Jacobus Arminius (d. 1609) argued that God elects those He foresees will believe, and that grace can be resisted. The Synod of Dort (1618–19) condemned the Arminian position. Chapter 70 sides with neither on election, since it does not discuss election at all; but its claim that “the choice of good or evil belongs to him alone”, and its reading of the potter as about degrees, are far from Calvin.

Molina and open theism. Luis de Molina (Concordia, 1588) proposed that God knows “counterfactuals of freedom”, what any free creature would do in any circumstances, and uses this “middle knowledge” in governing the world. In the twentieth century, open theists (Clark Pinnock, John Sanders, Gregory Boyd) rejected exhaustive foreknowledge, holding that free future acts are not yet there to be known. Gleanings LXXVII (“Every act ye meditate is as clear to Him as is that act when already accomplished”) rules out open theism for the Bahá’í texts. The texts say nothing for or against middle knowledge.

The modern debate

Modern philosophy usually frames the question without God, as a question about causation.

  • Determinism is the thesis that the state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes every later state. Pierre-Simon Laplace’s Philosophical Essay on Probabilities (1814) gave it its best-known image, an intellect that knows every force and position and for which “nothing would be uncertain”. The religious version, theological fatalism, was given a precise modern form by Nelson Pike, “Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action” (Philosophical Review, 1965).
  • Compatibilism holds that freedom and determinism are compatible: an act is free if it flows from the agent’s own desires and reasoning without external constraint. Hobbes and Hume (Enquiry, 1748, §8) held versions of it; Jonathan Edwards (Freedom of the Will, 1754) gave a Calvinist one. Harry Frankfurt’s 1969 paper “Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility” (Journal of Philosophy 66) argued that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. Daniel Dennett (Elbow Room, 1984; Freedom Evolves, 2003) is a leading current defender.
  • Libertarianism (in the metaphysical sense) holds that free will is incompatible with determinism and that we have it, so determinism is false for free acts. Versions include event-causal accounts (Robert Kane, The Significance of Free Will, 1996) and agent-causal accounts, in which the agent, not a prior event, causes the choice (Roderick Chisholm, Timothy O’Connor). Peter van Inwagen’s An Essay on Free Will (1983) gave the “Consequence Argument” for incompatibilism.
  • Hard incompatibilism and free-will scepticism hold that we lack the kind of freedom needed for deserved praise and blame, whether or not determinism is true (Galen Strawson, “The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility”, 1994; Derk Pereboom, Living Without Free Will, 2001).

The 2020 PhilPapers survey of professional philosophers (Bourget and Chalmers, Philosophers’ Imprint, 2023) found, as commonly reported, about 59 per cent leaning towards compatibilism, about 19 per cent towards libertarianism and about 11 per cent towards no free will. These figures come from secondary reports; the official results page could not be retrieved for this article.

Neuroscience. Benjamin Libet and colleagues (Brain 106, 1983) recorded a “readiness potential” in the brain beginning several hundred milliseconds before subjects reported being aware of deciding to flex a wrist. Many took this to show that the brain decides before the mind knows. Libet himself argued that consciousness could still veto the act. Chun Siong Soon, John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience 11, 2008) reported that fMRI patterns predicted which of two buttons a subject would press up to about ten seconds before awareness, though only modestly above chance. Critics have challenged the inference. Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt and Stanislas Dehaene (PNAS, 2012) modelled the readiness potential as the build-up of random neural noise, not as a decision already made. Uri Maoz, Gideon Yaffe, Christof Koch and Liad Mudrik (eLife, 2019) found a readiness potential before arbitrary choices but not before deliberate ones (decisions about donating real money to charities), which suggests the classic experiments, all of which used arbitrary choices, may say little about moral decisions. Recent books take opposite sides: Robert Sapolsky’s Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will (2023) argues that biology and environment leave no room for free will; Kevin Mitchell’s Free Agents: How Evolution Gave Us Free Will (2023) argues that organisms are real agents. Findings in this area are active and may change.

Where these chapters sit on the map

Placing chapter 70 in modern terms requires care, because the texts do not use these categories.

  • On God and foreknowledge, chapter 35 and Gleanings LXXVII take the classical position of Origen, Augustine and Boethius: exhaustive knowledge, which is not causal, and (in ch. 35 ¶4) timeless. Open theism is excluded.
  • On divine action, chapter 70 rejects both occasionalism (and the Jabrī and Ash’arite positions close to it) and full autonomy (tafwīḍ). It is a version of the Shí’í middle position.
  • On natural determinism, the texts affirm a law-governed nature (“necessary relationships”; the Guardian’s 1939 letter) and deny that human beings are simply part of it. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks say that “Nature is without volition and acts perforce, whereas man possesses a mighty will”, and that “in man there is a power beyond the limitations of nature” (Promulgation, talks of 9 June 1912 in Philadelphia and 15 April 1912 in New York). This reads most naturally as a libertarian view in which the rational soul, which the Writings treat as non-material, is the source of choice. But the texts never say whether a free choice is uncaused, or caused by the soul’s own reasons, or something else, so a compatibilist reading of the soul’s choices cannot be ruled out from these passages alone.
  • On responsibility and luck, chapters 32, 57 and 70 accept that capacities differ by birth and by education, and locate responsibility in effort and in what is acquired. That is a position on a question that modern sceptics such as Strawson press hard.

Questions a sceptic would raise

Each question below gives the objection, what the Bahá’í texts say, and what they leave open. Where an answer rests on this article’s reasoning and not on a text, it says so.

1. Does God’s foreknowledge really leave the future open?

The objection. Chapter 35 answers a weaker problem than the real one. Nobody claims that God’s knowledge pushes events into being. The problem is logical: if God infallibly knew yesterday that I will do X tomorrow, it is now impossible for me to do otherwise, because I cannot make God’s past belief false. “Knowledge is not a cause” does not touch this. (This is Pike’s 1965 argument.)

What the texts say. Taken alone, chapter 35 ¶2 answers only the causal version. But ¶4 adds that God’s knowledge “is freed from the distinctions of past, present, and future”. If God’s knowledge has no date, there is no “God believed yesterday” to be fixed, and Pike’s argument loses its premise. This is Boethius’s answer, and philosophers still debate whether it works (some argue that a timeless being cannot know what time it is now, or that timeless knowledge of my act is as fixed as past knowledge). Gleanings LXXVII states the strength of God’s knowledge without qualification: “Every act ye meditate is as clear to Him as is that act when already accomplished.”

What is left open. The texts do not explain how a timeless knowledge relates to events in time, and the phrase “identical with the realization of all things” in ¶4 is not expanded anywhere in this corpus. The eclipse caveat tells us God’s knowledge is not prediction from causes, but not what it is instead. The texts also never say the future is “open” in the sense of being unknown. They say it is known and not compelled.

2. If the prophets foretold Christ’s martyrdom, could His killers have done otherwise?

The objection. This is the Judas problem. If the betrayal and the crucifixion were foretold, the people who carried them out had no real alternative, so blaming them is unjust.

What the texts say. Chapter 35 ¶5 says the prophets’ knowledge was “a sign of their perfection and not the cause of His martyrdom”. The Báb’s epistle to Muḥammad Sháh holds both sides together: what befell Him was “ordained”, and the one who imprisoned Him “hath cast no one but himself into prison”. The king-and-governor analogy gives the structure: an act can fulfil a larger purpose and still be the agent’s own wrong.

What is left open. The texts do not say what would have happened had the actors chosen otherwise, or how a prophecy could be certain if its fulfilment depended on free choices. They imply that God’s knowledge of the free choice is what the prophecy draws on; how that knowledge is had is the same open question as in question 1.

3. What does dependence on divine assistance add? Isn’t it either empty or deterministic?

The objection. Either the divine assistance determines what I do, in which case I am not free, or it is a power I use as I choose, in which case it adds nothing to an ordinary account of freedom. If God supplies the power for evil acts, God is complicit.

What the texts say. Chapter 70 assigns the assistance a definite role: it is the condition of acting at all (“Should this assistance fail to reach him, he can do neither good nor evil”), not the source of the direction. So it is not deterministic. It is not empty either, for three reasons the texts give or imply. It denies human autonomy in the strong sense (tafwīḍ): the ability to act is always on loan. It explains why “the aid and assistance of God are mentioned in the Sacred Scriptures”, so prayer for strength is coherent. And it answers the complicity charge through the governor analogy: acting by someone’s authority is not acting with their approval, and the king has also shown “that which is just and unjust according to the law”. Chapter 74’s account of evil as absence adds that what God supplies is always being, and evil is a failure in its use.

What is left open. The governor analogy leaves an obvious follow-up: a king who knew his governor would commit a crime, and could revoke his authority at once, might be thought responsible for not doing so. The texts do not take up that question here. The broader problem of why God permits evil is addressed elsewhere (tests as means of growth; the 1985 letter’s “mystery that the mind of man cannot fathom”), but chapter 70 does not claim to solve it.

4. How does the irrevocable/conditional distinction relate to prayer and prudence? How would anyone know which kind of fate they face?

The objection. The distinction is unfalsifiable. Whatever happens can be called irrevocable after the fact, and whatever is avoided can be called conditional. And if I cannot tell in advance which kind I face, the distinction gives no practical guidance.

What the texts say. Chapter 68 gives the practical rule for conditional fate: “It is expedient to avoid this fate, to guard oneself against it, and to be cautious and prudent.” Gleanings LXVIII adds prayer: “The decree that is impending, however, is such that prayer and entreaty can succeed in averting it.” For irrevocable fate the rule is acceptance: “all, therefore, should willingly acquiesce in what God hath willed”. The Guardian’s 1937 letter says illness and poverty are “often” avoidable. And the irrevocable fate in chapter 68 has a recognisable mark: it comes from the thing’s own nature (the oil runs out, the body’s power is exhausted), while conditional fate comes from outside (the wind).

What is left open. No text in this corpus gives a test for telling the two apart in a given case. A reasonable reading of the passages together is that one should act as though a threatened outcome is conditional, with prudence and prayer, and accept with contentment whatever happens once it has happened. That is this article’s synthesis, not a stated rule. On the falsifiability point, the texts do not present the distinction as an empirical hypothesis; Bahá’u’lláh’s candle Tablet, on the reading given above, says it is drawn in view of human limitations and worldly causes (working paraphrase), which concedes that it describes things from our side.

5. If prayer can change outcomes, does God change His mind?

The objection. If God knows everything timelessly, prayer cannot alter anything. If prayer alters things, God’s will is changeable.

What the texts say. The texts affirm both that God’s knowledge is complete and that prayer can avert the impending decree. Bahá’u’lláh repeats the Qur’ánic “What He pleaseth will God abrogate or confirm” (Íqán; the Tablet to the Czar), and His words to the Czar show a station “ordained” and still able to be lost. One way to fit these together, consistent with chapter 35’s timelessness: if God knows timelessly, He knows the prayer and the outcome together, so the prayer can be one of the conditions on which the outcome depends without any change in God. The Islamic tradition made a similar move (“destiny includes preventing tribulation through supplication”, al-Ghazālī).

What is left open. The Bahá’í texts in this corpus do not state that reconciliation; it is this article’s. They also do not discuss the Shí’í doctrine of badā’ (God’s revising a decree), which a guest familiar with Islam may raise.

6. Isn’t the list of “compelled” matters too tidy? Much sickness and loss comes from our own or others’ choices.

The objection. Chapter 70 puts sickness, misfortune and material loss among things “not subject to the will of man”. But smokers choose lung disease, and fraud victims lose money through someone else’s free act.

What the texts say. Chapter 70’s point is about accountability: these things are “not subject to the will of man and he is not accountable for them, for he is compelled to endure them”. It is a claim about the person who suffers them as suffering, not a claim that no choice anywhere contributed. Chapter 68, the Guardian’s letter on illness, and the governor analogy (a third party’s injustice is that party’s own) cover the other cases.

What is left open. The texts do not discuss misfortunes a person brings on himself, where both lists apply at once. A natural reading is that the choice that led to the illness falls under the moral list and the illness itself under the compelled one, but that is interpretation.

7. Capacities differ by birth and upbringing. Is it fair to judge people at all?

The objection. Some people are born with weak constitutions, poor intelligence or bad tempers, and raised badly. What they “acquire” is itself the result of what they were given. Responsibility is luck all the way down (Strawson’s “Basic Argument”).

What the texts say. The texts accept the premise that people differ: chapter 57 describes innate, inherited and acquired character and gives education an “enormous influence”; chapter 32 says differences of degree are “not blameworthy”. They answer the conclusion in two ways. People are judged within their degree: the 1985 letter speaks of each being “assessed in terms of the efforts we have made ... to develop and use our allotted talent, be it large or small”. And reproach attaches to what is acquired, not to what is innate (ch. 57 ¶9). The potter passage adds that the lower station has no grievance: every degree is “perfect” in itself.

What is left open. The texts do not say where the line between given and acquired falls in a particular person, or how much of an acquired character the person chose. They assign judgment to God, who sees “allotted talent” and effort together, and the House of Justice’s letter speaks of “the possibility of the operations of God’s mercy in terms of compensation for earthly suffering, in the next life”. The sceptic’s point, that this line cannot be drawn by any outside observer, is one the texts do not dispute.

8. Doesn’t neuroscience show that the brain decides before we do?

The objection. Libet and Soon show that brain activity predicts choices before awareness. If decisions are brain events, and brain events follow physical law, the “rudder” is just more machinery.

What the texts say. Some Answered Questions treats the human spirit as non-material and connected with the body without being located in it: “The spirit is absolutely placeless and immaterial, but it has a connection with the body”, and “The mind, therefore, has no place, although it is connected with the brain” (ch. 67, ¶5–6). Chapter 61 adds that the spirit ordinarily acts “through the mediation of bodily instruments and organs”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s talks state that “in man there is a power beyond the limitations of nature” (Promulgation, 15 April 1912). The texts also hold that religion must agree with science; the foreword to the 2014 translation of Some Answered Questions puts it in one line: “Religious belief should not contradict science and reason.” The Guardian’s 1939 letter accepts causal law as fully compatible with God.

What is left open. The Bahá’í texts do not discuss neuroscience. On the science itself, the strong conclusion is contested: the reinterpretation by Schurger and colleagues and the finding by Maoz and colleagues that the readiness potential is absent before deliberate choices both weaken the inference from the classic experiments to moral decisions. How a non-material soul interacts with a law-governed brain is a version of the old mind-body problem, and the texts in this corpus do not give a mechanism.

9. If faith and the love of God come by grace and “attraction”, how is anyone responsible for lacking them?

The objection. Chapter 32 says faith “is a token of grace and not the result of justice”, and that the fire of love “burns by the power of attraction and not through human effort and striving”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá described early believers as “dispossessed of volition”. Then the most important thing in the spiritual life is not chosen, and chapter 70’s claim that the choice of good and evil “belongs to him alone” looks too strong.

What the texts say. Chapter 32 ¶7 locates the blame: the soul that lacks faith “must have fallen prey to its own lusts and passions to have been deprived of this bounty”. The reading this suggests is that the grace is offered and the turning away is chosen. The House of Justice calls the acceptance of the Manifestation “the highest expression of the free will”, so recognition is treated as a choice. The Guardian’s 1947 letter puts the two sides together: the Manifestation can “offer the truth”, and the “element of free will is there”.

What is left open. The relation between grace and choice is one of the hardest questions in every theistic tradition, and these passages do not set out a full account of it. The texts affirm both grace and responsibility and explain the failure to receive grace by the soul’s own attachments. Whether that fully answers the objection is a fair question for the guest to press.

10. Is moral freedom the only freedom? What about everything else we choose?

The objection. Chapter 70 lists only moral choices as free. Choosing a career, a spouse or what to eat is not mentioned. Is the claim that only moral choices are free?

What the texts say. Chapter 70 says “Certain matters are subject to the free will of man, such as...” and gives moral examples. “Such as” marks examples, not a complete list. Other texts treat ordinary choices as free; for instance, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on marriage: “first thou must choose one who is pleasing to thee” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, §85).

What is left open. The chapter’s focus on moral choice reflects its concern with accountability. It does not say whether non-moral choices are free in the same sense, and the texts do not offer a theory of the difference.

11. Isn’t “submission to God’s will” the end of free will?

The objection. A religion that asks for obedience to revealed law and contentment with God’s decree is asking people to give up their freedom.

What the texts say. The House of Justice describes the choice to accept the Manifestation and obey His laws as “the highest expression of the free will” (1988, 1992), and a letter on family life calls deference to parents and institutions “an expression of free will, not a curtailment of it”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “Man reacheth perfection through good deeds, voluntarily performed”. On this view obedience is something one chooses and goes on choosing, and its value depends on its being chosen.

What is left open. A sceptic may reply that a choice made under the threat of spiritual loss is not fully free. The texts do not address that version of the objection directly.
