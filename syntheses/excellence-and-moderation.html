Excellence and Moderation: How the Writings Ask Us to Decide How Hard to Push

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

Two commands sit next to each other in the Bahá’í Writings and appear, on a first reading, to pull in opposite directions. Bahá’u’lláh raises ordinary paid work to the rank of worship and states that “in every art and skill, God loveth the highest perfection.” ‘Abdu’l-Bahá tells craftsmen to “exert their highest endeavour” until their work manifests “the greatest beauty and perfection before the eyes of all men,” and tells Bahá’í children to learn in a month what other children learn in a year. Against that, Bahá’u’lláh writes that “in all matters moderation is desirable” and that anything carried to excess “will prove a source of evil” — including, explicitly, civilisation itself.

My position: these are not two settings of one dial, and reading them as a compromise is the error that does the damage. The two commands govern different objects. Excellence governs the standard of the work in front of you. Moderation governs the relationship between the goods you are pursuing. The Writings never ask you to do a job less well. They ask you not to pursue one good — even a genuine one — to the exclusion of the others you owe. The Universal House of Justice named the mechanism precisely in a letter of 3 January 1979: the characteristic failure of the human mind is “to concentrate on one virtue, one aspect of truth, one goal, to the exclusion of others,” which “leads, in extreme cases, to fanaticism… and in all cases to some degree of imbalance.” Excess, on this account, is not a quantity of effort. It is a structural fact about a life — a portfolio problem, not an intensity problem.

Three consequences follow, and they are the practical yield of the pair:

  • When you are over-extended, cut commitments, not craftsmanship. The instruction to halve your effort inside a task has no basis in the Writings; the instruction to stop pursuing one good at the cost of the rest is everywhere in them.
  • The excesses actually named are on both sides. Bahá’u’lláh prohibits monasticism, asceticism and self-imposed austerity as firmly as He prohibits idleness and begging, and Shoghi Effendi went out of his way to say the standard of a chaste and holy life “is not to be associated or confused with any form of asceticism, or of excessive and bigoted puritanism.” A believer wearing themselves out in service is not erring on the safe side.
  • Excellence is asymptotic, which is why it cannot be a finish line. The community’s own junior-youth text on the subject puts it in words a thirteen-year-old can hold: there are “infinite degrees of perfection and we can never claim that we have acquired them all.” A standard with no terminus cannot be discharged by exhaustion; it can only be served by a pace you can hold.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest on a reader deciding how hard to push: the diagnostic question is not “am I working too many hours?” It is “what have I stopped doing?” Health, family, the daily devotional core, sleep, the obligations of a trade — when one of these has quietly gone missing to feed another, the Writings’ word for that condition is excess, whatever the hour count says. The body, in the Guardian’s secretary’s memorable image, is “like a horse which carries the personality and spirit, and as such should be well cared for so it can do its work.” A rider who runs the horse into the ground has not been zealous. He has been imprudent, and he will arrive nowhere.

The standard the Writings set is not a moderate one

Any account of moderation that starts by softening the demand has misread the corpus, because the demand is startling. It reaches the ordinary trades directly, and it reaches them with the language of perfection.

Bahá’u’lláh’s briefest statement on craft is also His most exacting, and the Research Department’s 1981 compilation Excellence in All Things places it first among the passages on the subject:

“As for what the Supreme Pen hath previously set down, the reason is that in every art and skill, God loveth the highest perfection.” — Bahá’u’lláh, in Excellence in All Things

Not competence. Not sufficiency for the purpose. The highest perfection — and the sentence is framed as an explanation, the reason underlying a prior instruction, which means it is offered as a general principle rather than as praise for a particular piece of work. Elsewhere Bahá’u’lláh states the same expectation of the person rather than the product: “Strain every nerve to acquire both inner and outer perfections, for the fruit of the human tree hath ever been and will ever be perfections both within and without. It is not desirable that a man be left without knowledge or skills, for he is then but a barren tree.” The image is agricultural and unsentimental. A person without a developed skill is not neutral; he is a tree that bears nothing.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá addresses the working craftsman directly, and the sentence is one of the few places in the Writings where the aesthetic standard of ordinary manufacture is made an object of divine attention:

“It behoveth the craftsmen of the world at each moment to offer a thousand tokens of gratitude at the Sacred Threshold, and to exert their highest endeavour and diligently pursue their professions so that their efforts may produce that which will manifest the greatest beauty and perfection before the eyes of all men.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 127

Two things in that passage are easy to skim past. The first is “at each moment” — the obligation is continuous rather than occasional, attaching to the working day rather than to a career. The second is “before the eyes of all men.” The standard is public and visible; the craftsman’s work is going to be looked at, and its quality is treated as a witness.

The same demand is put to artists with a phrase that will land uncomfortably on anyone with a folder of unfinished projects. Writing about music, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says the art “must be brought to the highest stage of development,” and then adds the condition: “one must endeavour to attain the degree of artistic perfection and not be like those who leave matters unfinished.” Abandonment mid-way is named as the characteristic failure — not lack of talent, not slowness, but leaving things unfinished.

And the demand reaches children, in language that has embarrassed more than one Bahá’í parent:

“It is incumbent upon Bahá’í children to surpass other children in the acquisition of sciences and arts, for they have been cradled in the grace of God. Whatever other children learn in a year, let Bahá’í children learn in a month.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 119

Speaking to Persian students in 1918, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá was blunter still: “I hope that while you are studying in this college you may so excel all other students in the various branches of knowledge taught therein that all of them may testify that the Bahá’í students have another power… If you do not surpass the others, then what distinction will there remain for you?” (Star of the West, vol. 9, no. 9, 20 August 1918).

Anyone building a case for taking it easy has to get past that paragraph, and cannot. But the same speaker, in Chicago on 30 April 1912, drew a line around what kind of distinction He was asking for, and the two passages must be read together:

“I desire distinction for you. The Bahá’ís must be distinguished from others of humanity. But this distinction must not depend upon wealth—that they should become more affluent than other people. I do not desire for you financial distinction. It is not an ordinary distinction I desire; not scientific, commercial, industrial distinction. For you I desire spiritual distinction—that is, you must become eminent and distinguished in morals.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, p. 190

Held together, the two passages give the shape of the whole teaching. Excel — and know what the excelling is for. Attainment in the sciences and arts is commanded; attainment as a form of status is refused in the same voice. The intellectual excellence is instrumental to a moral one. That is already a first, quiet application of moderation: not a reduction of the standard, but a refusal to let one kind of achievement become the whole of a person’s aim.

Work is worship: what the law actually legislates

The reason excellence is not merely good advice in this Faith is that Bahá’u’lláh moved work out of the category of economic necessity and into the category of worship — by legislation, in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas.

“O people of Bahá! It is incumbent upon each one of you to engage in some occupation—such as a craft, a trade or the like. We have exalted your engagement in such work to the rank of worship of the one true God… Waste not your hours in idleness and sloth, but occupy yourselves with what will profit you and others… The most despised of men in the sight of God are they who sit and beg.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶33

The verse legislates in both directions at once. Occupation is obligatory, and it is elevated; idleness and mendicancy are not merely unwise but named among the things God most despises. The parallel text in the Twelfth Glad-Tidings of the Bishárát removes any doubt that the elevation is meant literally rather than as encouragement: “When anyone occupieth himself in a craft or trade, such occupation itself is regarded in the estimation of God as an act of worship; and this is naught but a token of His infinite and all-pervasive bounty.”

The notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas preserve the Guardian’s exposition of what the law does and does not mean, in a letter written on his behalf. It is the passage that supplies the criterion:

“Every individual, no matter how handicapped and limited he may be, is under the obligation of engaging in some work or profession, for work, especially when performed in the spirit of service, is according to Bahá’u’lláh a form of worship. It has not only a utilitarian purpose, but has a value in itself, because it draws us nearer to God, and enables us to better grasp His purpose for us in this world.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, quoted in the notes to Kitáb-i-Aqdas, note 56

A value in itself is the load-bearing phrase. Work is not worship because of what it produces or what it funds; the labour has standing before God independently of its output. That is what makes the excellence commands intelligible rather than merely demanding — if the making is itself worship, how well it is made is a religious question and not only a professional one.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave the same doctrine its most concrete illustration in London on 26 December 1912, answering a question about whether prayer should take the form of action:

“In the Bahá’í Cause arts, sciences and all crafts are (counted as) worship. The man who makes a piece of notepaper to the best of his ability, conscientiously, concentrating all his forces on perfecting it, is giving praise to God. Briefly, all effort and exertion put forth by man from the fullness of his heart is worship, if it is prompted by the highest motives and the will to do service to humanity.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, no. 55

Notepaper. Not medicine, not architecture, not teaching — the most disposable object ‘Abdu’l-Bahá could have named. The choice of example does the work of a whole argument: the elevation attaches to the manner of making, not to the dignity of the product. And the conditions are stated plainly — to the best of his ability, conscientiously, concentrating all his forces — with the motive clause that governs the whole: if it is prompted by the highest motives and the will to do service to humanity.

One caution about a much-loved sentence. The formulation most often quoted in Bahá’í gatherings — “Work done in the spirit of service is the highest form of worship” — is not from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablets. It circulates from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on Divine Philosophy, an unauthenticated 1918 compilation of Western pilgrim-talks; it is a popular gloss, not authoritative text. The doctrine survives the correction intact, because it rests on the Aqdas, the Bishárát, and Shoghi Effendi’s own summary in God Passes By of “the exaltation of work, performed in the spirit of service, to the rank of worship.” But the habit of quoting the gloss in preference to the legislation is worth breaking, and the general problem is treated in authenticated-word.

The House of Justice has kept the teaching operational rather than ornamental, most pointedly in its letters to the Bahá’ís of Iran. Writing on 2 April 2010 to a community systematically barred from universities and steady employment, it attributed the historic prosperity of the Iranian Bahá’ís to “a culture that lays great emphasis on education and learning and which recognizes as an act of worship the assiduous and honest pursuit of a useful trade or profession, undertaken in the spirit of service.” And on 2 March 2013, listing the convictions that underpin the oneness of humankind and that Bahá’í communities everywhere are trying to embody, it named among them “that work performed in the spirit of service to one’s fellow human beings is a form of prayer, a means of worshipping God.” A doctrine invoked as the explanation of a persecuted community’s economic resilience is not being used decoratively.

The institutional form of the same idea is the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár, where worship and service are architecturally inseparable. Shoghi Effendi’s statement of the relation is severe in both directions: Bahá’í worship “divorced from the social, humanitarian, educational, and scientific pursuits centring around the Dependencies… can never hope to achieve beyond the meagre and often transitory results produced by the contemplations of the ascetic”; and equally, the exertions of those administering its affairs, “no matter how disinterested and strenuous,” will not “fructify and prosper unless they are brought into close and daily communion” with the spiritual life at its centre. Note the word strenuous. Effort alone is explicitly denied the power to make service fruitful. That is the first appearance of the argument this article is making: quantity of exertion is not the variable the Writings track.

The rhythm this produces was described by the House of Justice on 1 August 2014, recalling the first Mashriqu’l-Adhkár at ‘Ishqábád, where the friends had built a meeting hall, schools, a hostel and a clinic around the temple. What they achieved, the letter says, was “a place where souls gathered at daybreak for humble invocation and communion before flowing out of its doors to engage in their daily pursuits.” Worship at daybreak, then the day’s work; the same motion, not two competing claims on a life. That is what work-as-worship looks like when it is working, and it is developed further in mashriqul-adhkar-unique-role and in the question page on work-as-worship.

Moderation is a structural word, not a temperamental one

Read casually, moderation sounds like a recommendation of mildness — do less, feel less strongly, stay in the middle. The Bahá’í texts use it in a different and much harder sense, and the Universal House of Justice has said so explicitly.

In a letter of 29 November 2017 answering three believers who were worried that some Bahá’ís had adopted extreme positions in the climate-change debate, the Department of the Secretariat set out what Bahá’u’lláh’s counsel of moderation does and does not mean:

“By moderation, Bahá’u’lláh is in no way referring to mere compromise, the dilution of truth, or a hypocritical or utopian consensus. The moderation He calls for demands an end to destructive excesses that have plagued humanity and fomented ceaseless contention and turmoil. Moderation in deliberation and action stands in contrast to the arbitrary imposition of views through power or insistence upon ideological aims, both of which obstruct the search for truth and sow the seeds of continuing injustice.” — Department of the Secretariat, on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 29 November 2017 (uhj-2017)

Not mere compromise. Not the dilution of truth. A moderate person, on this account, is not one who holds weaker views; he is one who declines to press a single aim through by force at the expense of everything else in the field. The letter grounds the reading in the passage of Gleanings that supplies the whole teaching’s foundation:

“Whoso cleaveth to justice, can, under no circumstances, transgress the limits of moderation. He discerneth the truth in all things, through the guidance of Him Who is the All-Seeing.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CLXIV

Moderation is derived here from justice, not from prudence, temperament or timidity. And justice, in the Hidden Words, is defined as an instrument of perception — “By its aid thou shalt see with thine own eyes and not through the eyes of others” (Arabic 2). Put the two together and moderation turns out to be an epistemic condition: a person who is weighing all the claims fairly cannot, by construction, run past the limits of any one of them. Excess is what happens when one claim stops being weighed against the others. It is a failure of seeing before it is a failure of restraint.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the structural reading unmistakable in Some Answered Questions, where He applies the word not to a temperament but to legislation. Discussing strikes, and having rejected both “the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners” and the workers’ “gratuitous demands,” He writes:

“It is preferable, then, that some measure of moderation be achieved, and by moderation is meant the enactment of such laws and regulations as would prevent the unwarranted concentration of wealth in the hands of the few and satisfy the essential needs of the many.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 78

By moderation is meant the enactment of such laws. There is no gentleness in that sentence and no counsel of quietness. Moderation is the name of a distribution — a state of affairs in which no legitimate claim has been extinguished by another — and the means of reaching it are statutory. The economic dimension of this is developed in wealth-and-poverty and in stewardship-of-means.

Bahá’u’lláh Himself states the general rule twice, in two of the Tablets revealed after the Aqdas, and in both cases the context is governance rather than personal habit. In the Ninth Leaf of the Kalimát-i-Firdawsíyyih:

“In all matters moderation is desirable. If a thing is carried to excess, it will prove a source of evil.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Words of Paradise, Ninth Leaf

And in the Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd, addressed to the conduct of rulers:

“It is incumbent upon them who are in authority to exercise moderation in all things. Whatsoever passeth beyond the limits of moderation will cease to exert a beneficial influence. Consider for instance such things as liberty, civilization and the like. However much men of understanding may favorably regard them, they will, if carried to excess, exercise a pernicious influence upon men.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablet of Maqṣúd

The examples chosen are decisive for the argument. Bahá’u’lláh does not illustrate excess with vices. He illustrates it with liberty and civilisation — two of the goods most prized by the age He was addressing, and two that the Faith itself upholds. Excess is therefore not the amplification of something bad. It is a good pursued past the point where it remains answerable to the other goods around it. That is the definition this whole article turns on, and it is why “am I doing too much?” is nearly always the wrong question and “what am I no longer able to see?” is nearly always the right one.

Civilisation carried to excess

The most quoted moderation passage in the Bahá’í corpus is also the one whose implications the community has been slowest to absorb, because it applies the principle to progress itself.

“The civilization, so often vaunted by the learned exponents of arts and sciences, will, if allowed to overleap the bounds of moderation, bring great evil upon men… If carried to excess, civilization will prove as prolific a source of evil as it had been of goodness when kept within the restraints of moderation. Meditate on this, O people, and be not of them that wander distraught in the wilderness of error. The day is approaching when its flame will devour the cities.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CLXIV

The claim is symmetrical and it is severe. Civilisation is not neutral and not merely capable of misuse; “kept within the restraints of moderation” it is a source of goodness, and past those restraints it becomes a source of evil in the same measure. The sentence that follows in the same section generalises without exception: “All other things are subject to this same principle of moderation.”

Bahá’u’lláh had already given the concrete case in the Ninth Leaf of the Words of Paradise. Immediately after “if a thing is carried to excess, it will prove a source of evil,” He continues: “Consider the civilization of the West, how it hath agitated and alarmed the peoples of the world. An infernal engine hath been devised, and hath proved so cruel a weapon of destruction that its like none hath ever witnessed or heard.” The remedy He names is not less technical capacity but a common aim — “unless the peoples of the world unite in pursuit of one common aim and embrace one universal faith.” Shoghi Effendi read the passage as a prophecy about the twentieth century and quoted it at length in The Unfoldment of World Civilization (1936), immediately after his own analysis of the League of Nations’ failure. He was not treating it as a counsel of personal temperance, but as a diagnosis of what industrial modernity becomes when its technical achievements outrun every restraining principle.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá supplies the anatomy of the same failure at the level of a person, in The Secret of Divine Civilization, and this is the passage that connects the civilisational reading back to an individual life:

“How often has it happened that an individual who was graced with every attribute of humanity and wore the jewel of true understanding, nevertheless followed after his passions until his excellent qualities passed beyond moderation and he was forced into excess… A good character is… the most excellent and praiseworthy of all things, but always on condition that its center of emanation should be reason and knowledge and its base should be true moderation.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Secret of Divine Civilization, pp. 59–60

Excellent qualities passed beyond moderation. The subject of the sentence is a genuinely good person with genuinely good attributes; nothing has been added to him and nothing corrupt has entered. What has happened is that a quality has slipped its restraint. Two paragraphs earlier ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names the faculty that prevents this — the capacity to oppose one’s passions, which He calls “the balance wheel of all behavior, the means of keeping all man’s good qualities in equilibrium.” A balance wheel does not slow a mechanism down; it regulates its rate so the mechanism keeps time. That is exactly the function moderation performs in relation to excellence, and it is a better image than any metaphor of brakes.

The contemporary application is the House of Justice’s 1 March 2017 letter to the Bahá’ís of the world on the economic life of humanity, which reads the whole materialist settlement as a moderation failure:

“The forces of materialism promote a quite contrary line of thinking: that happiness comes from constant acquisition, that the more one has the better, that worry for the environment is for another day. These seductive messages fuel an increasingly entrenched sense of personal entitlement, which uses the language of justice and rights to disguise self-interest.” — The Universal House of Justice, 1 March 2017 (uhj-2017)

The same letter, having quoted ‘Abdu’l-Bahá that wealth is “praiseworthy in the highest degree, if it is acquired by an individual’s own efforts and the grace of God, in commerce, agriculture, art and industry, and if it be expended for philanthropic purposes,” names the discipline that keeps acquisition inside its bounds: “Examining one’s life to determine what is a necessity and then discharging with joy one’s obligation in relation to the law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh is an indispensable discipline to bring one’s priorities into balance.” That is moderation as a practice with a computation attached, not as a mood — see huqququllah-not-a-tax.

How the two commands govern each other

Now the central question can be answered rather than surveyed.

The pair works because excellence and moderation take different objects. Excellence takes the work: the notepaper, the diagnosis, the code, the lesson plan, the meal, the report. Moderation takes the set of claims on a life: the trade, the family, the health, the devotional obligations, the community’s activities, the sleep. Nothing in the corpus asks a believer to do a piece of work to less than the best standard he can reach. A great deal in the corpus asks him not to let one claim swallow the rest.

The clearest statement of the mechanism comes from a letter the Universal House of Justice addressed on 3 January 1979 to the participants in a Bahá’í Studies seminar held in Cambridge, on the spiritual qualities of Bahá’í scholars. Its diagnosis is general enough to govern any life:

“The sundering of science and religion is but one example of the tendency of the human mind (which is necessarily limited in its capacity) to concentrate on one virtue, one aspect of truth, one goal, to the exclusion of others. This leads, in extreme cases, to fanaticism and the utter distortion of truth, and in all cases to some degree of imbalance and inaccuracy.” — The Universal House of Justice, 3 January 1979 (muhj-1963-1986)

Three things in that sentence repay attention. First, the cause is limited capacity, not bad will — which means the failure is ordinary and expected rather than shameful. Second, the object of the over-concentration may be a virtue; the material is good, which is why the failure is so hard to see from inside. Third, the consequence at low intensity is not scandal but “imbalance and inaccuracy” — the over-concentrated person gets things wrong, quietly, before anything dramatic happens. The letter goes on to remind Bahá’í scholars that “being a scholar does not exempt him from the primal duties and purposes for which all human beings are created”: the excellence of the specialism does not buy an exemption from the rest of the obligations.

Read against this, the phrase “in all matters moderation is desirable” stops being a counsel of mildness. It is a rule about portfolios. A believer who has stopped sleeping in order to serve, or stopped serving in order to advance, or stopped seeing his children in order to build something worthy, has not violated a quota. He has concentrated on one good to the exclusion of others, which is the exact condition the letter describes — and the first symptom, before exhaustion arrives, will be that his judgement has quietly degraded.

The second half of the mechanism runs in the other direction: moderation cannot be used to lower the standard, because the standard has no ceiling to lower it from. This is where the community’s own pedagogy is unexpectedly precise. Learning about Excellence, the fifth book of the junior-youth programme’s curriculum, written for twelve-and thirteen-year-olds and published in a pre-publication edition in 2014, puts it this way: “’Acquiring perfections’ is another term that we can use to talk about ‘striving for excellence’. There are infinite degrees of perfection and we can never claim that we have acquired them all.” A standard with infinite degrees cannot be exhausted, and therefore cannot be discharged by any finite burst of effort. It can only be served by a rate you can sustain — which is precisely why moderation is required by the excellence command rather than being a concession against it.

The same book makes a second move that is worth borrowing at any age. Excellence, it argues, is achieved “through cooperation” rather than competition, because “when we compete, someone is bound to lose; when we cooperate, everyone wins.” Cooperative excellence has a different economics from competitive excellence: it does not require you to outrun a rival, and therefore does not import the rival’s pace as your own constraint. Most burnout in service is imported pace. Someone else’s rate becomes your standard, and the standard the Writings actually set — the best you can conscientiously do on this piece of work — gets replaced by a comparison, which has no natural stopping point.

Bahá’u’lláh’s own formulation of progress carries the same rate-limited shape. In the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat: “Let each morn be better than its eve and each morrow richer than its yesterday. Man’s merit lieth in service and virtue and not in the pageantry of wealth and riches.” The unit of improvement is one day against the day before it — the smallest possible increment, compared against the nearest possible baseline. It is not a comparison against a peer, an ideal, or a former self at their best. A person who took that sentence literally would be incapable of the kind of gap-to-the-ideal despair that drives most over-extension.

Two ditches, not one

A reader who has absorbed only the moderation passages will misjudge which failure they are actually at risk of, because the Writings name the excesses on both sides of the road, and are harsher about the ascetic one than most Bahá’ís expect.

Bahá’u’lláh prohibits monasticism and self-imposed austerity in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas with a sentence that is difficult to soften:

“How many a man hath secluded himself in the climes of India, denied himself the things that God hath decreed as lawful, imposed upon himself austerities and mortifications, and hath not been remembered by God, the Revealer of Verses. Make not your deeds as snares wherewith to entrap the object of your aspiration.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶36

Make not your deeds as snares. The image is of a person using his own effort as a trap set for God’s favour — an instrumental relation to one’s own austerity — and the verse is unambiguous that the man in question “hath not been remembered by God.” Effort spent on self-denial has bought exactly nothing. The Tenth Leaf of the Words of Paradise states the same law positively: “Living in seclusion or practicing asceticism is not acceptable in the presence of God. It behooveth them that are endued with insight and understanding to observe that which will cause joy and radiance… Deprive not yourselves of the bounties which have been created for your sake.”

Shoghi Effendi, in a passage of The Advent of Divine Justice that sits immediately after his most demanding statement of the chaste and holy life — the one requiring “daily vigilance in the control of one’s carnal desires” — takes trouble to close off the ascetic misreading:

“It must be remembered, however, that the maintenance of such a high standard of moral conduct is not to be associated or confused with any form of asceticism, or of excessive and bigoted puritanism. The standard inculcated by Bahá’u’lláh seeks, under no circumstances, to deny anyone the legitimate right and privilege to derive the fullest advantage and benefit from the manifold joys, beauties, and pleasures with which the world has been so plentifully enriched by an All-Loving Creator.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice

He then quotes Bahá’u’lláh’s own reassurance: “Should a man wish to adorn himself with the ornaments of the earth, to wear its apparels, or partake of the benefits it can bestow, no harm can befall him, if he alloweth nothing whatever to intervene between him and God.”

The same double-sidedness appears inside religious practice itself. Bahá’u’lláh commands daily recitation of the verses and then, in the same paragraph, caps it: “Pride not yourselves on much reading of the verses or on a multitude of pious acts by night and day… Read ye the sacred verses in such measure that ye be not overcome by languor and despondency. Lay not upon your souls that which will weary them and weigh them down, but rather what will lighten and uplift them” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶149). The named failure mode is weariness from excess, and it is named in a verse about worship. If the Lawgiver puts a ceiling on scripture reading, the notion that service is the one activity exempt from the principle cannot be sustained.

It appears even in religious education. In the Eighth Leaf of the Words of Paradise, Bahá’u’lláh instructs that schools should first train children in the principles of religion — and immediately qualifies it: “but this in such a measure that it may not injure the children by resulting in ignorant fanaticism and bigotry.” A good pursued past its measure injures; the good here is religious instruction, and the injury is named without euphemism.

And then the other ditch, which the same corpus refuses to let anyone forget. “Waste not your hours in idleness and sloth” (¶33). “The most despised of men in the sight of God are they who sit and beg” (¶33). “Guard against idleness and sloth, and cling unto that which profiteth mankind” (Words of Paradise). A believer who reads the moderation passages as licence to coast has misread them precisely as badly as the one who reads the excellence passages as licence to burn out.

The practical upshot: moderation is a two-sided constraint, and you must know which side you are failing on. The two do not feel different from the inside — both produce the sense of falling short — but the remedies are opposite. This is one reason the Writings put decisions of this kind inside relationships and consultation rather than leaving them to private self-assessment; see consultation and readiness-and-maturity.

Zeal, health, and the horse that carries you

The Bahá’í corpus does not use the word burnout, which entered occupational psychology in the 1970s. But the Guardian’s correspondence deals with the phenomenon repeatedly and without any romanticising of exhaustion, and the letters are unusually direct.

Writing on his behalf on 23 November 1947 to a believer who had recovered from illness and gone straight back to heavy work for the Faith:

“You should not neglect your health, but consider it the means which enables you to serve. It—the body—is like a horse which carries the personality and spirit, and as such should be well cared for so it can do its work! You should certainly safeguard your nerves, and force yourself to take time, and not only for prayer and meditation, but for real rest and relaxation. We don’t have to pray and meditate for hours in order to be spiritual.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 23 November 1947 (prayer-devotional-life)

Every clause of that passage is an instruction against a specific temptation. Consider it the means which enables you to serve — the argument for health is made in the currency of service, so that a believer who does not care about himself is given a reason he cannot dismiss as self-indulgence. Force yourself to take time — rest is placed in the imperative, as an act of will rather than a lapse of it. Not only for prayer and meditation, but for real rest and relaxation — devotion is explicitly named as insufficient substitute for rest, closing the loophole by which a tired believer prays instead of sleeping.

Two years earlier, on 26 March 1945, the Guardian’s secretary wrote to a believer in the middle of a mental-health difficulty and went further, telling him to stop:

“There is no object in over-taxing your will power and strength by forcing yourself to do things for the Cause. You should let your mind rest in the thought of the infinite love, mercy and forgiveness of Bahá’u’lláh, and cease to fret about whether you are or are not doing your share until you fully recover your health.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 26 March 1945

Cease to fret about whether you are or are not doing your share. That is guidance addressed to the precise anxiety that keeps over-extended believers over-extended, and it comes from the Guardian’s own office. Note also what it does not say: it does not recommend serving at reduced quality, and it does not moralise about the illness. It suspends the obligation, temporarily and completely, and names the recovery of health as the current work.

The pastoral shape of the resulting life was drawn in a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice on 8 May 1979, glossing Shoghi Effendi’s call to abandon frivolous conduct:

“Abandonment of ‘a frivolous conduct’ does not imply that a Bahá’í must be sour-faced or perpetually solemn. Humour, happiness, joy are characteristics of a true Bahá’í life. Frivolity palls and eventually leads to boredom and emptiness, but true happiness and joy and humour that are parts of a balanced life that includes serious thought, compassion and humble servitude to God, are characteristics that enrich life and add to its radiance.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 8 May 1979 (chaste-holy-life)

The same letter recalls ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s caution “that we should not let a pastime become a waste of time” — which is the moderation principle applied to leisure, and the reason this section is not an argument for less effort in general. The Writings do not trade service against rest as though the two were rivals for a fixed budget. They describe a life in which both are present, each in its measure, and in which the disappearance of either signals that something has gone wrong.

One point about pace the community’s own framework already encodes. Bahá’í collective work is organised in three-month cycles of expansion, consolidation and reflection, so that intensity alternates with consolidation and learning rather than running flat out (three-month-cycle-tempo). An individual serving at a constant maximum is out of step with the rhythm the institutions have established, and usually on private initiative — see initiative-and-plan.

The field a reader is actually deciding within, in 2026

The corpus supplies the principles. It cannot describe the conditions under which a believer today decides how hard to push, and here external evidence is worth weighing — strictly as description of the field, never as authority for a Bahá’í principle.

Exhaustion is now a classified occupational phenomenon. In May 2019 the World Health Organization included burn-out in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases, defining it as “a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed,” with three dimensions: energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental distance from one’s job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism related to it; and reduced professional efficacy. WHO was careful that it is not classified as a medical condition — it sits in the chapter on factors influencing health status — and that it “refers specifically to phenomena in the occupational context.” The third dimension is the one that matters most for this article’s argument: burnout is defined partly by reduced efficacy. Whatever else over-extension does, it degrades the quality of the work — which is the excellence command’s own concern, not merely the moderation command’s.

The baseline is not a healthy one. Gallup’s State of the Global Workplace: 2026 Report, drawing on data collected through 2025, found 20% of employees worldwide engaged at work, with 64% not engaged and 16% actively disengaged; 40% reported experiencing stress a lot of the previous day; and 34% were classified as thriving in overall wellbeing, against 56% struggling and 9% suffering. Manager engagement stood at 22%. These are self-report survey figures with all the limits that implies, but the direction is not ambiguous.

More hours stop producing more output at a fairly low threshold. John Pencavel’s study of British munitions workers, published in The Economic Journal in 2015, found that output was roughly proportional to hours up to about 48 hours a week, after which output per hour fell; beyond roughly 55 hours the additional hours contributed very little, such that a 70-hour week produced approximately what a 55-hour week did. The data are a century old and describe manual, measurable factory work, so the exact thresholds do not transfer to knowledge work — but the shape of the curve, a plateau followed by decline, has proved robust enough to have entered mainstream management practice.

Cutting hours does not have to cut output. The largest controlled trial of a four-day week to date was published in Nature Human Behaviour in July 2025 by Wen Fan and Juliet Schor of Boston College and colleagues: 2,896 employees across 141 organisations in Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States, over a six-month trial of reduced hours at unchanged pay, with 12 control companies. Work-related burnout fell from 2.83 to 2.38 on a 1–5 scale; job satisfaction rose 0.52 on a 0–10 scale; self-reported mental health improved 0.39 and physical health 0.28 on 1–5 scales; the control companies showed no such pattern. The study relies on self-report and on self-selected participating firms, and companies typically spent weeks restructuring their workflows before beginning — which is itself the finding worth carrying: the gains came with redesign, not merely with subtraction.

Effort explains less of excellence than the folklore claims. The popular account of mastery — that roughly ten thousand hours of deliberate practice makes an expert — was substantially qualified by Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald’s meta-analysis in Psychological Science in 2014, which found deliberate practice accounted for about 26% of the variance in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and less than 1% in the professions; for studies using practice logs rather than retrospective recall, the overall figure was around 5%. The finding is contested and has been re-analysed by others, and it does not say that practice is unimportant. It says that hours are a much weaker lever on excellence than the ambient culture assumes — which is an empirical argument for the position this article takes on principled grounds: adding hours is rarely the efficient route to doing the work well.

Religious service is not exempt. Barna’s tracking of American Protestant senior pastors found the proportion who had seriously considered leaving full-time ministry in the previous year rising from 29% in January 2021 to a peak of 42% in March 2022, and falling to 24% by early 2026. The Bahá’í community has no clergy and these figures describe a different institutional situation entirely — but the underlying pattern, of unbounded and unbudgeted service obligations falling on a small number of committed people, is one every community with a handful of over-committed friends will recognise.

None of this grounds a Bahá’í principle, and none of it should be cited as though it did. What it establishes is only that the conditions the Writings legislated for are, in the present state of the field, conditions of chronic over-extension in which the ascetic ditch is a good deal easier to fall into than the idle one.

Deciding how hard to push: a defensible rule

The recommendation follows the structure of the pair rather than the temperament of the reader.

Never lower the standard inside the work. “In every art and skill, God loveth the highest perfection.” This is not negotiable and no moderation passage negotiates it. When you are over-committed, the thing to reduce is the number of things, not the quality of any of them. Half-doing four commitments is not moderation; it is a fifth kind of excess, and it also violates ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s caution against being “like those who leave matters unfinished.”

Diagnose by absence, not by hours. The question the corpus actually equips you to answer is not “am I working too much?” but “which legitimate claim on me has gone silent?” If health, sleep, family, the daily obligatory prayer, the reading of the Word, or your trade has quietly dropped out, the condition is excess by the definition of the 3 January 1979 letter — one good pursued to the exclusion of others — regardless of the hour count. If nothing has dropped out, a demanding season is not excess.

Treat degraded judgement as the early warning, not exhaustion. The letter names “imbalance and inaccuracy” as the ordinary consequence, with fanaticism as the extreme case. WHO’s own definition puts reduced efficacy among the three dimensions of burnout. In practice this means the first reliable signal is not that you feel tired; it is that you are making decisions your rested self would not endorse — pressing a view too hard in consultation, missing what the other person actually said, letting a small task expand because you cannot see its edges any more.

Rest in the imperative. “Force yourself to take time, and not only for prayer and meditation, but for real rest and relaxation.” The Guardian’s secretary put rest in the same grammatical mood as service because for a certain temperament it requires the same act of will. If you find yourself praying instead of sleeping, that letter is addressed to you.

Take the exemptions when they are given. The 26 March 1945 letter tells a believer in poor health to “cease to fret about whether you are or are not doing your share until you fully recover.” The Kitáb-i-Aqdas itself exempts the ill and the aged from fasting and obligatory prayer. Exemption in this Faith is instruction, not concession, and a believer who serves through it is not being more devout — see health-and-medicine and daily-practices.

Let the excellence be cooperative rather than comparative. Someone else’s pace is not the standard; the standard is the best this piece of work can be done, by you, conscientiously. Competitive framing imports a rate with no stopping condition. The junior-youth curriculum teaches thirteen-year-olds this before they enter a labour market designed to unteach it, and it holds at forty as well as at thirteen.

Improve against yesterday. “Let each morn be better than its eve and each morrow richer than its yesterday.” The nearest baseline is the only one Bahá’u’lláh names, and it makes the gap between where you are and the ideal irrelevant to today’s decision. That is a genuinely usable rule for people whose over-extension is driven by a felt distance from the standard rather than by external demand.

Anchor the moderation in an obligation, not in a feeling. The 1 March 2017 letter names Ḥuqúqu’lláh computation as “an indispensable discipline to bring one’s priorities into balance” precisely because it converts a vague sense of “enough” into an examination of one’s life to determine what is a necessity. Moderation that has no procedure attached tends to be revised downward whenever one is busy.

And for a technically skilled reader — the highest-leverage contribution is unusual. The whole apparatus of contemporary productivity software optimises for the variable the Writings do not track: throughput, streaks, hours logged, tasks closed. Almost nothing in the field measures the variable they do track — whether a legitimate claim on a person has gone silent. A scheduling or planning tool for community life whose primary alert is “this person has taken on a fourth role” or “nobody has replaced the animator who moved” would be doing something the market has not built, because the metric has no commercial constituency. The same holds inside institutions: rota and cluster-planning tools that make coverage and succession visible, rather than activity counts, would let Assemblies and agencies see over-concentration before the over-concentrated person collapses. If you build such a thing, the design discipline is the hard part: a tool that surfaces absence must resist becoming a tool that surfaces performance, or it will manufacture exactly the comparative pressure it was meant to relieve. For non-technical readers the same leverage exists in a much simpler form — notice who is doing four things, and take one of them, which is a form of service that never appears in any report.

The measure of the whole is the sentence in which Bahá’u’lláh caps His own command to read scripture: “Lay not upon your souls that which will weary them and weigh them down, but rather what will lighten and uplift them.” A pattern of work and service that is producing weariness and heaviness is, by the Lawgiver’s own criterion, being done wrong — and the correction is almost never to do the individual pieces less well. It is to carry fewer of them, better, for longer.

Open questions / honest limits

  • The corpus gives no threshold, and this page has not invented one. There is no scriptural equivalent of a maximum working week, no ratio of service to rest, no number of community roles that is too many. The texts supply a criterion — one good not pursued to the exclusion of others — and leave the application to the individual, the family, and consultation. Any page offering a number would be exceeding its sources.
  • “Moderation” translates a term whose range this synthesis has not investigated philologically. The English word carries connotations of mildness that the House of Justice’s 29 November 2017 letter explicitly disclaims. A study of the underlying Arabic and Persian vocabulary across the Tablets would sharpen the argument and has not been attempted here.
  • The most-quoted sentence on this topic is not authoritative text. “Work done in the spirit of service is the highest form of worship” comes from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on Divine Philosophy (1918), an unauthenticated compilation of Western pilgrim-talks. The doctrine is securely grounded elsewhere; the sentence is not scripture, and this page has not relied on it.
  • The excellence passages addressed to children are cited from compilations of previously unpublished Tablets. Excellence in All Things (Research Department, November 1981, revised November 1990) gives several of the Bahá’u’lláh passages without a named Tablet, marking them as previously unpublished. They are authentic and authorised for use; a reader wanting the original context will not find it in the published Tablets.
  • How far the moderation principle constrains the community’s collective pace is not settled here. The Writings apply moderation to individuals, to those in authority, and to civilisation. Whether and how it bears on the intensity of a programme of growth is a question for the institutions rather than for a synthesis, and the relevant guidance is framed in terms of organic development and learning rather than restraint — see cluster-development-framework.
  • This page does not treat the ethics of particular occupations. Whether a given trade may be pursued at all, and the conduct required within it, belong to bahai-conduct-in-workplace and bahai-owned-business-conduct.
  • The external research will age and is contested. The Gallup figures are self-reported and their engagement construct is proprietary; Pencavel’s data are from First World War munitions plants; the four-day-week trial used self-selected firms and self-report instruments; the deliberate-practice meta-analysis has been re-analysed by researchers who argue its effect sizes are underestimated; the clergy figures describe an institutional situation with no Bahá’í counterpart. None of it grounds a principle of the Faith, and none should be cited as if it did.
