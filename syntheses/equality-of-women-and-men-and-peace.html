The Emancipation of Women and the Peace of the World: The Fifth Issue in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

In October 1985 the Universal House of Justice addressed a statement “To the Peoples of the World.” Part II of The Promise of World Peace reviews the declarations and conventions adopted by the United Nations and then singles out a few issues that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven: racism; the disparity between rich and poor; unbridled nationalism; religious strife; the emancipation of women; universal education; and an international auxiliary language. The fifth paragraph has four sentences:

“The emancipation of women, the achievement of full equality between the sexes, is one of the most important, though less acknowledged prerequisites of peace. The denial of such equality perpetrates an injustice against one half of the world’s population and promotes in men harmful attitudes and habits that are carried from the family to the workplace, to political life, and ultimately to international relations. There are no grounds, moral, practical, or biological, upon which such denial can be justified. Only as women are welcomed into full partnership in all fields of human endeavor will the moral and psychological climate be created in which international peace can emerge.” — The Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, October 1985, Part II

This page reads that paragraph closely, places it in the argument of the whole statement, and follows one question outward through the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, the talks and Tablets of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, the writings of Shoghi Effendi and the messages of the Universal House of Justice from 1985 to 2021: why is the equality of women and men a condition of peace among nations? It ends with a section prepared for a songwriter.

The knowledge base already has a long concept page, Equality of Women and Men, which covers the principle as a whole: the equality of souls, the two wings, equality within marriage, the provision that confines membership of the Universal House of Justice to men, the work of the Bahá’í International Community, and the comparison with secular feminism. This page does not repeat that material. Its subject is narrower. It asks what the 1985 statement claims about the link between equality and peace, how the earlier Writings ground that link, and how the House of Justice has developed it since.

The paragraph, sentence by sentence

“The emancipation of women, the achievement of full equality between the sexes.” The subject is given two names. Emancipation means release from a condition of subjection. It looks back at what women have been held in. The achievement of full equality looks forward to a state not yet reached. The word full matters: the statement is not satisfied with equality in law, in the vote, or in education alone.

“One of the most important, though less acknowledged prerequisites of peace.” This sentence makes two claims. The first is a ranking: among the conditions of peace, this is one of the most important. The second is an observation about the world in 1985: few people who worked for peace counted the status of women among its conditions. The word prerequisite is strong. A prerequisite is not something that peace will bring once it arrives. It must be in place first.

“An injustice against one half of the world’s population.” The second sentence names two harms. The first falls on women and is stated briefly: it is an injustice, and it touches half of humanity.

“And promotes in men harmful attitudes and habits.” The second harm falls on men. The denial of equality does not only take something from women; it forms something in men. The statement names attitudes (how men regard others) and habits (how they act without thinking).

“Carried from the family to the workplace, to political life, and ultimately to international relations.” This is the centre of the paragraph and its most original sentence. It describes a path with four stations. Habits are learned at home. They travel with a man to his work, then into the politics of his country, and finally into the dealings of nations with one another. If a boy learns in the home that the stronger party imposes its will, he carries that lesson into every larger setting he enters. War between nations is, on this account, partly the same habit at its largest scale.

“There are no grounds, moral, practical, or biological.” The third sentence rules out the three kinds of justification that have historically been offered for inequality. Moral grounds are claims that inequality is right or ordained. Practical grounds are claims that it is necessary or efficient. Biological grounds are claims that it follows from nature. The statement does not weigh them one by one. It says none of them holds.

“Only as women are welcomed into full partnership in all fields of human endeavor.” The fourth sentence gives the condition for peace, and three of its words carry the meaning. Welcomed places the action on those who already hold the fields of endeavour, who have mostly been men: women are to be received gladly, not admitted grudgingly. Partnership describes a relation between equals working together, not a transfer of power from one side to the other. All fields leaves no area exempt.

“Will the moral and psychological climate be created in which international peace can emerge.” Peace here is something that emerges when conditions allow it. It is not signed into being. The conditions are named as a moral and psychological climate, which is a matter of what people value and how they think and feel. Treaties cannot produce that. The statement says that a world which excludes women from partnership will lack it.

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The paralysis of will

Part II opens by saying that bans on particular weapons “will not remove the root causes of war,” and that the obstacle to peace is not ignorance of what needs to be done: “There is, however, a paralysis of will.” The statement traces this paralysis to two sources. It is “rooted, as we have stated, in a deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind,” and it is “also traceable to the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order in which they can live in peace, harmony and prosperity with all humanity.”

The women’s paragraph bears on both. The statement does not draw these connections in so many words; they are a reading of how its parts fit together.

  • The conviction of quarrelsomeness. The introduction to the statement rejects the view that aggression is part of human nature: “Dispassionately examined, the evidence reveals that such conduct, far from expressing man’s true self, represents a distortion of the human spirit.” The women’s paragraph names one place where that distortion is produced: the home in which men learn habits of domination. If the denial of equality forms aggressive habits in men, those habits are then observed and taken as proof that human beings are aggressive by nature. Ending the denial removes one of the sources of the evidence on which the paralysing conviction rests.
  • The subjugated masses. Women are a large share of those who have been kept without education and without a voice. A world in which half the population cannot speak in public affairs is a world in which much of the desire for peace goes unheard.

The conventions, and the “additional comment”

The seven issues follow a list of United Nations instruments, including “the similar measures concerned with eliminating all forms of discrimination based on race, sex or religious belief.” The House of Justice says these measures, “if courageously enforced and expanded,” will hasten the day when war loses its power over international relations. The seven paragraphs then add comment on issues already addressed in law. In the case of women, the comment is that the issue is a condition of peace, which the conventions did not say.

Its neighbour: the education of girls

The very next paragraph, on universal education, returns to women. Where resources are limited, it says, “the decision-making agencies involved would do well to consider giving first priority to the education of women and girls, since it is through educated mothers that the benefits of knowledge can be most effectively and rapidly diffused throughout society.” The two paragraphs together set out a remedy in two parts: open every field to women, and educate girls first where every child cannot yet be educated.

The two points that follow the seven

After the seventh issue, the House of Justice adds “two points” that apply to all of them. The first explains the phrase “less acknowledged”:

“One is that the abolition of war is not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols; it is a complex task requiring a new level of commitment to resolving issues not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.” — The Promise of World Peace, Part II

Of the seven, the status of women is the clearest case of an issue “not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.” Racism, poverty, nationalism and religious strife are visible causes of conflict. The habits a boy learns at home are not usually discussed at a peace conference.

The second point explains why a climate matters more than a document: “For, in essence, peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude, and it is chiefly in evoking this attitude that the possibility of enduring solutions can be found.” The women’s paragraph is an application of that sentence. The “moral and psychological climate” in which peace can emerge is the collective form of that inner state.

Parts I, III and IV

The theme appears in each of the other parts.

  • The introduction lists among the favourable signs of the age “the spread of women’s and youth movements calling for an end to war.” Before the statement names equality as a prerequisite of peace, it names women as people already working for peace.
  • Part III states that world order “can be founded only on an unshakable consciousness of the oneness of mankind,” and that recognising this truth “requires abandonment of prejudice—prejudice of every kind—race, class, color, creed, nation, sex, degree of material civilization, everything which enables people to consider themselves superior to others.” Prejudice of sex is listed with the rest. The women’s paragraph is the oneness of mankind applied to the relation between the two halves of the human race. Part III ends with an appeal addressed to both: “Let men and women, youth and children everywhere recognize the eternal merit of this imperative action.”
  • Part IV offers the Bahá’í community “as a model for study.” That offer is tested by whether the community practises what the paragraph demands. The House of Justice returned to that test in 2019 (see below).

Bahá’u’lláh: no distinction before God

Bahá’u’lláh establishes the principle on which the paragraph rests. In a Tablet published in Additional Tablets and Extracts from Tablets Revealed by Bahá’u’lláh, He writes:

“All should know, and in this regard attain the splendours of the sun of certitude, and be illumined thereby: Women and men have been and will always be equal in the sight of God.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablet (additional-tablets-bahaullah)

In another Tablet, translated in the Women compilation and in Bahá’í Sacred Writings, He describes the change His Revelation has made:

“Praised be God, the Pen of the Most High hath lifted distinctions from between His servants and handmaidens, and, through His consummate favours and all-encompassing mercy, hath conferred upon all a station and rank of the same plane.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablet translated from the Persian (women, no. 2)

The 1985 statement’s claim that there are “no grounds” for inequality follows from this. If God has lifted the distinction, no human argument can restore it.

Two further provisions of Bahá’u’lláh bear on the paragraph’s remedy. In the Kitáb‑i‑Aqdas He enjoins on every father “the instruction of his son and daughter in the art of reading and writing” (¶48). The education of daughters is a duty of law, not a favour. And in a passage the House of Justice quoted in 1993, He addresses the treatment of women directly:

“The friends of God must be adorned with the ornament of justice, equity, kindness and love. As they do not allow themselves to be the object of cruelty and transgression, in like manner they should not allow such tyranny to visit the handmaidens of God.” — Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in a letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 24 January 1993 (muhj-1986-2001)

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: equality and the end of war

The 1985 paragraph compresses an argument that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá made many times, in Paris in 1911 and across North America in 1912, often to audiences who had come to hear about peace or about women’s suffrage. He gives three reasons why equality and peace are bound together. They are distinct, and a reader should keep them apart.

First reason: distinction itself breeds strife

In Philadelphia, on 9 June 1912, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said:

“When all mankind shall receive the same opportunity of education and the equality of men and women be realized, the foundations of war will be utterly destroyed. Without equality this will be impossible because all differences and distinction are conducive to discord and strife. Equality between men and women is conducive to the abolition of warfare for the reason that women will never be willing to sanction it.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Unitarian Church, Philadelphia, 9 June 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, p. 175

The middle sentence is a general principle: “all differences and distinction are conducive to discord and strife.” A society that ranks one half of humanity below the other has accepted ranking as the normal order of things, and conflict follows from ranking. This is the same logic by which the Tablets to The Hague list prejudice of every kind as a cause of war. The first Tablet to The Hague, written on 17 December 1919 to the Central Organization for a Durable Peace, sets out the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh that bear on peace, and the equality of women and men is among them:

“And among the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh is the equality of women and men. The world of humanity has two wings—one is women and the other men. Not until both wings are equally developed can the bird fly. Should one wing remain weak, flight is impossible.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, First Tablet to The Hague, 17 December 1919 (tablets-hague-abdul-baha)

In this Tablet, written to a peace organisation, the equality of women and men appears in a list of the conditions of peace, sixty-six years before the House of Justice placed it in a similar list.

Second reason: the mother will not give her son

The reason ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá gave most often is the experience of mothers. At a Woman’s Suffrage Meeting at the Metropolitan Temple in New York, on 20 May 1912, He said:

“The most momentous question of this day is international peace and arbitration, and universal peace is impossible without universal suffrage. Children are educated by the women. The mother bears the troubles and anxieties of rearing the child, undergoes the ordeal of its birth and training. Therefore, it is most difficult for mothers to send to the battlefield those upon whom they have lavished such love and care. Consider a son reared and trained twenty years by a devoted mother. What sleepless nights and restless, anxious days she has spent! Having brought him through dangers and difficulties to the age of maturity, how agonizing then to sacrifice him upon the battlefield! Therefore, the mothers will not sanction war nor be satisfied with it. So it will come to pass that when women participate fully and equally in the affairs of the world, when they enter confidently and capably the great arena of laws and politics, war will cease; for woman will be the obstacle and hindrance to it. This is true and without doubt.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Woman’s Suffrage Meeting, New York, 20 May 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, pp. 134–135

He made the same argument in Pittsburgh on 7 May (“She will refuse to give her sons for sacrifice upon the field of battle. In truth, she will be the greatest factor in establishing universal peace and international arbitration. Assuredly, woman will abolish warfare among mankind,” p. 108); at the Church of the Ascension in New York on 2 June, in answer to a question about democracy and peace (“Women will not be willing to allow those whom they have so tenderly cared for to go to the battlefield. When they shall have a vote, they will oppose any cause of warfare,” p. 167); and in Sacramento on 25 October:

“Consider, for instance, a mother who has tenderly reared a son for twenty years to the age of maturity. Surely she will not consent to having that son torn asunder and killed in the field of battle. Therefore, as woman advances toward the degree of man in power and privilege, with the right of vote and control in human government, most assuredly war will cease; for woman is naturally the most devoted and staunch advocate of international peace.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Hotel Sacramento, 25 October 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, p. 375

The terms of this argument are specific. It does not say that women are morally better than men in every respect. It says that the people who bear and raise children are the people least willing to see them killed, and that while those people have no voice in government, decisions about war are made without them. The remedy He names is political: “the right of vote and control in human government,” entry into “the great arena of laws and politics.”

A year earlier in Paris, on 21 October 1911, grieving over news of the battle at Benghazi in the Italian–Turkish war, He had described war from the side of those who bear its cost at home:

“How many widows mourn their husbands, how many stories of savage cruelty do we hear! How many little orphaned children are crying for their dead fathers, how many women are weeping for their slain sons!” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks, “The Pitiful Causes of War, and the Duty of Everyone to Strive for Peace”

Third reason: a humanity that is incomplete

The third reason is that a human race which excludes half its members from public life is defective, and a defective body cannot reach peace. In the Pittsburgh talk, between the principle of equal education and the mother’s refusal, He said: “In past ages humanity has been defective and inefficient because it has been incomplete. War and its ravages have blighted the world; the education of woman will be a mighty step toward its abolition and ending, for she will use her whole influence against war.” In Montreal, on 5 September 1912, listing the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh, He used the image of the two wings: “If one wing remains incapable and defective, it will restrict the power of the other, and full flight will be impossible.”

The incompleteness harms men too. In Paris, explaining the tenth principle, “Education and Instruction of Women,” He said:

“Women have equal rights with men upon earth; in religion and society they are a very important element. As long as women are prevented from attaining their highest possibilities, so long will men be unable to achieve the greatness which might be theirs.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks

This is the root of the 1985 statement’s second harm. The statement says the denial of equality “promotes in men harmful attitudes and habits”; ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá had said that it holds men back from their own greatness.

Women and the work of peace

To an audience in Boston on 26 August 1912, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá turned the argument into a charge:

“Therefore, strive to show in the human world that women are most capable and efficient, that their hearts are more tender and susceptible than the hearts of men, that they are more philanthropic and responsive toward the needy and suffering, that they are inflexibly opposed to war and are lovers of peace. Strive that the ideal of international peace may become realized through the efforts of womankind, for man is more inclined to war than woman, and a real evidence of woman’s superiority will be her service and efficiency in the establishment of universal peace.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Franklin Square House, Boston, 26 August 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, p. 284

In the same talk He drew a limit: “Demonstrations of force, such as are now taking place in England, are neither becoming nor effective in the cause of womanhood and equality.” Equality is to be demonstrated by accomplishment, especially “toward the industrial and agricultural sciences, seeking to assist mankind in that which is most needful.” The aim is a world no longer ruled by force, and He did not endorse force as the means.

Force giving way

One passage connects the end of male domination directly to the end of the rule of force. It is recorded by J. E. Esslemont in Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era (p. 149), and the House of Justice quoted it as ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s in a letter of 24 January 1993:

“The world in the past has been ruled by force, and man has dominated over woman by reason of his more forceful and aggressive qualities both of body and mind. But the balance is already shifting; force is losing its dominance, and mental alertness, intuition, and the spiritual qualities of love and service, in which woman is strong, are gaining ascendancy. Hence the new age will be an age less masculine and more permeated with the feminine ideals, or, to speak more exactly, will be an age in which the masculine and feminine elements of civilization will be more evenly balanced.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, as recorded in Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era, p. 149 (women, no. 25)

The last clause corrects the one before it. The goal is not an age in which one set of qualities replaces the other, but one in which the two are “more evenly balanced.” The same structure appears in the 1985 paragraph: partnership, not reversal.

When men acknowledge it

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá also spoke of what men must do. In Paris He said: “When men own the equality of women, there will be no need for them to struggle for their rights!” (Paris Talks, “The Tenth Principle—Equality of Sex”). The 1985 statement’s word welcomed makes the same demand. And in a Tablet translated in the Women compilation, He describes the day when it will be met:

“Ere long the days shall come when the men addressing the women, shall say: ‘Blessed are ye! Blessed are ye! Verily ye are worthy of every gift.’” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Tablet translated from the Persian, published in Paris Talks, pp. 182–184 (women, no. 23)

Shoghi Effendi: a martyr’s last words, and public opinion against war

Ṭáhirih

The phrase with which the 1985 paragraph opens, “the emancipation of women,” appears in God Passes By in the last recorded words of Ṭáhirih, the poet and scholar whom the Báb named among the Letters of the Living. At the Conference of Badasht in 1848, Shoghi Effendi writes, she “appeared suddenly, adorned yet unveiled, before the assembled companions.” She was put to death in Ṭihrán in 1852, in the persecution that followed the attempt on the Sháh’s life. Shoghi Effendi describes her end:

“Thus ended the life of this great Bábí heroine, the first woman suffrage martyr, who, at her death, turning to the one in whose custody she had been placed, had boldly declared: ‘You can kill me as soon as you like, but you cannot stop the emancipation of women.’” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By (god-passes-by)

He records that before her execution “she put on the attire of a bride, and anointed herself with perfume,” and that she was strangled “with that same silken kerchief which she had intuitively reserved for that purpose.” In 2008 the House of Justice reminded the Bahá’ís of Iran that Ṭáhirih “courageously advocated the emancipation of women in 1848, at a time when efforts to improve the status of women were only beginning to gather momentum in a few parts of the world.”

The principle among the conditions of peace

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi summarises the principles ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá proclaimed on His Western journeys. Among them is “the equality of men and women, the two wings on which the bird of human kind is able to soar,” listed beside the condemnation of prejudice, compulsory education, a universal auxiliary language, the abolition of the extremes of wealth and poverty, a world tribunal, and “the establishment of a permanent and universal peace as the supreme goal of all mankind.” Each of the seven issues in the 1985 statement is already present in this list in some form (racial, national and religious prejudice appear together under the condemnation of prejudice), placed side by side as parts of one programme. Shoghi Effendi adds that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá “supplemented” this exposition “with grave and reiterated warnings of an impending conflagration” in Europe. The peace teachings, including equality, were offered as the alternative to a war He saw coming.

“There can be no war”

A letter written on behalf of the Guardian in 1945, in the last months of the Second World War, explains what ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá meant when He spoke of women arising for peace:

“What ‘Abdu’l-Bahá meant about the women arising for peace is that this a matter which vitally affects women, and when they form a conscious and overwhelming mass of public opinion against war there can be no war. The Bahá’í women are already organized through being members of the Faith and the Administrative Order. No further organization is needed. But they should, through teaching and through the active moral support they give to every movement directed towards peace, seek to exert a strong influence on other women’s minds in regard to this essential matter.” — Letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi to an individual believer, 24 March 1945 (women, no. 89)

The letter makes the mechanism plain. Women end war not by any power peculiar to them but by forming public opinion, “conscious and overwhelming.” It also sets out a method: no separate organisation, but teaching and “active moral support” to every movement for peace.

The Universal House of Justice after 1985

1986: a goal of the Six Year Plan

Four months after the statement, the House of Justice set the major objectives of the Six Year Plan (25 February 1986). Under “Greater involvement of the Faith in the life of human society,” it asked national communities to “foster the practice of the equality of the sexes both in the life of the Bahá’í community and in society as a whole and, for this purpose, hold special conferences and training programs for women and for men.” The final words matter: training programmes for men as well. If the denial of equality forms harmful habits in men, men must be part of the remedy.

1991: “a harbinger of the approach of global peace”

To the Pacific Women’s Conference, held from 30 May to 7 June 1991, the House of Justice wrote:

“One of the most encouraging developments, as a new millennium approaches, is the speed at which women everywhere are arising to assume an equal part with men in the management of the affairs of humankind. For the Bahá’í community this fundamental change in the role played by women is a harbinger of the approach of global peace. Only as the spiritual and intellectual capacities which have found particularly vigorous expression in women are able to exert their proper influence in the life of society will the conditions for lasting global reconciliation emerge.” — The Universal House of Justice, 10 May 1991 (muhj-1986-2001)

The last sentence has the same form as the last sentence of the 1985 paragraph (“Only as … will”). It adds a reason the 1985 text left implicit. Certain capacities “have found particularly vigorous expression in women”; while women are excluded, those capacities are missing from the life of society, and reconciliation lacks them. The message closes with women “awakening to the role they alone can play in building a just and peaceful world.”

1992: an office at the United Nations

On 10 December 1992 the House of Justice announced the Office for the Advancement of Women, an agency of the Bahá’í International Community, to “promote the principles of the Faith through its interaction with international entities concerned with matters affecting the rights, status and well-being of women.” It called the office “a visible instrument for the practical application of one of the cardinal principles of the Cause of Bahá’u’lláh.”

1993: the first station on the path — the home

The 1985 paragraph says harmful habits begin “in the family.” A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 24 January 1993, in reply to questions about violence and abuse, speaks to that first station directly:

“The use of force by the physically strong against the weak, as a means of imposing one’s will and fulfilling one’s desires, is a flagrant transgression of the Bahá’í Teachings. There can be no justification for anyone compelling another, through the use of force or through the threat of violence, to do that to which the other person is not inclined.” — Letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 24 January 1993 (muhj-1986-2001; also in set-world-order)

It quotes ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: “In this, the cycle of Almighty God, violence and force, constraint and oppression, are one and all condemned.” It states that “for a man to use force to impose his will on a woman is a serious transgression of the Bahá’í Teachings,” and that “no Bahá’í husband should ever beat his wife.” It also names what men can show the world:

“Bahá’í men have the opportunity to demonstrate to the world around them a new approach to the relationship between the sexes, where aggression and the use of force are eliminated and replaced by cooperation and consultation.” — Letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 24 January 1993

The phrase “aggression and the use of force are eliminated and replaced by cooperation and consultation” could describe relations between nations; in this letter it describes a marriage. That is the 1985 path seen from its starting point: the methods of relationship a man practises at home are the methods he will bring to every larger setting. The letter adds that consultation in the family “requires all participants to express their opinions with absolute freedom,” and that this is “unattainable if the fear of violence or abuse is present.”

2002: the principle accepted in discourse, resisted in religion

In April 2002 the House of Justice wrote “To the World’s Religious Leaders.” It observed that the equality of the sexes had changed status in the seventeen years since the Promise:

“In the past, apart from isolated exceptions, women were regarded as an inferior breed, their nature hedged about by superstitions, denied the opportunity to express the potentialities of the human spirit and relegated to the role of serving the needs of men. Clearly, there are many societies where such conditions persist and are even fanatically defended. At the level of global discourse, however, the concept of the equality of the sexes has, for all practical purposes, now assumed the force of universally accepted principle.” — The Universal House of Justice, April 2002, to the World’s Religious Leaders

It restated the 1985 triple denial in slightly different words: the emancipation of women “has entailed the willingness of both society’s institutions and popular opinion to acknowledge that there are no acceptable grounds—biological, social or moral—to justify denying women full equality with men, and girls equal educational opportunities with boys.” The letter’s concern was that religious leadership lagged behind this change. The principle had become, in this sense, more acknowledged in the world at large; it remained less acknowledged in the institutions that claim to speak for God.

2008: “not a Western construct”

On 20 June 2008 the House of Justice wrote to the Bahá’ís of Iran on “Equality of Men and Women in Families and the Community.” It made the link to peace explicit and gave the principle its standing:

“For you, the equality of men and women is not a Western construct but a universal spiritual truth—a statement about human nature—that was promulgated by Bahá’u’lláh nearly one hundred and fifty years ago in His native Iran. That women should enjoy equal rights with men is a requirement of justice. It is a principle consonant with the highest standard of purity and sanctity, whose application strengthens family life and is essential to the regeneration and progress of any nation. Indeed, peace in the world and the advancement of civilization depend on its realization.” — The Universal House of Justice, 20 June 2008, to the Bahá’ís of Iran (muhj-2001-2022)

It recalled Ṭáhirih in 1848; the Tarbíyat School for Girls founded in Ṭihrán in 1911; half a century of Bahá’í women “shoulder to shoulder with men” in administering the community; and the elimination of illiteracy among women under forty. And it said the work was not done: “True equality is not easily attained; the transformation required is difficult for men and women alike.”

2009: the path restated

The clearest elaboration of the 1985 path came eighteen months later, in a letter of 24 November 2009 to the Bahá’ís of Iran on family life. Its wording follows the 1985 sentence closely:

“The family unit, the nucleus of human society, constitutes a space within which praiseworthy morals and essential capacities must be developed, for the habits and patterns of conduct nurtured in the home are carried into the workplace, into the social and political life of the country, and finally into the arena of international relations.” — The Universal House of Justice, 24 November 2009, to the Bahá’ís of Iran (muhj-2001-2022)

It then names what the denial of equality produces in that space:

“Among the signs of moral decay in the present social order is the weakening of the spiritual ties that bind the family. Failure within the household to acknowledge the equality of the sexes and to respect the rights of children gives rise to a culture that belittles women and children, condones the imposition of a single will upon others, and opens the way for aggression and violence—first in the family, then at school and work, and eventually in the streets and in society at large. Under such circumstances, the family environment, potentially the ideal milieu for learning the principles of consultation and collective decision making, serves to perpetuate tyranny and oppression in society.” — The Universal House of Justice, 24 November 2009

Here the “harmful attitudes and habits” of 1985 are named: a culture that “belittles women and children” and “condones the imposition of a single will upon others.” The path is traced again, from the family to the school, the workplace, the streets and society. And the alternative is stated: the family as “the ideal milieu for learning the principles of consultation and collective decision making.” A home that practises equality and consultation trains the people who will later practise them among nations; a home that practises domination trains people for tyranny.

2014: change of attitude, not contest

A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 21 January 2014 answered a question about how the Bahá’í approach to equality differs from others. “The main concern and contribution of the followers of Bahá’u’lláh is the spiritual transformation of human society,” it said, and “the Bahá’í Faith directs its attention principally to the promotion of attitudinal change.” It warned that discussion of the subject “in the wider society is, alas, not immune” from contention and extreme positions, and asked for “a greater level of detachment” and humility. The method matches the end. The 1985 paragraph asks for a climate in which peace can emerge; a campaign carried on through contest would add to the very attitudes the paragraph wants removed.

2019: the prerequisites, embodied

On 18 January 2019, a hundred years after the Paris Peace Conference opened, the House of Justice wrote to the Bahá’ís of the world about humanity’s progress towards peace. It looked back to 1985 and described what Bahá’í communities had been learning since:

“They are learning how to nurture communities that embody those prerequisites of peace we identified in 1985. They cultivate environments in which children can be raised untainted by any form of racial, national, or religious prejudice. They champion the full equality of women with men in the affairs of the community.” — The Universal House of Justice, 18 January 2019, to the Bahá’ís of the World

The letter repeats the word prerequisites from the 1985 paragraph. The community’s offer of itself “as a model for study” depends on its practising these prerequisites first.

2021: habits that endure

Two letters at the end of 2021 show that the House of Justice does not treat the work as finished within the Bahá’í community either. On 10 December 2021, writing to a National Spiritual Assembly about the low number of women serving on its institutions, it said that “applying the principle of the equality of women and men is essential to the regeneration and progress of any nation, the peace of the world, and the advancement of civilization,” and warned:

“This the dear friends must not doubt: the subtle influence of habits of thought, culture, and traditions that are inconsonant with the Bahá’í standard endure even among dedicated followers of the Blessed Beauty if there is no conscious and unflagging resolve to counter them.” — The Universal House of Justice, 10 December 2021, to a National Spiritual Assembly (muhj-2001-2022)

On 30 December 2021, listing the challenges Auxiliary Board members must help communities face, it included “to learn to put aside inherited customs and attitudes that belong to humanity’s period of adolescence” and “to put the equality of women and men into practice.”

The phrase “habits of thought” connects the two ends of this history. In 1985 the House of Justice told the world that the denial of equality forms harmful habits in men. In 2021 it told a Bahá’í community that such habits survive even faith, unless they are resisted on purpose.

The Bahá’í International Community (institutional statements)

The Bahá’í International Community, which represents the Bahá’í community at the United Nations, has carried the 1985 argument into international forums. Its statements are institutional discourse, not authoritative guidance. Two of them extend the 1985 paragraph.

In The Prosperity of Humankind (1995) it wrote that “the principle of the equality of the sexes is fundamental to all realistic thinking about the future well-being of the earth and its people,” and that it “represents a truth about human nature that has waited largely unrecognized throughout the long ages of the race’s childhood and adolescence.”

In Turning Point for All Nations (1995), a statement on the fiftieth anniversary of the United Nations, it wrote that “the creation of a peaceful and sustainable world civilization will be impossible without the full participation of women in every arena of human activity.” It named violence against women and girls “one of the most blatant and widespread abuses of human rights,” and said that “even strong legal remedies and enforcement mechanisms will have little effect until they are supported by a transformation in the attitudes of men.” It restated the 1985 path in its own words: “the family remains the basic building block of society and behaviors observed and learned there will be projected onto interactions at all other levels of society.”

The argument assembled

Read together, the sources give a single account of why the equality of women and men is a prerequisite of peace. It has four parts.

  1. Equality is a truth about human beings. Bahá’u’lláh states that women and men “have been and will always be equal in the sight of God” and that He has “lifted distinctions” between them. A world order built on a false account of human beings will not hold. That is why the 1985 statement can say there are “no grounds” for denial: the question is settled at its root.
  1. Inequality forms the habits that make war. The home is the first school. Where the stronger imposes his will on the weaker, boys learn domination, and they carry it into work, politics and the relations of nations (1985, 1993, 2009). War is not the expression of an unchangeable human nature but, in part, of habits learned and therefore unlearnable. This answers the conviction of “inevitable quarrelsomeness” at the root of the paralysis of will.
  1. Exclusion leaves out the capacities peace needs. While women are absent from “the great arena of laws and politics,” decisions about war are made without those who raise the children who fight it (‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, 1912), and without the “spiritual and intellectual capacities which have found particularly vigorous expression in women” (1991). The human race is incomplete; “humanity has been defective and inefficient because it has been incomplete.”
  1. Ranking itself breeds strife. “All differences and distinction are conducive to discord and strife.” A society that accepts the subordination of half its members has accepted the principle of superiority, the same principle that Part III of the Promise names as the common root of every prejudice: “everything which enables people to consider themselves superior to others.”

The remedy follows the same four parts: recognition of the truth; transformation of the home through consultation and the end of force; welcome of women into full partnership in every field, beginning with the education of girls; and the abandonment of every form of superiority. The action asked of men is named directly. They must welcome. “When men own the equality of women, there will be no need for them to struggle for their rights.”

The present field (external sources — description only, current as of October 2026)

The corpus supplies the principle; it cannot describe the world as it now is. The following sources describe the field. They are not authority for any Bahá’í teaching, and their figures will change.

  • The link is now on the Security Council’s agenda. On 31 October 2000 the United Nations Security Council adopted resolution 1325 on women, peace and security, the first Council resolution to address women’s participation in preventing and resolving conflict. In the terms of the 1985 statement, a link that was “less acknowledged” has since been acknowledged formally at the highest level of the international system.
  • Participation remains low. The UN Secretary-General’s report on women, peace and security, released on the twenty-fifth anniversary of resolution 1325, reported that in 2024 “9 out of 10 peace processes had no women negotiators,” with women “making up just 7 per cent of negotiators and 14 per cent of mediators globally.” The same release reported that “676 million women now live within 50 kilometers of deadly conflict, the highest level since the 1990s,” and that conflict-related sexual violence “increased by 87 per cent in two years” (UN Women press release, 20 October 2025).
  • Research on durability. A 2018 study by Jana Krause, Werner Krause and Piia Bränfors in International Interactions found a robust correlation between peace agreements signed by women delegates and the durability of peace, and more provisions for political reform with higher implementation rates in those agreements. It attributes the effect to links between women signatories and women’s civil-society groups. It reports a correlation, not proof of cause.
  • Research on the status of women and state peacefulness. Valerie Hudson, Bonnie Ballif-Spanvill, Mary Caprioli and Chad Emmett, in Sex and World Peace (Columbia University Press, 2012), argue from the WomanStats database that the security of women is closely tied to the peacefulness of states. The claim that it is the strongest predictor of peacefulness is the authors’ own framing as reported in coverage of the book; I have not checked it against the book’s statistical models.

These findings bear on the 1985 paragraph without settling its claims. The paragraph says the denial of equality forms habits that travel “from the family … ultimately to international relations,” and that peace needs a “moral and psychological climate.” The research measures correlations between the status of women and the peacefulness of states or agreements; the statement’s claim concerns the moral formation of human beings, which such measures reach only indirectly.

Open questions and limits

  • “Woman by nature is opposed to war.” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s talks speak of women’s tenderness of heart and natural opposition to war. These statements give one of His reasons, and they are not to be dismissed. But the 1985 statement does not rest its case on them. It rests on justice (“an injustice against one half of the world’s population”), on the formation of men’s habits, and on the need for “full partnership.” The 1945 letter on behalf of the Guardian explains the mechanism as public opinion, not innate virtue. A reading that makes women the sole guardians of peace, and men incapable of it, departs from the 1985 text, which asks for partnership and for a change in men.
  • The military exemption. In a Tablet published in Paris Talks (pp. 182–184), ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá says women are exempt from military engagements in defence of a community, “because their hearts are tender and they cannot endure the sight of the horror of carnage.” This is a statement about exemption from combat, not about exclusion from public life; the same Tablet says women “will enter, in the future, into all branches of the administration of society.”
  • “A spiritual world, not a political one.” A Tablet of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá to Persian women, in the Women compilation (no. 11), says “the world of women should be a spiritual world, not a political one.” Elsewhere He says women must “enter confidently and capably the great arena of laws and politics.” The Tablet concerns a women’s assembly in Persia and the subjects of its gatherings; it is not a general bar on women’s participation in government, which He calls “the inalienable right of women” in the matter of voting. I have not found an authoritative text that addresses the two statements together.
  • The Esslemont passage. The words “The world in the past has been ruled by force” come from Esslemont’s record of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s words, not from a Tablet. The House of Justice quoted them as ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s in 1993, and the Women compilation includes them, so they are cited here; but their textual standing is that of a recorded saying.
  • Membership of the Universal House of Justice. This page does not discuss the provision that confines membership of the Universal House of Justice to men. It is treated at gender-equality-uhj-membership and in the concept page.
  • Translations. Several Tablets quoted here are provisional translations from the Women compilation, and the 1912 talks are from notes taken by listeners, as the Promulgation records for each talk.
  • A correction to a sibling page. The concept page EqualityOfWomenAndMen dates the House of Justice’s letter on equality to the Bahá’ís of Iran as 2 April 2010. In Messages from the Universal House of Justice 2001–2022 that letter is dated 20 June 2008; the letter of 2 April 2010 concerns the economic life of the Iranian community.

For the song

The heart of it, in one sentence. The House of Justice calls the emancipation of women one of the most important, though least acknowledged, conditions of peace, because denying women equality wrongs half of humanity and teaches men habits of domination that travel from the home to the workplace, to politics and finally to war between nations, and peace can only emerge when women are welcomed as full partners in every field of life.

Concrete images, stories, people, places and moments

  • The two wings of one bird — neither wing can fly alone; the weak wing grounds the strong one.
  • The path of a habit — a boy learning at home that the stronger decides, then carrying that lesson to work, to the council chamber, and to the war room. The 1985 sentence and the 2009 letter both trace it.
  • The mother and the twenty years — “What sleepless nights and restless, anxious days she has spent!” — a son raised for twenty years, and the mother asked to send him to the battlefield.
  • The Woman’s Suffrage Meeting, Metropolitan Temple, New York, 20 May 1912 — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá telling suffragists that the vote for women and the end of war are one cause.
  • A letter to The Hague, 1919 — after the First World War, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá writing to a peace society and putting the equality of women and men among the conditions of peace.
  • Paris, October 1911 — news of the battle at Benghazi, and widows, orphans and “women … weeping for their slain sons.”
  • Ṭáhirih at Badasht, 1848 — a woman appearing “adorned yet unveiled, before the assembled companions”; and in 1852, dressed as a bride, perfumed, handing over the silk kerchief that would be used to kill her, and saying the emancipation of women could not be stopped.
  • The Tarbíyat School for Girls, Ṭihrán, 1911 — a school opened for girls of every background by a persecuted community.
  • A peace conference with no women at the table — the present-day picture, set against the 1985 call.
  • The table set for consultation — the 1993 letter’s image of a marriage in which force is replaced by “cooperation and consultation,” the same methods that peace between nations requires.
  • The Pacific islands, 1991 — small, scattered nations whose women’s conference the House of Justice called a sign of “the approach of global peace.”

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • Not a battle of the sexes. The statement asks for “full partnership,” and the House of Justice warns against contention and extreme positions (2014). ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said demonstrations of force were “neither becoming nor effective in the cause of womanhood.” A song that makes men the enemy works against the remedy the texts prescribe. This does not soften the judgement: denial of equality is “an injustice” for which there are “no grounds.”
  • Not “women good, men bad.” The 1985 text says the denial “promotes in men harmful attitudes and habits”; habits are learned and can be unlearned. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá said men themselves are held back from “the greatness which might be theirs.” Men are both the ones who must change and among those who gain.
  • Not peace by women’s nature alone. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s talks speak of mothers’ unwillingness to send their sons to war, and that image sings well. But the remedy the texts name is participation: the vote, “the great arena of laws and politics,” public opinion, partnership. A song that makes women passive symbols of peace, rather than participants in making it, misses the point.
  • Not a reversal. The goal is an age in which “the masculine and feminine elements of civilization will be more evenly balanced” — not one in which women rule men.
  • Not identity of role. The Writings speak of equality of rights and capacities, and also of the mother as the first educator. A song can honour motherhood without suggesting that women’s place is confined to the home; the House of Justice states plainly that it is not.
  • Not a Western idea. The House of Justice told the Iranian Bahá’ís that equality “is not a Western construct but a universal spiritual truth,” proclaimed by Bahá’u’lláh in Iran. Ṭáhirih, whom Shoghi Effendi called “the first woman suffrage martyr,” was an Iranian woman.
  • Not a problem solved. The House of Justice wrote in 2008 that ”true equality is not easily attained,“ and in 2021 that old habits ”endure even among dedicated followers.“ Neither the world nor the Bahá’í community is presented as having arrived.
  • Attribute accurately. The two-wings image is ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s, stated as one of the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh; the ”ruled by force“ passage is ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá as recorded by Esslemont; Ṭáhirih’s last words are as recorded by Shoghi Effendi; the 1945 ”public opinion“ sentence is from a letter written on the Guardian’s behalf, not by him.
