Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb (Tablet of the Wondrous Maiden)

Last updated 17 October 2026

The Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb is a Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh in which a heavenly Maiden unveils her face, gives the lovers wine and song, is denied by those who doubt, and leaves with a warning. Each of its thirty verses ends in a refrain: “How wondrous … how wondrous indeed!” in most verses, and “How astounding … how astounding indeed!” in verses 9, 21–27 and 30. The authorised English translation is in Days of Remembrance, among the selections for Riḍván. This page gives that translation in full, says what is known of the Tablet, and lists what a songwriter can use from it. It is one page in the series Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poem

Where the text is. The Tablet is the twelfth selection in Days of Remembrance, in the chapter for Riḍván. Its paragraph address is DOR 12.1 to DOR 12.30. The volume attaches no note to it. The volume’s preface says that some of the Tablets in it are “expressed in a celebratory and uplifting tone, occasionally with repeated refrains” (DOR Preface 6) and that “the current translations endeavour to afford a glimpse of the poetic tone of these celebrated Texts, even though they can never convey their full beauty” (DOR Preface 7). The preface does not name this Tablet in either sentence.

When and where. The authorised volume gives no date or place. Scholars place it in Baghdad, shortly before Bahá’u’lláh’s declaration at Riḍván in 1863:

  • Adib Taherzadeh, in The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, Vol. 1, p. 218, lists it among the Tablets “revealed in the same period” as the odes of the months before the Declaration, a chapter he opens with the words “As the year 1863 drew closer, the signs of the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh’s Mission became increasingly manifest”.
  • Winters’s bibliography of Tablets of 1853–63 and the Leiden list of Tablets (McGlinn), both in the Baha’i Library, give the place as Baghdad and the language as Arabic rhyming prose. The Leiden list cites its printed text as “Ad‘iyyih-e H. Maḥbúb”, pp. 153–158.
  • John Walbridge lists it among the “tablets and writings associated with Ridvan” in his article on Riḍván, citing Ayyám-i-Tis‘ih pp. 251–54 and Taherzadeh. The authorised volume’s placing under Riḍván agrees with this grouping.

None of these sources gives a day or a month. See “What is not settled.”

Language and form. Taherzadeh writes that it “is in Arabic.” Winters and the Leiden list call it rhyming prose. The English text has thirty numbered verses, and every verse ends with a refrain of the same pattern. The refrain changes to “How astounding” in verse 9 and in verses 21–27 and 30 (for example “How astounding a denial, how astounding indeed!”). Verse 29 ends “as bidden by One mighty and bounteous indeed!”

What scholars say it is about. Taherzadeh describes the Tablet as one that “conveys the same glad-tidings” as the two before it in his list, “is written in allegorical language and contains the symbolism of the ‘Maid of Heaven’.” He says that in it “Bahá’u’lláh alludes to the unveiling of His glorious station,” that the outpouring of the Revelation is so great as to leave “the pure in heart” dumbfounded, and that He “denounces the perversity and blindness of the unfaithful among His companions,” which Taherzadeh reads as an allusion to Mírzá Yaḥyá and his associates. The reading of the rejecting “people of the Book” as Yaḥyá’s party is Taherzadeh’s; the Tablet does not name anyone. Walbridge’s summary is shorter: in the allegory “the Maid of Heaven comes forth and unveils herself,” her beauty inflames creation, she passes round the wine of life and plays music, the arrogant reject her, and she returns to her palace in sadness “vowing not to return to them until the Day of Resurrection.”

The text in English

Authorised translation (Days of Remembrance, Tablet of the Wondrous Maiden, DOR 12.1–12.30). Licence: `ok-with-credit` (© Bahá’í International Community; check the bahai.org terms before a commercial release). The verse numbers are those of the volume.

1. The hallowed Beauty shone resplendent from behind the veil. How wondrous a thing, how wondrous indeed!

2. And, lo, the flame of rapture caused all souls to swoon away. How wondrous is this, how wondrous indeed!

3. Rising up, they soared unto the blest pavilion ’neath the throne of heaven’s canopy. How wondrous a mystery, how wondrous indeed!

4. Say: The Maiden of Eternity unveiled Her face—may her wondrous beauty be exalted indeed!—

5. Shedding forth from earth to heaven its resplendent rays. How wondrous a light, how wondrous indeed!

6. A lightning glance She cast, as piercing as a shooting star—how wondrous Her glance, how wondrous indeed!—

7. A glance consuming every name and every title in its flames. How wondrous a feat, how wondrous indeed!

8. To the dwellers of the realm of dust She turned Her gaze. How wondrous Her gaze, how wondrous indeed!

9. And then did all creation shake and pass away. How astounding a death, how astounding indeed!

10. She then let fall a raven lock, an ornament of spirit in the darkest night—how wondrous a hue, how wondrous indeed!—

11. From which the fragrant breezes of the spirit were perceived. How wondrous a scent, how wondrous indeed!

12. In Her right hand She bore the ruby wine and in Her left a portion of the finest fare. How wondrous a grace, how wondrous indeed!

13. With hands encrimsoned with Her ardent lovers’ blood—how wondrous is this, how wondrous indeed!—

14. In cups and chalices She passed round the wine of life. How wondrous a draught, how wondrous indeed!

15. With harp and lute She sang in praise of Her Beloved. How wondrous a song, how wondrous indeed!

16. Whereat the hearts were melted in consuming flames. How wondrous a love, how wondrous indeed!

17. Of Her sustaining beauty She bestowed a boundless share—how wondrous a share, how wondrous indeed!—

18. Then brought Her sword of charm upon Her lovers’ necks. How wondrous a blow, how wondrous indeed!

19. Her pearl-like teeth did flash, no sooner had She smiled. How wondrous a pearl, how wondrous indeed!

20. Whereat the hearts of them that know cried out and wept. How wondrous a piety, how wondrous indeed!

21. But they that doubt and boast of self denied Her truth. How astounding a denial, how astounding indeed!

22. And, hearing this, in sorrow, She repaired to Her abode. How astounding Her grief, how astounding indeed!

23. She returned from whence She came: How lofty were the steps She traced! How astounding a decree, how astounding indeed!

24. She cried a cry of anguish, as to reduce all things to naught. How astounding Her woe, how astounding indeed!

25. And from Her lips there streamed these words of warning and rebuke—how astounding a stream, how astounding indeed!—

26. “Why do ye gainsay Me, O people of the Book?” How astounding is this, how astounding indeed!

27. “Claim ye to be the guided and the loved ones of the Lord?” By God! How astounding a lie, how astounding indeed!

28. “O my friends,” She said, “We shall not come again,”—how wondrous a return, how wondrous indeed!—

29. “But will conceal God’s secrets in His Scriptures and His Books,” as bidden by One mighty and bounteous indeed!

30. “Nor shall ye find Me till the Promised One appear on Judgement Day.” By My life! How astounding an abasement, how astounding indeed!

A provisional translation, for comparison. Denis MacEoin translated the Tablet from the text in the Ad‘iyyih collection, pp. 153–158, in his Rituals in Babism and Bahá’ísm (Pembroke Persian Series, Vol. 2, 1994, Appendix XVII, pp. 132–133). This is a provisional translation by a scholar, not authorised, and the copyright is the publisher’s: licence `permission-needed`. It is quoted here only briefly, to show where it differs. MacEoin renders the opening as “The Beauty of holiness hath risen from behind the veil. And this is a wondrous thing!” His refrain is a plain “And this is a wondrous …”, while the authorised text varies the refrain’s exclamation and its noun from verse to verse. Five differences are worth noting:

  • Verse 5: MacEoin has the light shine “from the earth unto the clouds”; the authorised text has “from earth to heaven.”
  • Verse 10: MacEoin has a “jet-black hair, like the ornament of the spirit in the darkness of torment”; the authorised text has “a raven lock, an ornament of spirit in the darkest night.”
  • Verse 12: MacEoin has “a portion of roast sweetmeats”; the authorised text has “a portion of the finest fare.”
  • Verse 18: MacEoin has “the sword of her flashing eyes”; the authorised text has “Her sword of charm.”
  • Verse 20: MacEoin has the hearts of “the possessors of minds” crying out, with the refrain “a wondrous renunciation”; the authorised text has “the hearts of them that know” crying out and weeping, with the refrain “How wondrous a piety.”

Where the two differ, the authorised text is the one to quote.

No other English translation of the whole Tablet is in the corpus. No Persian or Arabic original of this Tablet is in the saved texts under `sources/web/poetry/originals/`, so the Arabic cannot be checked here.

Themes and images

The unveiling. The Tablet opens with a Beauty appearing “from behind the veil” (verse 1) and then names the Maiden: “The Maiden of Eternity unveiled Her face” (verse 4). Light goes out “from earth to heaven” (verse 5). Her glance is “as piercing as a shooting star” and burns “every name and every title in its flames” (verses 6–7). The unveiling is described through beauty, light, fire and a glance.

The effect on creation. The flame of rapture makes “all souls … swoon away” (verse 2). When She turns Her gaze to “the dwellers of the realm of dust,” “all creation” shakes and passes away (verses 8–9), and the text exclaims “How astounding a death.” The souls that swooned in verse 2 rise to “the blest pavilion ’neath the throne of heaven’s canopy” (verse 3). The first nine verses give a sequence: the vision, the loss of self, the rising up, and the shaking of creation.

What She brings. She lets fall “a raven lock” from which “the fragrant breezes of the spirit” come (verses 10–11). In her right hand is “the ruby wine,” in her left “a portion of the finest fare” (verse 12). She passes “the wine of life” in “cups and chalices” (verse 14), sings “in praise of Her Beloved” with “harp and lute” (verse 15), and gives “a boundless share” of “Her sustaining beauty” (verse 17). The hands that give are “encrimsoned with Her ardent lovers’ blood” (verse 13), and “Her sword of charm” falls “on Her lovers’ necks” (verse 18). The gifts and the wounding are given together.

Two responses. When She smiles, “the hearts of them that know cried out and wept” (verses 19–20). “But they that doubt and boast of self denied Her truth” (verse 21). The Tablet sets the two responses side by side and names the second a “denial.”

Her withdrawal and her words. She “repaired to Her abode” and “returned from whence She came” (verses 22–23). She cries “a cry of anguish” (verse 24) and speaks “words of warning and rebuke” (verse 25). Her speech is the only direct speech in the Tablet, and it is in verses 26–30:

  • She asks the “people of the Book” why they gainsay her, and questions their claim to be “the guided and the loved ones of the Lord” (verses 26–27).
  • She says, “We shall not come again,” that she “will conceal God’s secrets in His Scriptures and His Books,” and that “ye” will not find her “till the Promised One appear on Judgement Day” (verses 28–30).

The Tablet ends on this. It does not describe her return.

The Maiden elsewhere in the Writings. The image is not confined to this Tablet. Shoghi Effendi, introducing Bahá’u’lláh’s own account in the Súratu’l-Haykal of His first revelation in the Síyáh-Chál of Ṭihrán, writes of “those breathless moments when the Maiden, symbolizing the ‘Most Great Spirit’ proclaimed His mission to the entire creation” (GPB VI.28). In the passage he quotes, Bahá’u’lláh says: “Turning My face, I beheld a Maiden—the embodiment of the remembrance of the name of My Lord—suspended in the air before Me” (Súriy-i-Haykal; also SLH 1.6). The Maiden speaks in her own voice elsewhere in the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh and the Báb:

  • “I am the Maid of Heaven, the Offspring begotten by the Spirit of Bahá” (Bahá’u’lláh, GWB CXXIX.11).
  • “By God! I am the Maid of Heaven, abiding in the midmost heart of Paradise” (Súriy-i-Qalam, DOR 23.17), after the command “O Pen! Announce unto the Maid of Paradise” (DOR 23.14). The volume’s note on that verse says that a dialogue between the Maid of Heaven and Bahá’u’lláh begins there.
  • “I am the Maid of Heaven begotten by the Spirit of Bahá” (the Báb, SWB 10.41).
  • In the Tablet of the Immortal Youth, a refrain of rejoicing: “Rejoice! This is the Maid of Heaven, come with a mighty dispensation” (DOR 27.20).

The Epistle to the Son of the Wolf does not contain the Maiden passage. It quotes a different Síyáh-Chál experience, the dream in which “these exalted words were heard on every side”; the Maiden passage is in the Súriy-i-Haykal. The Wilmette Institute’s notes list the Maiden of Heaven among the “characters” whose voice Bahá’u’lláh sometimes uses in His Tablets (scholarship, not authority). Kamran Ekbal has written on the Zoroastrian background of the image of the Maiden of Heaven (Scripture and Revelation, 1997, pp. 125–169; scholarship).

Because these passages come from different Tablets, a reader should not carry the Súriy-i-Haykal identification into the Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb without saying so. In the Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb itself the Maiden is not identified. Taherzadeh calls her the “Maid of Heaven” of the allegory. The Maiden’s rejection and withdrawal are particular to this Tablet.

What is not settled

  • Date. Taherzadeh puts it near the Declaration of 1863, Winters and the Leiden list say Baghdad, and the authorised volume places it under Riḍván. No source gives the exact date, and no source says it was revealed in the garden of Riḍván itself.
  • Verse or prose. The authorised volume does not say whether the original is verse. Winters and the Leiden list call it rhyming prose. This page therefore calls it a poem only in the sense used for the series: a Tablet with a regular refrain and rhyme.
  • Who the “people of the Book” are. Taherzadeh reads the rejection as an allusion to Mírzá Yaḥyá and his associates. The text of verses 21–30 speaks of “they that doubt and boast of self” and of the “people of the Book,” and does not name a group. A lyric should not name one either.
  • Who the Maiden is in this Tablet. The sources above call her a symbol (“Maid of Heaven”). Shoghi Effendi’s statement that the Maiden symbolises the Most Great Spirit is made about the Súriy-i-Haykal passage (GPB VI.28).
  • Translations. Only the authorised English text and MacEoin’s provisional version are in the corpus. The differences above show that the Arabic would have to be consulted to settle individual readings.
  • Verses 28–30. The reported speech has a line in the first person plural (“We shall not come again”) and then a promise (“Nor shall ye find Me till the Promised One appear on Judgement Day”). Walbridge reads the last as the Day of Resurrection. This page does not interpret it beyond what the text says.

For songwriting

Exact phrases (authorised translation, DOR 12; licence `ok-with-credit`):

  • “How wondrous a thing, how wondrous indeed!” (verse 1, the refrain; 8 words)
  • “The hallowed Beauty shone resplendent from behind the veil.” (verse 1; 9 words)
  • “The Maiden of Eternity unveiled Her face” (verse 4; 6 words)
  • “Shedding forth from earth to heaven its resplendent rays.” (verse 5; 9 words)
  • “In Her right hand She bore the ruby wine” (verse 12; 9 words)
  • “In cups and chalices She passed round the wine of life.” (verse 14; 11 words)
  • “With harp and lute She sang in praise of Her Beloved.” (verse 15; 11 words)
  • “Whereat the hearts were melted in consuming flames.” (verse 16; 8 words)
  • “Her pearl-like teeth did flash, no sooner had She smiled.” (verse 19; 10 words)
  • “She returned from whence She came” (verse 23; 6 words)
  • “Nor shall ye find Me till the Promised One appear on Judgement Day.” (verse 30; 13 words)

The refrain can be sung as written. Its second half, “how wondrous indeed!”, is the same in most verses; in verses 9, 21–27 and 30 it is “how astounding indeed!”

Concrete images: a face behind a veil; a flame that makes souls faint; a glance like a shooting star; a raven lock of hair and a scent from it; ruby wine in the right hand and food in the left; cups and chalices; harp and lute; hands reddened with the lovers’ blood; pearl-like teeth in a smile; a sword laid on the neck; an abode to which She returns; a cry of anguish.

A possible arc, taken from the Tablet’s own order:

  1. Appearance: the veil lifts and light crosses from earth to heaven (verses 1–5).
  2. Overwhelming: the glance, the swoon, the shaking of creation (verses 6–9).
  3. Gift: scent, wine, food, song, a boundless share (verses 10–17).
  4. Cost and joy together: the sword, the smile, the hearts that cry out and weep (verses 18–20).
  5. Denial and grief: those who doubt and boast; She goes home (verses 21–25).
  6. Rebuke and warning: the questions, the secrets concealed, the Promised One (verses 26–30).

A lyric must not claim:

  • that the Maiden is a particular person, or that the Tablet says who she is. Say “the Maiden” as the text does;
  • that the Tablet names or condemns a particular group or person. Taherzadeh’s reading of the rejection is scholarship about the Tablet, not a statement in it;
  • that the Tablet is dated or was revealed at the garden of Riḍván;
  • that the Maiden says she will return soon, or that this Tablet describes a reconciliation. Verses 28–30 speak of a coming away and a concealment, and of finding her when the Promised One appears;
  • that the Maiden’s identification with the Most Great Spirit (GPB VI.28) is stated in this Tablet;
  • that MacEoin’s wording is the text of the Writings. If the words of the verse differ from the authorised text, a singer should use the authorised ones;
  • that any line is a quotation if it is a paraphrase. Where words are adapted, say so in the credit.
