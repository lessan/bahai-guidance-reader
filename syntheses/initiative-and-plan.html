Individual Initiative and the Institutional Plan

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í teachings do not treat individual initiative as a concession the institutions make to enthusiastic believers. They treat it as the location of the community’s power, constitutionally protected and institutionally solicited. The Universal House of Justice put the division with unusual precision in a letter of 19 May 1994: “The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally, is divinely conferred on elected institutions. However, the power to accomplish the tasks of the community resides primarily in the mass of the believers.” Authority sits in one place; power sits in another; and the Constitution of the Universal House of Justice lists among the duties of that body “to safeguard the personal rights, freedom and initiative of individuals.” Initiative is not merely permitted. Protecting it is written into the charter of the highest institution in the Faith.

My position on the central question: the relationship is not “encouraged, coordinated, or constrained” as three competing options — it is all three at once, sorted by domain. In the domain of teaching, service, friendship and personal transformation, the believer is told in the strongest terms not to wait: “Let him not wait for any directions, or expect any special encouragement, from the elected representatives of his community.” In the domain of the Faith’s collective interests — anything undertaken in the community’s name, with its funds, on its behalf, or about it in public — consultation with the institutions is not optional and never has been. The apparent collision between Shoghi Effendi’s injunction and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s command that believers take “no step without consulting the Spiritual Assembly” is not a contradiction; it is two rules addressed to two different territories, and the community has spent a century learning to tell them apart.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest: for most believers the binding constraint is not institutional restriction. It is permission-seeking that no institution ever asked for. The characteristic failure of a serious Bahá’í in 2026 is not acting rashly without consultation; it is waiting to be assigned a role in a Plan whose smallest working unit is a decision only that believer can make — “what he or she will do to serve the Plan, and as to how, where and when to do it.” Nobody else can make it, and no cluster agency is waiting to hand it out.

Two commands that look like they collide

Read side by side, the two most-quoted passages on this subject appear to point in opposite directions, and any honest treatment has to begin there rather than smooth it over.

The first is from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, quoted by Shoghi Effendi in his letter of 5 March 1922 to the Bahá’ís of the United States and Canada — one of the founding documents of Bahá’í administration:

“It is incumbent upon every one not to take any step without consulting the Spiritual Assembly, and they must assuredly obey with heart and soul its bidding and be submissive unto it, that things may be properly ordered and well arranged. Otherwise every person will act independently and after his own judgment, will follow his own desire, and do harm to the Cause.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, quoted in Shoghi Effendi’s letter of 5 March 1922 (Bahá’í Administration, p. 21)

The second is Shoghi Effendi’s own, sixteen years later, in The Advent of Divine Justice, addressing believers who might imagine that the inter-American teaching work belonged to the committees appointed to run it:

“It is the bounden duty of every American believer, as the faithful trustee of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Divine Plan, to initiate, promote, and consolidate, within the limits fixed by the administrative principles of the Faith, any activity he or she deems fit to undertake for the furtherance of the Plan… Let him not wait for any directions, or expect any special encouragement, from the elected representatives of his community, nor be deterred by any obstacles which his relatives, or fellow-citizens may be inclined to place in his path, nor mind the censure of his critics or enemies.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice (1938)

Both are binding. Neither has been softened by later guidance; the House of Justice quoted the second verbatim in its Riḍván 1998 message and has never qualified the first. So the analytical task is the one the Bahá’í authority framework always sets when two authoritative sources seem to conflict: find the missing connecting concept rather than declare one of them superseded (bahai-authority-framework).

The connecting concept is domain, and Shoghi Effendi supplies it himself in a letter written on his behalf on 30 August 1933:

“The principle of consultation, which constitutes one of the basic laws of the Administration, should be applied to all Bahá’í activities which affect the collective interests of the Faith, for it is through co-operation and continual exchange of thoughts and views that the Cause can best safeguard and foster its interests. Individual initiative, personal ability and resourcefulness, though indispensable, are, unless supported and enriched by the collective experiences and wisdom of the group, utterly incapable of achieving such a tremendous task.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 30 August 1933 (Social Action)

Activities which affect the collective interests of the Faith. That is the trigger — not “activities”, not “anything to do with the Faith”. The 5 March 1922 letter says the same thing in its own terms two pages later: “all matters without any exception whatsoever, regarding the interests of the Cause in … [a] locality, individually or collectively, should be referred exclusively to the Spiritual Assembly in that locality.” The reference class is the Cause’s interests in a place, not the believer’s own acts of service. And the Advent passage contains its own limiting clause, usually dropped when the sentence is quoted for encouragement: within the limits fixed by the administrative principles of the Faith. Neither text is unconditional. Each names the other’s territory.

Eight decades later the House of Justice named the same distinction as a capacity — something a maturing community learns rather than a rule it looks up. Writing to the Conference of the Continental Boards of Counsellors on 28 December 2010, it described what happens as a Bahá’í community grows up: “all come to discern with ease those areas of activity in which the individual can best exercise initiative and those which fall to the institutions alone.” And in its 28 November 2023 message to the Bahá’ís of the World, surveying a century of the Administrative Order, it described the same discernment as an ongoing practice: the friends “attempt to differentiate those areas of activity in which the individual can best exercise initiative from those which fall to the institutions alone, and with heart and soul they welcome the guidance and direction of their institutions.”

Attempt to differentiate. The verb is doing real work. The line is not published as a schedule; it is learned, case by case, by a community that keeps acting.

Where the power actually sits

If the boundary is learned, the underlying anthropology is not. It is stated flatly, and it is the reason initiative matters so much in this Faith and so little in most religious systems.

The 19 May 1994 letter is the sharpest statement of it. Having distinguished authority from power, the House of Justice continues:

“The power of action in the believers is unlocked at the level of individual initiative and surges at the level of collective volition… Individual initiative is a pre-eminent aspect of this power; it is therefore a major responsibility of the institutions to safeguard and stimulate it.” — The Universal House of Justice, 19 May 1994 (The National Spiritual Assembly)

Read that as an instruction to institutions and it changes the picture entirely. An Assembly that suppresses initiative is not being cautious; it is failing a duty. The same letter refuses the opposite error in the same breath: “individuals must not be abandoned entirely to their own devices with respect to the welfare of society as a whole, neither should they be stifled by the assumption of a dictatorial posture by members of the institutions.” Two failure modes, named together, in one sentence.

The appointed arm carries the same charge more explicitly than the elected one. The Institution of the Counsellors, the House of Justice’s 2001 document defining that arm’s functions, states: “The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally is divinely conferred on elected institutions. The power to act, however, resides primarily in the entire body of the believers.” And then, in the section on the Auxiliary Boards: “Stimulating individual initiative is one of the paramount duties of the Auxiliary Board members.” If a believer’s initiative has never been stimulated by anyone in the appointed arm, something in the system is not working as designed — and the believer should notice that the remedy is not to stop initiating.

The Guardian had built this into the machinery from the start. In a letter of 18 October 1927, listing the duties of elected representatives, he told National Assemblies to “invite discussion, provide information, ventilate grievances, welcome advice from even the most humble and insignificant member of the Bahá’í Family, expose their motives, set forth their plans, justify their actions, revise if necessary their verdict, foster the spirit of individual initiative and enterprise, and fortify the sense of interdependence and co-partnership.” Fostering initiative appears in a list of institutional duties, between justifying one’s actions and revising one’s verdict. Three years earlier, on 23 February 1924, he had gone further and located the right at the foundation:

“Let us also remember that at the very root of the Cause lies the principle of the undoubted right of the individual to self-expression, his freedom to declare his conscience and set forth his views.” — Shoghi Effendi, 23 February 1924 (Bahá’í Administration, pp. 63–64)

The same letter supplies the sentence that should be read beside every discussion of obedience in the Faith: “the keynote of the Cause of God is not dictatorial authority but humble fellowship, not arbitrary power, but the spirit of frank and loving consultation.”

Finally, the constitutional layer. When the House of Justice was constituted in 1963, its powers and duties were codified in a document that lists, among the functions inherited from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Will and Testament: “to safeguard the personal rights, freedom and initiative of individuals; and to give attention to the preservation of human honour.” The House of Justice drew out the implication in its 29 December 1988 statement Individual Rights and Freedoms in the World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, arguing that the relationship between society and the individual “must allow ‘free scope’ for ‘individuality to assert itself’ through modes of spontaneity, initiative and diversity that ensure the viability of society.” That statement’s most useful sentence for a believer wondering how much room he has is this one: “in no other system do individuals exercise such a breadth of freedom.” It is written about the electoral process, and it generalises.

The one thing no institution can do for you

The Plan’s dependence on the individual is not a matter of manpower. It is a matter of what kinds of act exist.

The Riḍván 1996 message launching the Four Year Plan — the message that re-founded Bahá’í teaching as a systematic capacity rather than a campaign — pauses before all of that to say what the individual is:

“Bahá’u’lláh’s command to each believer to teach His Faith confers an inescapable responsibility which cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution of the Cause. The individual alone can exercise those capacities which include the ability to take initiative, to seize opportunities, to form friendships, to interact personally with others, to build relationships, to win the cooperation of others in common service to the Faith and society, and to convert into action the decisions made by consultative bodies.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 1996 (Turning Point)

Every item on that list is a thing a body cannot do. An Assembly cannot form a friendship. A cluster agency cannot be present at the moment a colleague says something that opens a conversation about the purpose of life. And the last clause is the one most often missed: to convert into action the decisions made by consultative bodies. Institutional decisions are not self-executing. A plan adopted at a reflection meeting exists as a plan until a particular person walks to a particular door.

Behind this sits the law itself. Bahá’u’lláh writes in Gleanings: “God hath prescribed unto every one the duty of teaching His Cause. Whoever ariseth to discharge this duty, must needs, ere he proclaimeth His Message, adorn himself with the ornament of an upright and praiseworthy character.” Shoghi Effendi read the universality out of that verse without hedging: teaching “should at no time be regarded as the exclusive concern or sole privilege of Bahá’í administrative institutions, be they Assemblies, or committees. All must participate, however humble their origin, however limited their experience, however restricted their means, however deficient their education, however pressing their cares and preoccupations, however unfavorable the environment in which they live.”

And where the individual’s confidence fails, the guidance treats it as a defect to be cured rather than a temperament to be respected. Riḍván 1996 quotes a letter written on the Guardian’s behalf about believers paralysed by a “feeling of inferiority”: “It is precisely these weaknesses that he wishes the friends to overcome, for these do not only paralyse their efforts but actually serve to quench the flame of faith in their hearts… Everyone is a potential teacher. He has only to use what God has given him and thus prove that he is faithful to his trust.”

The personal plan is the Plan’s smallest unit

Two years into the Four Year Plan, the House of Justice made an unusual admission. Reviewing progress in its Riḍván 1998 message, it observed that the institutions had absorbed the Plan’s method but the individuals had not: “The facts at hand confirm that the Four Year Plan works where a systematic approach is understood and applied. These same facts show that the institutions of the Faith, in their collaborative efforts at national, regional, and local levels, have clearly been adhering to this understanding. However, with individuals, on whom rests the ultimate success of the Plan, this understanding is less clear.”

What followed is, so far as the corpus goes, the closest thing to a definition of a personal plan:

“As translated into programs and projects by national and local institutions, the Plan, among other things, gives direction, identifies goals, stimulates effort, provides a variety of needed facilities and materials to benefit the work of teachers and administrators. This is of course necessary for the proper functioning of the community, but is of no consequence unless its individual members respond through active participation. In so responding, each individual, too, must make a conscious decision as to what he or she will do to serve the Plan, and as to how, where and when to do it. This determination enables the individual to check the progress of his actions and, if necessary, to modify the steps being taken.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 1998 (Turning Point)

Four components, and they are worth naming because friends routinely build something far more elaborate and then abandon it. What — a specific act of service, not an aspiration. How, where, when — the conditions under which it will actually happen. A check — some means of noticing whether it is happening. A modification — the expectation that the first version will be wrong. That is the whole apparatus. The message adds the reason it matters personally rather than only institutionally: “Becoming accustomed to such a procedure of systematic striving lends meaning and fulfillment to the life of any Bahá’í.”

Note what is not prescribed: any particular act, any quota, any form, any reporting line. The content is left to the believer, and the Guardian’s own account of how to choose it is expansive to the point of exuberance. In The Advent of Divine Justice he asks the teacher to “carefully consider every avenue of approach which he might utilize”, to “survey the possibilities which the particular circumstances in which he lives offer him, evaluate their advantages, and proceed intelligently and systematically to utilize them” — and then lists clubs, exhibitions, societies, lectures on temperance and social welfare and comparative religion, and participation in “social, cultural, humanitarian, charitable, and educational organizations and enterprises.” This is not a script. It is an instruction to survey your own life for openings that nobody else can see, because nobody else is standing where you are.

The personal plan then meets the collective one at a specific joint: the three-month cycle. Expansion, consolidation, reflection — the rhythm by which a cluster plans, acts and learns (three-month-cycle-tempo). A believer’s decision about what to do next is made inside that rhythm, informed by the reflection meeting’s reading of the local reality, and reported back into it. The relationship is genuinely two-way: the House of Justice’s 27 December 2005 message observes that in robust clusters the friends have learned “to weave their individual initiatives and collective endeavours into an effective pattern of unified action.” Weaving, not subordinating. The cluster plan is calibrated to the initiative that actually exists, which is why an under-reported act of service distorts planning as surely as an unperformed one.

Riḍván 1998 closes on the note that ought to end any deliberation about whether one is ready: “To ensure a soul-satisfying answer, one thing above all else is necessary: to act, to act now, and to continue to act.”

A framework, not a formula

The word the House of Justice chose for the Plan’s shape — framework — was chosen against alternatives, and the guidance has defended the choice repeatedly and against a specific misreading.

The misreading is that systematic action means uniform action. Riḍván 1998 blocked it in the sentence that introduced systematisation to the Bahá’í vocabulary: “Systematization ensures consistency of lines of action based on well-conceived plans… While allowing for individual initiative and spontaneity, it suggests the need to be clear-headed, methodical, efficient, constant, balanced and harmonious.” Initiative and spontaneity are named as things systematisation must accommodate, not overcome.

By Riḍván 2008 the House of Justice could report on the experiment’s result:

“What we continue to find encouraging is how well disciplined is this individual initiative. Communities everywhere are gradually internalizing the lessons being learned from systematization, and the framework defined by the current series of Plans lends consistency and flexibility to the endeavours of the friends. Far from restricting them, this framework enables them to seize opportunities, to build relationships, and to translate into reality a vision of systematic growth. In a word, it gives shape to their collective powers.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2008 (muhj-2001-2022)

Gives shape to is a claim about form, not limit. A believer with no framework does not have more freedom; he has less traction, because his acts do not compound with anyone else’s.

The most precise statement came in the Riḍván 2021 message, looking back over the twenty-five years since the institute process began:

“As the efforts of the friends intensified, various principles, concepts and strategies of universal relevance to the growth process began to crystallize into a framework for action that could evolve to accommodate new elements. This framework proved fundamental to the release of tremendous vitality… But a framework is not a formula. By taking into account the various elements of the framework when assessing the reality of a cluster, a locality, or simply a neighbourhood, a pattern of activity could be developed that drew on what the rest of the Bahá’í world was learning while still being a response to the particulars of that place. A dichotomy between rigid requirements on the one hand and limitless personal preferences on the other gave way to a more nuanced understanding of the variety of means by which individuals could support a process that, at its heart, was coherent.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

That last sentence names both errors and dismisses both. “Rigid requirements” and “limitless personal preferences” are not the two ends of a spectrum on which the community should find a comfortable midpoint; they are the dichotomy that was superseded. What replaced it is a shared set of elements applied to a particular place by people who live there.

The most recent guidance keeps hammering the same nail. Writing to the Counsellors on 31 December 2025 about clusters where notable progress had occurred, the House of Justice identified as the essential factor “the capacity of the friends at the grassroots to learn together, combining insights gained from their own experience with those of advanced communities nearby or further afield, without any sense of applying a fixed formula.” And it stated the underlying claim outright: “Although the fundamental features of a programme of growth—its aim, its guiding principles, and its basic instruments—are the same everywhere, growth is an organic process, not a mechanical one.”

One further guard is worth knowing, because friends erect fences the guidance never built. When the House of Justice’s Secretariat set out the now-familiar three areas of endeavour — expansion and consolidation, social action, participation in the discourses of society — it added an explicit caution: the scheme “should be regarded as merely one way of conceptualizing the work of the Bahá’í community… Nor should it assume the status of a definition, as reflected in statements such as ‘There are three areas of Bahá’í activity.’ Further, in no way should the friends feel there is a division of labor, in which one group participates in the work of expansion and consolidation, and another group in each of the other two areas.” The framework’s own authors warn against reading the framework as a taxonomy of permitted roles.

Where the line actually falls, in practice

Principles are cheap until they decide cases. Here is the operative rule, followed by the cases that test it.

Act without asking in the domain of your own service and relationships. Speak with a colleague about the purpose of life. Invite neighbours to pray in your home. Offer to tutor a study circle or animate a junior youth group. Visit a friend who has stopped coming to Feast. Memorise a passage, change a habit, move to a cluster that needs help (homefront-pioneer-first-year). Bring a talent — legal, medical, agricultural, technical — to bear on a local need. None of these requires a decision from anyone. In every one of them the Guardian’s sentence applies without qualification.

Consult before acting when the act touches the Faith’s collective interests. Four families of case recur.

Speaking in the Faith’s name. The clearest precedent in the whole corpus is a small administrative act of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s. Shaykh Faraju’lláh, a Kurdish believer from Cairo, produced an Arabic translation of the Ishráqát; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá corrected it in His own handwriting — and then directed that it still be submitted to the Spiritual Assembly of Egypt before publication. His stated reason is the whole doctrine in a sentence: “This is so that things may be arranged in an orderly manner, for should it not be so any one may translate a certain Tablet and print and circulate it on his own account.” The modern descendant is the practice of review. Its purpose, the House of Justice has explained, “is to protect the Faith against misrepresentation by its own followers at this early stage of its existence” — a purpose deliberately narrow: the reviewer checks the accuracy of the presentation of the Faith, not the literary merit, and Bahá’í authors are free to quote the Sacred Text without restriction. Works that merely mention the Faith in passing need no review at all.

Publishing about the community. The Secretariat’s guidance on personal websites shows how the boundary is drawn in a medium where anyone can look official. The friends “are generally free to determine what aspects of the Teachings they wish to touch upon” but should “clearly distinguish their sites from those created by the institutions”, avoid “a tone that could be perceived as authoritative or instructional”, and “steer away from areas that lie exclusively within the sphere of institutional responsibility, such as offering a comprehensive collection of the writings and of the messages of the House of Justice or an exhaustive calendar of Bahá’í events across many countries.” The stated aim: “not to unduly restrict their efforts but to avoid confusion and misinformation.” A believer building something for other believers should ask a single question — could a stranger mistake this for the institution speaking? — and design so that the answer is no.

Social action beyond the modest scale. Grassroots initiative is not merely permitted here, it is the expected origin: social action “can range from fairly informal efforts of limited duration undertaken by individuals or small groups of friends to programmes of social and economic development with a high level of complexity.” But two hazards trigger consultation. The first is money from outside: experience has shown “that the right of the local community to trace its own path of progress can be compromised when those providing resources from outside of the community assume responsibility for the management and direction of an initiative.” The second is subtler and worth quoting because it is so easily missed by exactly the people most eager to help: “The effective use of the talents of individuals with particular expertise also demands vigilance in ensuring that the initiative of some, usually those with access to more resources, does not end up suffocating the initiatives of others.” A well-resourced believer’s initiative can be a form of crowding out. The Local Assembly’s role, the House of Justice has written, is to help the friends steer past “the allurements of overly ambitious projects that would consume energies and ultimately prove untenable”.

Representing the community’s experience publicly. Participation in the discourses of society is open to every believer, in the many “social spaces in which thinking and policies evolve.” The caution attached is specific: friends should “avoid overstating the Bahá’í experience and drawing attention to fledging efforts of the Bahá’í community which are best left to come to maturity without interference, such as the junior youth spiritual empowerment program.” Enthusiasm about a young programme, broadcast too widely, can damage it.

And when the decision goes against you, the framework provides an answer that is neither submission nor exit. The 29 December 1988 statement sets out the reciprocal rights precisely: the Assembly “has the responsibility to guide, direct and decide on community affairs and the right to be obeyed and supported by members of the community. The individual has the responsibility to establish and maintain the Assembly through election, the offering of advice, moral support and material assistance; and he has the right to be heard by it, to receive its guidance and assistance, and to appeal from any Assembly decision which he conscientiously feels is unjust or detrimental to the interests of the community.” Obedience and appeal are held together, and neither cancels the other (independent-investigation-and-obedience).

Initiative the institutions later systematised

The strongest evidence that this system is designed to metabolise individual initiative is historical. Several of the Faith’s most consequential institutions began as something one person, or one small group, simply decided to do.

Green Acre. In 1894 Sarah Farmer established summer conferences at Green Acre, on the banks of the Piscataqua in Maine, as “a center for the promotion of unity and fellowship between races and religions.” She was not yet a Bahá’í. After her pilgrimage to ‘Akká in 1900 she placed the facilities “at the disposal of the followers of the Faith which she had herself recently embraced.” In 1912 ‘Abdu’l-Bahá blessed the Open Forum there in person. Green Acre was formally constituted as a Bahá’í summer school in 1929, joining Geyserville in the Californian hills (1927) and Louhelen Ranch near Davison, Michigan (1931), and was later supplemented by the International School at Pine Valley, Colorado Springs. Shoghi Effendi described these “three embryonic Bahá’í educational institutions” as having “set so noble an example as to inspire other Bahá’í communities in Persia, in the British Isles, in Germany, in Australia, in New Zealand, in India, in ‘Iráq and in Egypt” to build along the same lines. A private initiative of the 1890s became, within four decades, a global institutional form.

Martha Root. The Tablets of the Divine Plan were unveiled at the first American Convention after the First World War. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá had named continents, states, islands and languages, and had written a sentence that contains both modes of response in a single clause — “Select ye important personages, or else they by themselves, becoming severed from rest and composure of the world, may arise and travel.” Institutional dispatch, or self-dispatch: the Charter of Bahá’í teaching authorises both. Martha Root took the second route. She “embarked on the first of her historic journeys which were to extend over a period of twenty years, and to carry her several times around the globe, and which ended only with her death far from home.” On 30 January 1926 she was received by Queen Marie of Romania at Cotroceni Palace in Bucharest; the Queen’s resulting testimonies appeared “in nearly two hundred newspapers of the United States and Canada” and were subsequently republished across Europe, Asia and Australia. Shoghi Effendi later called Root “that archetype of Bahá’í itinerant teachers.” No committee assigned her.

Hyde Dunn and Agnes Alexander. In the same wave, Hyde Dunn, “at the advanced age of sixty-two, promptly forsook his home in California” and settled in Australia, where he carried the Message “to no less than seven hundred towns.” Agnes Alexander is named by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in the Tablets themselves: “Miss Agnes Alexander… traveled alone to the Hawaiian Islands, to the Island of Honolulu, and now she is gaining spiritual victories in Japan!” The Master’s comment on what such a life can accomplish is the theological warrant for the whole pattern: “It has often happened that one blessed soul has become the cause of the guidance of a nation.”

The Ruhi Institute. The most consequential case is recent, and its documentation is unusually complete (ruhi-institute-founding-and-globalisation). Following a period of mass teaching in Colombia, the Colombian community faced a consolidation crisis: new believers in villages the travelling teachers could not keep visiting. Two sister institutions emerged from the same diagnosis in the same region. FUNDAEC was co-founded in 1974 in the Cali–Norte del Cauca region; the Ruhi Institute followed in 1976 in Puerto Tejada, directed from the outset by Laurie Arbab, who served until 1988. By 1985 nine courses existed in Spanish, English and French. The materials spread by adoption, not by directive — Trinidad and Tobago in October 1982, Panama in February 1983, the Central African Republic in April 1984 — and the first English editions of what became Books 1–4 appeared between 1994 and 1996.

What the institutions did was wait, then choose. The 26 December 1995 message launching the Four Year Plan called for permanent training institutes but deliberately declined to prescribe a curriculum. The House of Justice explained the reasoning a decade later, on 28 December 2005: “we were aware that certain elements of a curriculum meeting the necessary requirements existed in the materials of the Ruhi Institute. It was our conviction, however, that the accumulated experience at that point did not justify our recommending a specific set of materials.” National Assemblies were encouraged to develop their own. Then the field decided: “By the time the Four Year Plan came to a close, it was all too apparent that national communities which had vigorously set out to implement the sequence of courses designed by the Ruhi Institute were far ahead of those who had attempted to develop their own program.” Only then did the House of Justice conclude “that the books of the Ruhi Institute should constitute the main sequence of courses for institutes everywhere.”

That letter also shows the cost of systematisation, honestly stated. Designating one curriculum “is not to ignore the variety of needs and interests of the friends”, and the institutions “will continue to respect the wishes of those who, for whatever reason, do not feel inclined to participate in the study of the books of the Ruhi Institute” — who “should recognize that there are many avenues of service open to them, including, above all, individual teaching which is the paramount duty of every Bahá’í.” The request made of them is modest and precise: “that they not allow their personal preferences to hamper in any way the unfoldment of an educational process that has shown the potential to embrace millions of souls.” Adoption of one initiative necessarily displaces others; the guidance neither pretends otherwise nor apologises for it.

And the pattern is still running. In its 31 December 2025 message the House of Justice described the newest instance: groups of friends — women or youth, farmers or educators, animators or children’s class teachers — “set about organizing their own efforts to improve some aspect of the life of the community” and do so “without the need for new layers of administrative structure.” It calls this “the emerging capacity of the community to be a visible protagonist in the Plan”, and reports that “in the last four years, the number of community-based initiatives emerging out of the activities of the Plan has increased considerably.” The Bahá’í International Development Organization is now studying the conditions under which such initiatives appear, “including how, in time, some of these initiatives develop into community-based organizations.” That is the Green Acre sequence and the Ruhi sequence, observed live, with an institution assigned to learn from it.

The shape is consistent enough to state as a rule. Individual or local initiative → sustained practice in a real place → demonstrated performance across several places → institutional recognition → generalisation into the framework. The institutions do not commission the innovation. They notice it, test it against wider experience, and then carry it further than its originators could.

The two ways this goes wrong

The 28 December 2010 message opens its treatment of the three protagonists with a description of history rather than doctrine, and it is the most useful diagnostic in the corpus:

“Throughout human history, interactions among these three have been fraught with difficulties at every turn, with the individual clamouring for freedom, the institution demanding submission, and the community claiming precedence… The worldwide crisis of authority provides proof enough. So grievous have been its abuses, and so deep the suspicion and resentment it now arouses, that the world is becoming increasingly ungovernable—a situation made all the more perilous by the weakening of community ties.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

Both classic distortions are named there, and both show up in Bahá’í communities in muted form.

The institution demanding submission. This is the failure the guidance guards against most explicitly, presumably because religious history supplies so many examples. The Guardian’s remedies are structural: institutions must “purge once for all their deliberations… from that air of self-contained aloofness, from the suspicion of secrecy, the stifling atmosphere of dictatorial assertiveness”; their “function is not to dictate, but to consult, and consult not only among themselves, but as much as possible with the friends whom they represent”; they should “never be led to suppose that they are the central ornaments of the body of the Cause.” The House of Justice reinforced this in 2010 by recalling the Guardian’s warning that the administrative machinery “is to be regarded as a means, and not an end in itself”, and adding a caution aimed squarely at institutional members: service on an institution does not “provide an opportunity to promote one’s own understanding of what is recorded in the Sacred Text and how the teachings should be applied, steering the community in whatever direction personal preferences dictate.” Writing on the House of Justice’s behalf in 2013, the Secretariat put the pastoral version plainly: institutions’ “is not the duty to pry into personal lives or to impose Bahá’í law on the individual but to create an environment in which the friends eagerly arise.”

The individual clamouring for freedom. The Bahá’í form of this is rarely defiance. It is more often energy that never compounds — the believer whose service is real but sits outside the pattern, unshared and unlearned-from, so that when he stops, nothing remains. The 19 May 1994 letter states the limit without softening it: “Even though individuals may strive to be guided in their actions by their personal understanding of the Divine Texts, and much can be accomplished thereby, such actions, untempered by the overall direction provided by authorized institutions, are incapable of attaining the thrust necessary for the unencumbered advancement of civilization.” Much can be accomplished thereby is a genuine concession; incapable of attaining the thrust is a genuine limit.

There is also a counterfeit of initiative worth naming, because it is common among capable and well-read believers: criticism experienced as contribution. The Riḍván 2026 message, describing youth serving under conditions of “conflict and disorder, economic inequity, and deep social divisions”, commends them “for resisting the impulse to criticize and condemn, finding instead constructive ways to steer around these imposing obstacles and work towards ultimately overcoming them.” Diagnosing what the institutions are doing wrong is not an act of initiative, however accurate the diagnosis. Initiative produces something that exists tomorrow which did not exist today.

The third protagonist is the corrective for both. The 30 December 2021 message states the interdependence in one line: each of the three “is incapable of manifesting its full potential on its own. It is by strengthening their dynamic relationships with one another that their powers are combined and multiplied.” The failure modes of a cluster where one protagonist is weak are worked out in detail in three-protagonists-interaction-and-failure-modes.

What the wider evidence says about frameworks and initiative

The Writings settle the principle; they cannot describe what is known about how frameworks affect human motivation in general. Two bodies of external work are worth weighing here strictly as description of a field — never as authority for a Bahá’í principle, and never as the reason to do what the guidance already asks.

The first is the research programme around self-determination theory, which distinguishes environments that support autonomy from those that control it. A meta-analysis by Slemp, Kern, Patrick and Ryan, published in Motivation and Emotion in 2018, pooled 754 correlations from 72 studies (83 samples, N = 32,870) and found that leader autonomy support — offering meaningful rationales, acknowledging others’ perspectives, providing choice — correlated strongly with autonomous motivation, well-being and positive work behaviours, and was negatively associated with distress. The finding that matters for institutions is the asymmetry: autonomy support predicted autonomous motivation and was essentially unrelated to controlled motivation. Directive pressure does not simply produce a different flavour of the same engagement; it produces a different and less durable kind. This does not validate the Bahá’í arrangement, which rests on the Covenant and not on motivation research. It does suggest that the Guardian’s insistence that institutions “foster the spirit of individual initiative and enterprise” is asking for something with observable consequences.

The second is Elinor Ostrom’s work on the governance of shared resources, for which she received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009. Governing the Commons (1990) set out eight design principles distilled from long-enduring self-governing institutions. Two are directly analogous to the arrangement described in this article. The principle of collective-choice arrangements holds that most individuals affected by the operating rules should be able to participate in modifying them — the empirical cousin of the Guardian’s instruction that Assemblies “invite discussion… welcome advice from even the most humble and insignificant member” and “revise if necessary their verdict.” The principle of nested enterprises holds that governance activities in large systems should be organised in multiple layers rather than concentrated at the top — which is what the House of Justice described in operational terms when it told National Assemblies in 1996 that the administration of teaching “calls for a decentralized mode of management” because the necessary closeness of attention “is not possible from the top, whatever mechanisms may be set in place at the National Center.”

The convergence is interesting and it is also limited. Ostrom studied irrigation systems, fisheries and forests, where the resource is finite and the incentive problem is overuse. A Bahá’í cluster is not a common-pool resource, and the Faith’s institutions derive their authority from a Covenant rather than from the consent of those they serve. The parallel is worth one paragraph and no more.

Taking a position: what to do on Monday

The recommendation follows the structure of the guidance rather than the anxieties of the conscientious.

Assume permission in your own domain, and stop auditioning. If the act is teaching, service, friendship, hospitality, study or personal transformation, the Advent sentence governs and nobody is going to send you a directive. The most common self-imposed obstacle is the belief that one must first be invited, trained further, or noticed. Riḍván 1996 answers all three at once: everyone is a potential teacher; he has only to use what God has given him.

Write down four things, not a system. What you will do, and how, where and when. Check it monthly against reality; change it when it is wrong. That is what the Riḍván 1998 message asks and it is the whole of what it asks. A personal plan that requires a spreadsheet has already exceeded its specification; a personal plan that survives a bad month is a plan.

Ask one question before acting: does this touch the collective interests of the Faith? If the act speaks in the community’s name, spends its funds, represents it to outsiders, publishes about it, or commits others to a course, consult — an Assembly, a coordinator, an Auxiliary Board member — before, not after. If it does not, act. When genuinely unsure, the cost asymmetry favours a short conversation: consultation about a private act wastes twenty minutes, while unilateral action in the collective domain can cost the community years.

Connect the act to the cycle. An unshared act of service is not a smaller contribution than a shared one; it is a contribution the community cannot learn from. Bring it to the reflection meeting. Let it be counted. This is the mechanism by which individual initiative becomes, in the Riḍván 2008 phrase, “shape to their collective powers.”

If you build software, data tools or AI systems, the leverage is real and the discipline is specific. Three rules follow directly from the guidance. First, design so that no one can mistake your work for the institution’s — in name, tone and framing, per the Secretariat’s website guidance; avoid an “authoritative or instructional” register and steer clear of what belongs to the institutions alone, such as comprehensive archives of the Writings and House of Justice messages, or authoritative global event calendars. Second, do not build parallel statistics. Cluster and community data are the raw material of institutional planning; a well-meaning independent dashboard fragments the very learning it appears to serve. Build tools that help friends contribute to the institutional record, not alternatives to it. Third, build for the individual’s own practice, where the corpus explicitly leaves form to the believer — study aids, memorisation, prayer, source lookup, translation support, accessibility for friends with disabilities, offline tools for low-connectivity clusters. And note the failure mode that the social-action guidance names for the well-resourced: an elegant tool imposed from outside can suffocate the simpler thing a community would have built itself. Ask first what the friends in one neighbourhood actually need, and expect the answer to be smaller than what you wanted to build.

Do not mistake analysis for initiative. The clearest test of whether an act is initiative is whether something exists afterwards that did not exist before.

And when a decision goes against you, obey it and appeal it. Both. The 1988 statement grants the right of appeal in the same sentence as the duty of obedience, and a believer who exercises one while refusing the other has misread the system in a way the Guardian anticipated a century ago: the keynote “is not dictatorial authority but humble fellowship.”

Open questions / honest limits

  • The boundary is described but never enumerated. No authoritative text lists which activities require consultation. The corpus supplies a criterion — “activities which affect the collective interests of the Faith” — and the House of Justice speaks of a capacity to “discern” and “attempt to differentiate”. This page has assembled cases from the guidance; it has not produced a rule that decides new cases mechanically, because the sources decline to.
  • “Personal teaching plan” is community vocabulary, not corpus vocabulary. The phrase does not appear in the authoritative texts consulted for this page. What does appear is the Riḍván 1998 requirement that each individual “make a conscious decision as to what he or she will do to serve the Plan, and as to how, where and when to do it.” This page treats that sentence as the substance of the idea and does not attribute the phrase itself to any authoritative source.
  • The Guardian’s “let him not wait” was written to a specific community about a specific Plan. The Advent of Divine Justice addresses the American believers regarding the Seven Year Plan of 1937–1944. The House of Justice has quoted the passage as generally applicable — most directly in Riḍván 1998 — which is the ground on which this page generalises it. A reader should know the original occasion.
  • How institutions decide which initiatives to systematise is not documented. The Ruhi case is unusually well recorded because the House of Justice explained its own reasoning in the 28 December 2005 letter. Green Acre’s institutionalisation, by contrast, is known chiefly from the outcome. Whether there is a general institutional method for recognising a promising initiative — and how many initiatives were not adopted — this page cannot say.
  • This page does not treat consultation itself. Its method, its epistemology and its conduct belong to consultation; only the trigger for consultation is treated here.
  • Nor does it treat institutional authority in depth. The nature and limits of that authority, the division between interpretation and legislation, and the architecture of elected and appointed arms belong to bahai-authority-framework and bahai-institutions-architecture.
  • The external research is descriptive and will age. The 2018 meta-analysis is correlational, drawn largely from workplace settings that differ from voluntary religious service in motivation and structure; Ostrom’s design principles were derived from resource-governance cases that differ from a Bahá’í community in kind. Neither grounds a Bahá’í principle and neither should be cited as if it did.
  • Figures on current community-based initiatives are not public. The 31 December 2025 message reports that the number “has increased considerably” over four years without giving a count. This page reproduces that formulation rather than estimating.
