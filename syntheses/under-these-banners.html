Under These Banners: the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ, the Arrival at ‘Akká, and the Spread of the Faith

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page covers

In Adrianople, before He was sent to ‘Akká, Bahá’u’lláh wrote a sentence describing His arrival there. The sentence says the opposite of what an observer on the quay saw on 31 August 1868. This page sets the two side by side.

  1. The sentence itself, in its exact wording, and every place in the corpus where it is quoted.
  2. The Tablet it comes from, the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ: who received it, where it was revealed, and what else it says.
  3. The arrival at ‘Akká on 31 August 1868, as Shoghi Effendi and Bahá’u’lláh describe it, with Adib Taherzadeh’s historical narrative added where it supplies detail.
  4. What Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice say about the spread of the Faith, and what official sources currently report.

Quotations from the Writings, Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice are copied from the texts held in this knowledge base. Adib Taherzadeh’s Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, Hasan Balyuzi’s Bahá’u’lláh: The King of Glory and the Bahá’í News items are historical or secondary sources, and are labelled as such wherever they are used. Taherzadeh’s books and the Bahá’í News files use straight apostrophes; they are normalised here to the standard transliteration.

The sentence

“Upon Our arrival,” that Tablet had predicted, “We were welcomed with banners of light, whereupon the Voice of the Spirit cried out saying: ‘Soon will all that dwell on earth be enlisted under these banners.’” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI, quoting the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ ([source], line 791)

The same words, without Shoghi Effendi’s framing, are printed in Bahá’í Sacred Writings, a compilation of the Universal House of Justice, in the section on the coming of Bahá’u’lláh:

“Upon Our arrival, We were welcomed with banners of light, whereupon the Voice of the Spirit cried out saying: ‘Soon will all that dwell on earth be enlisted under these banners.’” — Bahá’í Sacred Writings, “The Coming of Bahá’u’lláh”, no. 27 ([source], line 560)

That compilation prints the passage without naming the Tablet. The attribution to the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ rests on Shoghi Effendi (God Passes By) and on Taherzadeh, who gives it as a quotation from the Tablet of Sayyáḥ.

Two small points about the wording. The sentence has three parts: a welcome (“banners of light”), a speaker (“the Voice of the Spirit”) and a prediction (“Soon will all that dwell on earth be enlisted under these banners”). And the sentence is in the past tense (“We were welcomed”), although it was written before the arrival. Taherzadeh calls it a description “in allegorical terms” of the arrival, and says Bahá’u’lláh “by allusion, foretells His exile to the city of Akka” in this Tablet.

Shoghi Effendi’s sentence introducing it:

“Bahá’u’lláh Himself, as attested by Nabíl in his narrative, had, as far back as the first years of His banishment to Adrianople, alluded to that same city in His Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ, designating it as the ‘Vale of Nabíl,’ the word Nabíl being equal in numerical value to that of ‘Akká.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI ([source], line 791)

The 1972 report is the only place found where the sentence is described as sung:

“Mr. Van Gilmer led the singing of Bahá’u’lláh’s words, ‘Soon will all that dwell on earth be enlisted under these banners.‘ Surely the faces of the many new believers present, from both South Carolina and other states were a testimony to those words, and the evening session closed with the friends eager to greet their new Bahá’í brothers and sisters.” — Bahá’í News no. 501, December 1972 (a periodical report, not an authoritative text)

The report does not say what tune was used.

The Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ

Recipient and place

The Tablet is named for Mírzá ‘Alíy-i-Sayyáḥ. Taherzadeh (historical narrative) gives these facts about him:

“One of the Tablets of this period is the Lawh-i-Sayyah revealed in honour of Mulla Adi-Guzal, otherwise known as Mirza Aliy-i-Sayyah. The title ‘Sayyah’ (Traveller) was given to him by the Báb. He was a native of Maraghih, and had received his education as a mulla in that town.” — Adib Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2, ch. 9 (`sources/books/The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, Vol. 2 – Adib Taherzadeh.md:7282`)

According to Taherzadeh, Sayyáḥ served the Báb as a messenger while the Báb was imprisoned at Máh-Kú and Chihríq. He carried the Báb’s Tablets and letters between Persia and the Báb. Balyuzi (The King of Glory) adds that Sayyáḥ, as courier for the Báb, visited the house of Bahá’u’lláh in Ṭihrán. Taherzadeh says he came to Adrianople in the early part of 1284 A.H. (1867), told the believers how the Báb’s promise that he would meet “Him Whom God shall make manifest” had been fulfilled, and after about three months was sent by Bahá’u’lláh, with Mishkín-Qalam and Jamshíd-i-Gurjí, to Constantinople on a mission. In Constantinople the three were arrested. They were later exiled, with Mírzá Yaḥyá’s party, to Cyprus. (Taherzadeh, vol. 2, `…Vol. 2…md:7433`, and `…Vol. 2…md:11156`; Balyuzi, `sources/bahairesearch/english/Baha’i/Baha’i_Studies/H.M._Balyuzi/Bahá’u’lláh_The_King_of_Glory.md:5184`.)

Date

The sources place the Tablet in Adrianople (1863–68) but do not agree on a precise date, and none gives one.

  • Shoghi Effendi lists the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ among the Tablets Bahá’u’lláh “had already set down when He transferred His residence to the house of ‘Izzat Áqá” in Adrianople, that is, early in the stay and before the Most Great Separation from Mírzá Yaḥyá (`god-passes-by.md:719`). He says Bahá’u’lláh alluded to ‘Akká “as far back as the first years of His banishment to Adrianople”.
  • Taherzadeh places it among the Tablets of the period when Bahá’u’lláh lived in the house of Riḍá Big and then the house of Amru’lláh, (vol. 2, ch. 9). He separately says Sayyáḥ reached Adrianople in the early part of 1284 A.H. (1867), and does not say whether the Tablet was written before or after that visit.
  • Taherzadeh’s vol. 3 says the Tablet foreshadowed the arrival “a few years before” 1868.

This page therefore says only: revealed in Adrianople, during the first years of that exile according to Shoghi Effendi.

What else the Tablet contains

The full text of the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ is not in the corpus. The only material is Taherzadeh’s summary (historical narrative, not authoritative text) and the quoted sentence. According to Taherzadeh:

“In the Tablet of Sayyah Bahá’u’lláh unveils the glory of His station, states that He is the Ancient Beauty through Whose command the whole of creation has come into being, affirms that mankind turns to Him in adoration and clings to the hem of His bounty even though it is unable to recognize Him in His wondrous Revelation.” — Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2, p. 213 (`…Vol. 2…md:7438`)

He adds that the Tablet refers to followers of the Bayán who rejected the Cause, says that “the days of tests have come and that the balance has been established”, and tells people that if they wish to behold the Face of God they cannot do so unless they “cleanse their hearts of all idle fancy and detach themselves from this world and all that is therein.” He also says the Tablet names pride and attachment to self as the reason some of the most devout were “shut out as by a veil”.

Two things follow for reading the sentence about the banners. It sits in a Tablet that is mostly about a Day of testing, and its wording, “the days of tests have come”, matches the next several years in ‘Akká. And Shoghi Effendi’s own introduction of the Tablet in God Passes By (below) uses the sentence as a prediction, not only an image.

The arrival, 31 August 1868

The route

According to Shoghi Effendi, Bahá’u’lláh left Adrianople on 12 August 1868, was taken to Gallipoli, embarked for Alexandria on 21 August, transhipped to a steamer for Haifa, and then crossed to ‘Akká by sailing vessel:

“…where they disembarked, in the course of the afternoon of the 12th of Jamádíyu’l-Avval 1285 A.H. (August 31, 1868).” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. X (`god-passes-by.md:773`)

At Gallipoli, before the sea voyage, He warned His companions:

“…this journey will be unlike any of the previous journeys,” and that whoever did not feel himself “man enough to face the future” had best “depart to whatever place he pleaseth, and be preserved from tests, for hereafter he will find himself unable to leave” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. X, quoting Bahá’u’lláh (`god-passes-by.md:771`)

The companions “unanimously chose to disregard” the warning.

The landing and the crowd

Taherzadeh gives the following account of the landing. It is a historical narrative; Shoghi Effendi’s own account, quoted next, is brief on this point.

“As there were no landing facilities at ‘Akka, the men had to wade ashore from the boat and it was ordered that the women were to be carried on the backs of men. But at ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s insistence the women were carried ashore one by one sitting in a chair which He Himself procured.” — Taherzadeh, vol. 3, ch. 1, p. 12 (`…Vol. 3…md:649`)

About the crowd, Taherzadeh writes:

“Among these people wild rumours and false accusations were circulating concerning Bahá’u’lláh and His followers as they were about to arrive. … It is no wonder, therefore, that great numbers from among the inhabitants of ‘Akka had assembled at the landing site to jeer at them and at their Leader whom they referred to as ‘the God of the Persians’.” — Taherzadeh, vol. 3, ch. 1, p. 12

Shoghi Effendi:

“Having, after a miserable voyage, disembarked at ‘Akká, all the exiles, men, women and children, were, under the eyes of a curious and callous population that had assembled at the port to behold the ‘God of the Persians,’ conducted to the army barracks, where they were locked in, and sentinels detailed to guard them.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:803`)

Taherzadeh adds that some in the crowd saw something else. One old man, Khalíl Aḥmad ‘Abdu, told people he could see signs of greatness in Bahá’u’lláh’s face and that they should be thankful for “the footsteps of this great Personage”. Another onlooker, ‘Abdu’llah Tuzih, was drawn to Him and later became a believer. (Taherzadeh, vol. 3, pp. 12–13.) These are single reports in a historical narrative, but they are the only accounts found of anyone in the crowd who saw otherwise.

The city

Shoghi Effendi describes what the city was:

“‘Akká, the ancient Ptolemais, the St. Jean d’Acre of the Crusaders, that had successfully defied the siege of Napoleon, had sunk, under the Turks, to the level of a penal colony to which murderers, highway robbers and political agitators were consigned from all parts of the Turkish empire. It was girt about by a double system of ramparts; was inhabited by a people whom Bahá’u’lláh stigmatized as ‘the generation of vipers’; was devoid of any source of water within its gates; was flea-infested, damp and honey-combed with gloomy, filthy and tortuous lanes.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:799`)

He quotes Bahá’u’lláh’s own description of it, written in Adrianople before the exile, in the Lawḥ-i-Sulṭán:

“According to what they say, it is the most desolate of the cities of the world, the most unsightly of them in appearance, the most detestable in climate, and the foulest in water. It is as though it were the metropolis of the owl.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Sulṭán, quoted in God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:799`); also in Bahá’í Sacred Writings, “The Coming of Bahá’u’lláh”, no. 26 (`bahai-sacred-writings.md:556`)

In the same Tablet He also wrote of the imprisonment, “Though weariness lay Me low, and hunger consume Me, and the bare rock be My bed, and My fellows the beasts of the field, I will not complain, but will endure patiently…” (`bahai-sacred-writings.md:556`). The Bahá’í Sacred Writings compilation prints, in the next sections, the banners sentence (no. 27) and the statement that follows it (no. 28):

“Know thou that upon Our arrival at this Spot, We chose to designate it as the ‘Most Great Prison’. Though previously subjected in another land (Ṭihrán) to chains and fetters, We yet refused to call it by that name. Say: Ponder thereon, O ye endued with understanding!” — Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:795`), and printed in Bahá’í Sacred Writings, “The Coming of Bahá’u’lláh”, no. 28 (`bahai-sacred-writings.md:562`)

The barracks, the first night and the first weeks

Shoghi Effendi, with Bahá’u’lláh’s words:

“‘The first night,‘ Bahá’u’lláh testifies in the Lawḥ-i-Ra’ís, ‘all were deprived of either food or drink … They even begged for water, and were refused.’ So filthy and brackish was the water in the pool of the courtyard that no one could drink it. Three loaves of black and salty bread were assigned to each, which they were later permitted to exchange, when escorted by guards to the market, for two of better quality. … All fell sick, except two, shortly after their arrival. Malaria, dysentery, combined with the sultry heat, added to their miseries. Three succumbed, among them two brothers, who died the same night, ‘locked,‘ as testified by Bahá’u’lláh, ‘in each other’s arms.’ The carpet used by Him He gave to be sold in order to provide for their winding-sheets and burial.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:803`; abridged where marked by ellipses)

Bahá’u’lláh adds, in words Shoghi Effendi quotes in the same place: “None,” He Himself has written, “knoweth what befell Us, except God, the Almighty, the All-Knowing … From the foundation of the world until the present day a cruelty such as this hath neither been seen nor heard of.”

Taherzadeh supplies detail (historical narrative, drawing on Ishráq Khávarí and the memoirs of Ḥusayn-i-Áshchí):

  • Bahá’u’lláh was first put in a bare, filthy room, and later in an upper room whose floor was covered with earth and whose ceiling plaster was falling. (vol. 3, p. 17)
  • The companions were held in another room whose floor was covered with mud, with ten soldiers at the gate. (ibid.)
  • Bahá’íyyih Khánum fainted on arrival from the stench and heat. (ibid.)
  • Women and children went without water through the first night. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá appealed to the guards and sent a message to the Governor, “but all was without avail”. (ibid.)
  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá lived on the ground floor in a room formerly used as a morgue; “Its moist air affected His health for the rest of His life.” (vol. 3, p. 19)
  • For three months Bahá’u’lláh was not allowed to go to the public bath, and a barber who came to attend to His hair was accompanied by a guard. (ibid.)
  • After the three deaths, Bahá’u’lláh revealed a short healing prayer for the believers in the barracks, and “soon everyone recovered”. (vol. 3, p. 21)

Three days after arrival, Shoghi Effendi says, the Sultán’s farmán of 26 July 1868 was read publicly in the principal mosque “as a warning to the population”. It condemned the exiles to “perpetual banishment”, stipulated “their strict incarceration” and forbade them to associate with each other or with the local inhabitants (`god-passes-by.md:801`). Taherzadeh says that the exiles were introduced as criminals who had corrupted the morals of the people (vol. 3, p. 18).

Bahá’u’lláh describes the whole experience in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf:

“More grievous became Our plight from day to day, nay, from hour to hour, until they took Us forth from Our prison and made Us, with glaring injustice, enter the Most Great Prison. And if anyone ask them: ‘For what crime were they imprisoned?’ they would answer and say: ‘They, verily, sought to supplant the Faith with a new religion!’” — Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf (`sources/reference_library/epistle-son-wolf.md:182`)

The two scenes together

Taherzadeh puts the two scenes in one passage:

“How incomparable is the difference between the vision of those assembled at the sea gate of ‘Akka to jeer at and demonstrate their hostility towards the company of exiles and their Leader, and the vision of Bahá’u’lláh Who a few years before, in the Tablet of Sayyah foreshadowing His arrival in the city of ‘Akka, disclosed to those who were endowed with spiritual insight a vastly different spectacle…” — Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 3, ch. 1, p. 13 (`…Vol. 3…md:663`)

His comment on what the crowd did not see (this is Taherzadeh’s reading, not an authoritative text):

“The attitude of these onlookers, blind to the world of the spirit and the all-encompassing vision of Bahá’u’lláh, is characteristic of man’s attitude to the Revelation of God in every age. Over one hundred years have passed since Bahá’u’lláh uttered these words. The majority of mankind, its rulers and wise men, have so far failed to recognize their truth.” — Taherzadeh, vol. 3, p. 13

He compares it to Christ on the cross and to Muḥammad striking the rock, each of whom, he says, had a vision of the future that those around Him could not share.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá, writing later of the same place, uses the same reversal of prison and palace:

“‘Abdu’l-Bahá is also a captive in this Most Great Prison. But I have found this prison to be a palace, and regard this bondage as true freedom. This cage is to me a heavenly rose garden, and this captivity an everlasting throne, for it hath befallen me in the path of God and for the sake of the love of the Abhá Beauty—may my life be offered up for His loved ones.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Light of the World, no. 48, ¶2 (`sources/reference_library/light-of-the-world.md:778`); also in Bahá’í Sacred Writings (`bahai-sacred-writings.md:2551`)

Bahá’u’lláh also states what He took captivity to be. He wrote this of the conduct of followers who failed Him, and Shoghi Effendi quotes it in that setting:

“My captivity can bring on Me no shame. Nay, by My life, it conferreth on Me glory.” — Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:817`)

How Shoghi Effendi describes the turn

Shoghi Effendi does not say the banners appeared immediately. He describes a long period of confinement, a crisis in the community, and then a reversal:

“The cup of Bahá’u’lláh’s tribulations was now filled to overflowing. A situation, greatly humiliating, full of anxieties and even perilous, continued to face the exiles, until the time, set by an inscrutable Will, at which the tide of misery and abasement began to ebb, signalizing a transformation in the fortunes of the Faith even more conspicuous than the revolutionary change effected during the latter years of Bahá’u’lláh’s sojourn in Baghdád.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, ch. XI (`god-passes-by.md:825`)

The causes he lists are: “The gradual recognition by all elements of the population of Bahá’u’lláh’s complete innocence; the slow penetration of the true spirit of His teachings through the hard crust of their indifference and bigotry”, a change of governor, the “unremitting labors of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá”, and the dismissal of the officials who had prolonged the confinement (`god-passes-by.md:827`). He gives one example of the change: the Muftí of ‘Akká, “a man notorious for his bigotry, had been converted to the Faith” (`god-passes-by.md:829`).

Taherzadeh reports that the climate and water of ‘Akká were said to have improved after Bahá’u’lláh’s arrival, quoting a Christian scholar of ‘Akká in 1895–96 (vol. 3, pp. 21–22). This is a report of local testimony, not a statement of the Writings, and is not relied on here.

The prediction: what the authoritative texts say about its fulfilment

The second half of the sentence is about the future. The authoritative texts below describe progress toward it. None claims it is complete.

Shoghi Effendi

At the close of God Passes By, written in 1944 at the end of the first Bahá’í century, he describes the Faith’s extent and what remained:

“In the brief space of the first hundred years of its existence it has succeeded in diffusing its light over five continents, in erecting its outposts in the furthermost corners of the earth, in establishing, on an impregnable basis its Covenant with all mankind, in rearing the fabric of its world-encompassing Administrative Order, … and in launching the first of its systematic crusades for the spiritual conquest of the whole planet.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, concluding chapter (`god-passes-by.md:1858`)

And, in the same chapter, on the future:

“…we, who have been privileged to apprehend, to the degree our finite minds can fathom, the significance of these marvelous phenomena associated with its rise and establishment, can harbor no doubt that what it has already achieved in the first hundred years of its life provides sufficient guarantee that it will continue to forge ahead, capturing loftier heights, tearing down every obstacle, opening up new horizons and winning still mightier victories until its glorious mission, stretching into the dim ranges of time that lie ahead, is totally fulfilled.” — Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, concluding chapter (`god-passes-by.md:1862`)

The Guardian also writes of the banner raised at the end of the process:

“Then will the banner of the Most Great Peace be hoisted. Then will the worldwide sovereignty of Bahá’u’lláh … be recognized, acclaimed, and firmly established.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come (`sources/reference_library/promised-day-come.md:710`)

In the preceding paragraph of that book he quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s explanation of “the hoisting of the Standard of God among all nations”: “all nations and kindreds will be gathered together under the shadow of this Divine Banner” (`promised-day-come.md:694`, quoting Some Answered Questions). The word “banner” in the sentence of the Lawḥ-i-Sayyáḥ is not glossed by Shoghi Effendi or by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in any text found; the link to this passage is the reader’s, not the texts’. The same applies to the fuller parallel between the two passages: both speak of one banner under which all nations gather, but neither cites the other.

The Universal House of Justice

The Universal House of Justice quoted the sentence on 28 November 2023, in its account of the first century of the Formative Age, in a paragraph on the arrival of Bahá’u’lláh at ‘Akká. It says of the fulfilment of the older prophecy of the Lord of Hosts in the Holy Land that it “was not the result of His own volition but was compelled by His persecution at the hands of His avowed enemies, culminating in His exile.” It then quotes the sentence and continues:

“The spiritual potency of that land was immeasurably enhanced by His presence and the interment of His sacred remains and, soon after, those of His Herald, Himself a Manifestation of God. It is now the point to which every Bahá’í heart is drawn, the focal centre of their devotions, the goal of every aspiring pilgrim. The Bahá’í Holy Places welcome the peoples of the Holy Land, and indeed the peoples of every land. They are a precious trust held for all humanity.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 November 2023 (`sources/reference_library/20231128_001.md:161`)

In the same message the House of Justice describes the extent of the community:

“This community—the people of Bahá, ardent lovers of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá—now millions strong, has today spread to some one hundred thousand localities in 235 countries and territories. It has emerged from obscurity to occupy its place on the world stage.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 November 2023 (`sources/uhj_messages/merged_by_year/2023.md:416`)

And it summarises the Ten Year Crusade, begun in 1953 by Shoghi Effendi:

“After the passing of Shoghi Effendi, the Hands of the Cause ensured the successful completion of the Ten Year Crusade by following undeviatingly the path he had outlined. By applying the lessons learned under the guidance of the Guardian, more was accomplished in the teaching field over a single decade than in the previous century. The Faith spread to 131 new countries and territories, and the number of localities where Bahá’ís resided surpassed eleven thousand…” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 November 2023 (`sources/uhj_messages/merged_by_year/2023.md:1092`)

The Century of Light (2001, prepared under the House of Justice) gives the stage reached while the Guardian was still alive: “By October 1957, by which time the Faith had been established in over two hundred and fifty countries and territories…” (`sources/reference_library/century-light.md:456`). The Crusade it describes had set the goal of “one hundred and thirty-one additional countries and territories” (`century-light.md:444`).

The present stage of the work

The Universal House of Justice describes the current Nine Year Plan, in its Riḍván 2026 message, as entering a new phase:

“Now begins the second, greater phase of the Nine Year Plan. We see the global Bahá’í community advancing in unison, justly confident in the course it has set.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2026 ([source])

In its message of 31 December 2025 to the Counsellors it reports that “by Riḍván there will be a cluster where the third milestone has been passed in around half of the 160 countries and regions where, at the start of the Plan, still no such cluster existed” ([source], line 15). The Riḍván 2026 message also urges the friends, in a time it calls turbulent, “not to be apprehensive or disheartened”, and quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá that they be “ever hopeful”.

Present-day facts from official sources

The following is descriptive, taken from sources of the Bahá’í community itself. It is not authority for a teaching.

  • Universal House of Justice, 28 November 2023: “some one hundred thousand localities in 235 countries and territories”; “now millions strong”. This is the most recent dated figure for the number of countries and territories found in the corpus.
  • Bahá’í World News Service statistics page (https://news.bahai.org/media-information/statistics, read 13 October 2026; the page is undated, with a 2026 copyright notice): “There are more than 5 million Bahá’ís in the world”; the Faith is “established in virtually every country” and in many dependent territories; Bahá’ís “reside in well over 100,000 localities”; “About 2,100 indigenous tribes, races, and ethnic groups are represented”; “There are currently 188 councils at the national level”; “There are currently 14 Bahá’í Houses of Worship”; Bahá’í writings and literature have been translated into “more than 800 languages”.

These figures show reach. They do not show what the sentence predicts, that “all that dwell on earth” will be enlisted. At more than five million, the Bahá’ís are a small fraction of the world’s population, and the sources say as much of themselves: Taherzadeh, writing over a century after 1868, says that “the majority of mankind … have so far failed to recognize” the truth of the words. No authoritative text in the corpus gives a date or a number at which the prediction would be counted as fulfilled.

What the sources do not settle

  • The text of the Tablet. Only the one sentence and a summary by Taherzadeh are in the corpus. The Persian or Arabic original and the Tablet’s full English translation were not found. The wording of the sentence is the same in every English source found, apart from punctuation and the form of the opening.
  • Nabíl’s attestation. Shoghi Effendi says the allusion is “attested by Nabíl in his narrative”. The Dawn-Breakers ends before Adrianople, so the narrative he means is another work of Nabíl’s. It was not found in the corpus.
  • The date of the Tablet. See above: Adrianople, no date fixed.
  • The “Vale of Nabíl”. Shoghi Effendi and Taherzadeh say the name has the same numerical value as ‘Akká. The arithmetic was not checked here.
  • “Allegorical”. Taherzadeh calls the sentence an allegorical description. Shoghi Effendi calls it something the Tablet “had predicted”. The texts do not say what, if anything, was physically seen on arrival.
  • Present-day figures. The 235 countries and territories figure is dated 28 November 2023; the other statistics come from an undated page. Both will age.
