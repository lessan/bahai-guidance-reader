Where Does Evil Come From? Some Answered Questions 57, 74 and 75

Last updated 12 October 2026

What this page covers

Three short chapters of Some Answered Questions answer the question of where evil comes from. Chapter 57, “The Differences in Human Character”, asks why people differ in character and whether the wicked can be blamed for what they are. Chapter 74, “On Good and Evil”, argues that evil has no positive existence: it is the absence of a good, or a good thing that harms another thing in relation to it. Chapter 75, “Two Kinds of Torment”, distinguishes the torment that a vice is in itself from the torments that are inflicted from outside. Together they come to about 2,300 words.

The chapters make seven claims:

  1. Human beings have three kinds of character: innate, inherited and acquired through education (ch. 57 ¶2).
  2. The innate nature is purely good. People differ in innate capacity, but the difference is one of degree, “not a matter of good or evil” (ch. 57 ¶3–4, ¶9–11).
  3. Inheritance matters, but character outweighs descent. The strength or weakness of parents’ constitution passes to children, and certain lineages received a special blessing; but someone whose character does not match his forebears “would not be accounted among that lineage in spirit” (ch. 57 ¶5–7).
  4. Education makes enormous differences, and blame attaches to what is acquired, not to innate capacity (ch. 57 ¶8–9).
  5. Even apparent vices in a newborn are good capacities that can be misdirected, and even lying, “the very foundation of all evil”, is not blameworthy when a physician uses it to comfort a patient (ch. 57 ¶10–12).
  6. Evil has no positive existence. Ignorance is the want of knowledge, illness the want of health, darkness the absence of light; and a thing that has a positive existence, such as a scorpion’s venom, is evil only in relation to something it harms (ch. 74).
  7. Every vice is a torment in itself. Physical punishments are a second, “palpable” kind of torment; for a person of conscience the first kind is worse, and for “the people of God” the worst is to be veiled from Him (ch. 75).

The page is also the research base for a video episode on these chapters, with a host, a guest who is a retired palliative-care doctor and a doubting Anglican, and a narrator who reads the passages. It reads each chapter paragraph by paragraph; gathers the same teachings from other texts of Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice; describes the debates these chapters entered around 1904–1906 (on evil as privation, on suffering, on heredity and eugenics, on Christian Science, on whether doctors should tell patients the truth); and sets out the objections a physician would raise, with what the texts say and what they leave open.

Quotations of the Writings, of Shoghi Effendi and of the Universal House of Justice are verbatim from the texts held in this knowledge base. Persian terms are read from the 1908 Persian edition held in the corpus ([source]), a machine-read scan that has been checked only for the words cited here. The intellectual history and the medical material come from outside sources. They describe the field and are never cited as authority for a Bahá’í teaching.

The setting

Some Answered Questions records answers ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave to Laura Clifford Barney in ‘Akká between 1904 and 1906. The Foreword to the 2014 translation says that “Many of the conversations took place at the lunch table” and that “‘Abdu’l‑Bahá then corrected these notes twice in His own hand, sometimes substantially revising them in the process as well as carefully reviewing the final wording.” That review gives the book the standing of His own writing, unlike notes of His talks that He did not see.

The three chapters come from different parts of the book. Chapter 57 is in Part 4, on “the origins, powers, and conditions of man”, between the chapters on the soul’s powers (ch. 55–56) and on human comprehension (ch. 58). Chapters 74 and 75 open Part 5, “Miscellaneous Subjects”, and lead into the justice and mercy of God (ch. 76) and the punishment of criminals (ch. 77). Chapter 57 begins with a question; chapters 74 and 75 do not. Barney’s preface says the talks “were not given in any special order, but have now been roughly classified for the convenience of the reader”, so their placement is hers.

The Persian titles are more direct than the English ones:

  • Chapter 57: akhláq-i mutafávitih-yi naw’-i insán, “the differing characters of the human race”. The three kinds are akhláq-i fiṭrí (innate, from fiṭrat, the nature God gives at creation), akhláq-i irthí (inherited) and akhláq-i iktisábí (acquired), “which is gained through education” (bi-tarbiyat).
  • Chapter 74: dar bayán-i ánkih dar vujúd sharr níst, “explaining that there is no evil in existence”. The key word is ’adam, non-existence or privation: evil is “the ’adam of” the good. A good is amr-i vujúdí, “a matter of existence”; an evil is amr-i ’adamí, “a matter of non-existence”.
  • Chapter 75: ’adháb bar du qism ast, “torment is of two kinds”: ’adháb-i laṭíf (“subtle”, literally fine or delicate) and ’adháb-i ghalíẓ (“palpable”, literally coarse or gross).

The household in which these answers were given knew suffering at first hand. Youness Afroukhteh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s secretary and interpreter, who interpreted at the lunch table, describes the news of the 1903 persecutions in Yazd and Isfahan arriving in ‘Akká the year before Barney’s visits began: “every day a flood of agonizing, painful letters arrived ... and each one was perused by the Master”. He writes that in this calamity “there was no comfort”, and gives the number killed as “Some three hundred”. (Afroukhteh’s figure; historians’ counts differ.) Mary Lucas, an American visitor in January–February 1905, found in the household “some little children who are orphans and have no one to take care of them, and women who are widows, their husbands having suffered martyrdom in the Path of God.” The palpable torments that chapter 75 lists, “imprisonment, beating, expulsion, and banishment”, were the life of the speaker: ‘Abdu’l-Bahá had been an exile since childhood and was a prisoner of the Ottoman state while He gave these answers.

The theme was discussed more than once. Two Western visitors wrote down talks on the same subject: Ethel Rosenberg, who sat beside Barney at the table, in her “Notes taken in 1904”, and Mary Lucas, on 4 February 1905 (A Brief Account of My Visit to Acca, Chicago, 1905). Both record the teaching that evil is the absence of good, with the examples of darkness, poverty and ignorance. These are pilgrims’ notes, not authenticated text. They show that the subject came up at the table in these years; they are not quoted here as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s words.

The Foreword’s reading rule applies strongly to these chapters: “the concepts required to fully understand a given subject may be spread across different chapters”. Chapter 74 depends on the distinction between sensible and intelligible realities set out in chapter 16. Chapter 57’s account of the material and spiritual natures is fuller in chapter 29. Chapter 75’s “subtle torment” is explained in chapter 60 and applied in chapter 77. Chapter 57 ¶12, on the physician, is explained by chapter 72 on healing. All are discussed below.

Chapter 57: “The Differences in Human Character”

¶1–2. The question and the three kinds

“QUESTION: HOW MANY kinds of character are there in man and what are the causes of the differences and variations among them?” (¶1)

“Answer: There are the innate character, the inherited character, and the acquired character, which is gained through education.” (¶2)

The question has two parts: how many kinds of character there are, and what causes the differences. The answer adds a third question in ¶9: if people differ by nature, can the wicked be blamed? The chapter’s English word “character” translates akhláq, which means moral dispositions and habits as well as temperament. The three kinds are the same three that modern psychology calls nature, heredity and environment, but the division is not the same: “innate” here is what God gives every human being, and “inherited” is what comes from particular parents.

¶3–4. Innate character: differences of degree, not of good and evil

“As to the innate character, although the innate nature bestowed by God upon man is purely good, yet that character differs among men according to the degrees they occupy: All degrees are good, but some are more so than others. Thus every human being possesses intelligence and capacity, but intelligence, capacity, and aptitude differ from person to person. This is self-evident.” (¶3)

“For example, take a number of children from the same place and family, attending the same school and instructed by the same teacher, raised on the same food and in the same climate, wearing the same clothing and studying the same lessons: It is certain that among these children some will become skilled in the arts and sciences, some will be of average ability, and some will be dull. It is therefore clear that in man’s innate nature there is a difference in degree, aptitude, and capacity, but it is not a matter of good or evil—it is merely a difference of degree. One occupies the highest degree, another the middle degree, and yet another the lowest degree. Thus man, the animal, the plant, and the mineral all exist, but the existence of these four kinds of beings is different. Indeed, what a difference there is between the existence of man and that of the animal! Yet all these do exist, and it is evident that in existence there are differences of degree.” (¶4)

Two claims are made. First, the innate nature (fiṭrat) bestowed by God is “purely good” (khayr-i maḥḍ). Second, people do differ in innate “intelligence, capacity, and aptitude”, and the evidence is the children who share everything and still turn out differently. These differences are differences of degree. The comparison with the mineral, plant, animal and human is a comparison of degrees of existence: all four exist, and existing is good, but they exist in different degrees. The paragraph does not say that people of lower innate capacity are worse people. It says the opposite: “it is not a matter of good or evil”.

The example in ¶4 is the observation that behavioural genetics would later put in the title of a famous paper: “Why are children in the same family so different from one another?” (Plomin and Daniels, 1987). See “Objections” below.

¶5. Inherited character: constitution and “bloodline”

“As to differences in inherited character, they arise from the strength and weakness of man’s constitution; that is, if the parents are of weak constitution, then the children will be likewise, and if they are strong, then the children will also be robust. Moreover, the excellence of the bloodline exerts a major influence; for the goodly seed is like the superior stock that exists, likewise, among plants and animals. For example, you see that children born of a weak and sickly mother and father will naturally have a weak constitution and nerves, will lack patience, endurance, resolution, and perseverance, and will be impulsive, for they have inherited the weakness and frailty of their parents.” (¶5)

The paragraph’s first and last sentences are about health. The cause named is “the strength and weakness of man’s constitution” (quvvat va ḍa’f-i mizáj), and the example is “children born of a weak and sickly mother and father”, who inherit “weakness and frailty”. Between them stands the sentence that needs care: “the excellence of the bloodline exerts a major influence; for the goodly seed is like the superior stock that exists, likewise, among plants and animals.” The Persian is ṭahárat-i khún ḥukm-i kullí dárad, literally “purity of blood has a general effect”, and nuṭfih-yi ṭayyibih mánand-i jins-i a’lá, “a goodly seed is like a superior breed”.

In its context the sentence most naturally refers to the soundness of the parents’ bodies: the example that follows it (“For example ...”) is again about sickly parents. Nothing in the paragraph names a race, nation or class, and the effects it describes are effects on “constitution and nerves”, patience, endurance and resolution. This reading is the article’s own; the text does not define “bloodline”.

The sentence nevertheless uses the breeders’ analogy (seed, stock, plants and animals) that the eugenicists of the same years used. Karl Pearson’s Huxley Lecture of October 1903 concluded: “That good stock breeds good stock is a commonplace of every farmer” (see “The debate these chapters entered”). An honest reading should admit the shared vocabulary and then notice where the chapter goes next: ¶7 subordinates descent to character, ¶8 says education’s effects are “enormous”, and ¶9 says the wicked are blamed only for what they acquired.

¶6. Lineages singled out for a blessing

“Aside from this, certain families and lineages have been singled out for a special blessing. Thus the descendants of Abraham received the special blessing that all the Prophets of the House of Israel were raised up from among their ranks. This is a blessing that God bestowed upon that lineage. Moses, through both His father and His mother; Christ, through His mother; Muḥammad; the Báb; and all the Prophets and Holy Ones of Israel belong to that lineage. Bahá’u’lláh too is a lineal descendant of Abraham, for Abraham had other sons besides Ishmael and Isaac who in those days emigrated to the regions of Persia and Afghanistan, and the Blessed Beauty is one of their descendants.” (¶6)

This is the one place in the chapter where “inherited” means something other than health. The “special blessing” is that prophets arose from Abraham’s descendants. The paragraph names Moses, Christ, Muḥammad, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh as descendants. The “other sons besides Ishmael and Isaac” are the sons of Keturah (Genesis 25:1–6); Shoghi Effendi writes in God Passes By that Bahá’u’lláh “derived His descent, on the one hand, from Abraham (the Father of the Faithful) through his wife Katurah”. The paragraph says that a blessing was given to a lineage; it does not say that the members of that lineage are better people, and the next paragraph says that a descendant without the character of the line is not counted in it.

¶7. Canaan: descent without character does not count

“Hence it is evident that inherited character also exists, to such a degree that if one’s character does not conform to that of one’s forebears, one would not be accounted among that lineage in spirit even if one were a descendant in body. Such is the case of Canaan, who is not reckoned among the descendants of Noah.” (¶7)

The conclusion of the section on inheritance is a limit on it. Inherited character “also exists”, but descent “in body” without conformity of character does not make a person a member of the line “in spirit”. The example is Canaan. The 2014 edition’s footnote points to Genesis 9:22–27, where Noah curses Canaan, son of Ham. The Persian (kan’án az sulálih-yi Núḥí shumurdih namíshavad, “Canaan is not counted among Noah’s progeny”) fits even more closely the Qur’anic story of Noah’s son who refused to board the ark and drowned, of whom God tells Noah, “he is not of thy family” (Qur’an 11:46, in the translation shown on quran.is, which appears to be Yusuf Ali’s). Islamic commentary commonly names that son Kan’án. Either way, the point the paragraph draws is that character outweighs descent.

A note for presenters. Genesis 9 is the passage from which the so-called “curse of Ham” was later drawn and used, from the medieval period to the nineteenth century, to justify the enslavement of Black Africans. Paragraph 7 does not use the story that way: it uses Canaan as an example of a descendant who is not counted in a blessed line because of his own conduct. But a reader or viewer who knows that history may hear the name with that weight, and a presenter should be ready to say what the paragraph does and does not say.

¶8. Acquired character: the “enormous” effect of education

“As to the differences of character arising from education, they are great indeed, for education exerts an enormous influence. Through education the ignorant become learned, the cowardly become courageous, the crooked branch becomes straight, the acrid and bitter fruit of the mountains and woods becomes sweet and succulent, and the five-petalled flower puts forth a hundred petals. Through education barbarous nations become civilized and even animals take on human-like manners. Education must be accorded the greatest importance; for just as diseases are highly communicable in the world of bodies, so is character highly communicable in the realm of hearts and spirits. The differences caused by education are enormous and exert a major influence.” (¶8)

The paragraph repeats the claim three times (“great indeed”, “enormous influence”, “enormous and exert a major influence”). The examples are those of chapter 3: cultivation turns bitter fruit sweet and the five-petalled flower into a hundred petals. The new element is contagion: “just as diseases are highly communicable in the world of bodies, so is character highly communicable in the realm of hearts and spirits.” Character spreads from person to person, which is why education “must be accorded the greatest importance”.

¶9. Can the wicked be blamed? Innate and acquired capacity

“Now, someone might say that, since the capacity and aptitude of souls differ, how can one reproach the wicked for it is their capacities themselves that are different. Such difference in capacity must inevitably lead to a difference in character. But this is not so, for capacity is of two kinds: innate and acquired. The innate capacity, which is the creation of God, is wholly and entirely good—in the innate nature there is no evil. The acquired capacity, however, can become the cause of evil. For example, God has created all men in such a fashion, and has given them such a capacity and disposition, that they are benefited by sugar and honey and are harmed or killed by poison. This is an innate capacity and disposition that God has bestowed equally upon all men. But man may begin little by little to take poison by ingesting a small quantity every day and gradually increasing it until he reaches the point where he would perish if he were not to consume several grams of opium every day, and where his innate capacities are completely subverted. Consider how the innate capacity and disposition can be so completely changed, through variation of habit and training, as to be entirely perverted. It is not on account of their innate capacity and disposition that one reproaches the wicked, but rather on account of that which they themselves have acquired.” (¶9)

This is the moral centre of the chapter. The objection (footnote 140: “That is, that people cannot be held responsible for their own character”) is answered by dividing capacity (isti’dád) into innate and acquired. Innate capacity is “wholly and entirely good—in the innate nature there is no evil”. Acquired capacity can be “completely subverted” by habit. The example is addiction: a body made to be harmed by poison can be trained, dose by dose, to need it. The Persian has “one dirham of opium” (yak dirham afyún) a day, where the English has “several grams”; a dirham was about three grams. Opium use was widespread in Iran at the time. The conclusion is a principle of responsibility: “It is not on account of their innate capacity and disposition that one reproaches the wicked, but rather on account of that which they themselves have acquired.”

The paragraph does not deal with acquired dispositions that a person did not choose (a child raised in violence, for example). Chapter 77 on criminals, the next release’s subject, says that societies that fail to educate are partly responsible for crime (“ignorance is the root cause of crime”, ch. 77 ¶13).

¶10–11. The nursing child, and the misuse of good qualities

“In the innate nature of things there is no evil—all is good. This applies even to certain apparently blameworthy attributes and dispositions which seem inherent in some people, but which are not in reality reprehensible. For example, you can see in a nursing child, from the beginning of its life, the signs of greed, of anger, and of ill temper; and so it might be argued that good and evil are innate in the reality of man, and that this is contrary to the pure goodness of the innate nature and of creation. The answer is that greed, which is to demand ever more, is a praiseworthy quality provided that it is displayed under the right circumstances. Thus, should a person show greed in acquiring science and knowledge, or in the exercise of compassion, high-mindedness, and justice, this would be most praiseworthy. And should he direct his anger and wrath against the bloodthirsty tyrants who are like ferocious beasts, this too would be most praiseworthy. But should he display these qualities under other conditions, this would be deserving of blame.” (¶10)

“It follows therefore that in existence and creation there is no evil at all, but that when man’s innate qualities are used in an unlawful way, they become blameworthy. Thus if a wealthy and generous person gives alms to a poor man to spend on his necessities, and if the latter spends that sum in an improper way, that is blameworthy. The same holds true of all the innate qualities of man which constitute the capital of human life: If they are displayed and employed in an improper way, they become blameworthy. It is clear then that the innate nature is purely good.” (¶11)

The objection in ¶10 is the strongest empirical one: a newborn already shows greed, anger and ill temper, so good and evil seem innate. The answer is that these are capacities, not vices. “Greed, which is to demand ever more” (ḥirṣ ... ṭalab-i izdiyád), is praiseworthy when it is directed at knowledge, compassion and justice. Anger is praiseworthy when it is directed “against the bloodthirsty tyrants”. A quality is “blameworthy” only when it is used “in an unlawful way” (dar mavaḍi’-i ghayr-i mashrú’ih, literally “in places not lawful”). The innate qualities are “the capital of human life” (sarmáyih-yi ḥayát); misuse is like a poor man spending alms on the wrong things.

The same observation of a nursing infant was used by Augustine for the opposite conclusion. In the Confessions (Book I) he writes: “Myself have seen and known even a baby envious; it could not speak, yet it turned pale and looked bitterly on its foster-brother”, and concludes that “The weakness then of infant limbs, not its will, is its innocence.” The contrast is exact and is discussed below under “The debate these chapters entered”.

¶12. The worst vice, and the physician’s comforting words

“Consider that the worst of all qualities and the most odious of all attributes, and the very foundation of all evil, is lying, and that no more evil or reprehensible quality can be imagined in all existence. It brings all human perfections to naught and gives rise to countless vices. There is no worse attribute than this, and it is the foundation of all wickedness. Now, all this notwithstanding, should a physician console a patient and say, “Thank God, you are doing better and there is hope for your recovery”, although these words may be contrary to the truth, yet sometimes they will ease the patient’s mind and become the means of curing the illness. And this is not blameworthy.” (¶12)

The example is chosen to show that even the worst quality is not evil in every use. Lying is “the very foundation of all evil” (asás-i jamí’-i shurúr), so if even lying can be “not blameworthy” in a given setting, then the principle of ¶10–11 (that qualities are blameworthy only in their misuse) holds without exception.

Four details of the wording matter:

  • The speaker is a physician, ḥakím, the word for a traditional doctor (and also for a sage), speaking to a patient.
  • The words are hopeful in general terms: “Thank God, you are doing better and there is hope for your recovery” (al-ḥamdu li’lláh aḥvál-i tu bihtar ast va umíd-i ḥuṣúl-i shifást).
  • The effect is limited: “sometimes” (gáhí) they “ease the patient’s mind and become the means of curing the illness”.
  • The verdict is “not blameworthy” (madhmúm níst). The paragraph does not say that a doctor should, or must, say such things.

The mechanism is explained in chapter 72 on healing. There, good news can produce “a nervous excitement” that dispels an illness, but “this is effective only to a point and not in all cases. For instance, should someone contract a grave illness or be physically injured, these means will neither dispel the illness nor soothe and heal the injury” (ch. 72 ¶5–6). Read together, the two chapters allow a comforting word for its effect on the patient’s state of mind; they do not claim that it changes the course of a grave illness.

The paragraph sits beside the rest of the Writings on truthfulness: “truthfulness is the foundation of all human virtues. Without truthfulness progress and success, in all the worlds of God, are impossible for any soul” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, translated by Shoghi Effendi in The Advent of Divine Justice). The KB page on truthfulness notes that “The Writings do not settle the hard disclosure cases” (truthfulness-foundation). No other passage found for this article in the Writings held in this knowledge base addresses a doctor’s words to a patient. Modern medical ethics, which now requires honest disclosure, is discussed under “Objections”.

Chapter 74: “On Good and Evil”

¶1–2. Sensible and intelligible realities

“TO EXPLAIN THE truth of this matter is difficult indeed. Know that created things are of two kinds: material and spiritual, sensible and intelligible. That is, some are perceptible to the senses, while others are only perceived by the mind.” (¶1)

“Sensible realities are those which are perceived by the five outer senses: So, for example, those outward things which the eye sees are called sensible. Intelligible realities are those which have no outward existence but are perceived by the mind. For example, the mind itself is an intelligible reality and has no outward existence. Likewise, all human virtues and attributes have an intelligible rather than a sensible existence; that is, they are realities that are perceived by the mind and not by the senses.” (¶2)

The chapter opens by admitting difficulty: “TO EXPLAIN THE truth of this matter is difficult indeed” (bisyár mushkil). It then sets up the distinction that chapter 16 calls “pivotal to grasping the essence of the other questions”: sensible realities (maḥsús), perceived by the five senses, and intelligible realities (ma’qúl), perceived by the mind, among them the mind itself and “all human virtues and attributes”. Chapter 16 adds that intelligible realities can only be spoken of through sensible images: “knowledge is figuratively described as light, and ignorance as darkness” (ch. 16 ¶5). Chapter 74 uses exactly that image in ¶6.

¶3. Intelligible evils are privations

“Briefly, intelligible realities such as the praiseworthy attributes and perfections of man are purely good and have a positive existence. Evil is simply their non-existence. So ignorance is the want of knowledge, error is the want of guidance, forgetfulness is the want of remembrance, foolishness is the want of understanding: All these are nothing in themselves and have no positive existence.” (¶3)

¶4. Sensible evils are privations

“As for sensible realities, these are also purely good, and evil is merely their non-existence; that is, blindness is the want of sight, deafness is the want of hearing, poverty is the want of wealth, illness is the want of health, death is the want of life, and weakness is the want of strength.” (¶4)

The two lists cover two kinds of evil. ¶3 is about moral and mental evils (ignorance, error, forgetfulness, foolishness). ¶4 is about physical evils (blindness, deafness, poverty, illness, death, weakness). Each evil is named as the absence of a good: “the want of” translates ’adam. The claim is not that these evils are unreal, imagined or harmless. It is that each one is defined by a good that is missing: there is no such thing as blindness apart from an eye that does not see.

The list in ¶4 is the one a doctor will notice: “illness is the want of health, death is the want of life”. Chapter 73 explains what “health” means in this book: “The human body is composed of numerous elements according to a particular state of equilibrium. So long as this equilibrium is maintained, man is preserved from sickness” (ch. 73 ¶3), and illness occurs when “there is a deficiency in one of the component parts of the body and a surfeit of another” (ch. 73 ¶4). On this account health is a balance, and illness is the loss of that balance, which can be caused by an excess as well as a deficiency. “The want of health” therefore does not mean that every illness is a mere gap. It means that what is lost in illness is an order. This matters for the objection that a tumour is “very much a thing” (see “Objections”).

¶5–6. The scorpion: evil in relation, good in itself

“Now, a doubt comes to mind: Scorpions and snakes are poisonous—is this good or evil, for they have a positive existence? Yes, it is true that scorpions and snakes are evil, but only in relation to us and not to themselves, for their venom is their weapon and their sting their means of defence. But as the constituent elements of their venom are incompatible with those of our bodies—that is, as these constituent elements are mutually opposed—the venom is evil, or rather, those elements are evil in relation to each other, while in their own reality they are both good.” (¶5)

“To summarize, one thing may be evil in relation to another but not evil within the limits of its own being. It follows therefore that there is no evil in existence: Whatsoever God has created, He has created good. Evil consists merely in non-existence. For example, death is the absence of life: When man is no longer sustained by the power of life, he dies. Darkness is the absence of light: When light is no more, darkness reigns. Light is a positively existing thing, but darkness has no positive existence; it is merely its absence. Likewise, wealth is a positively existing thing but poverty is merely its absence.” (¶6)

Paragraph 5 raises the obvious objection: venom is not an absence; it is a substance. The answer is that a thing with positive existence can be evil “in relation to” another thing (bi’n-nisbih), while good “within the limits of its own being” (dar ḥadd-i dhátash). The venom is the scorpion’s weapon and defence. Its harm to us lies in the incompatibility between its elements and ours: “those elements are evil in relation to each other, while in their own reality they are both good.”

So the chapter recognises two kinds of evil: privation (¶3–4), and relation, where two goods are incompatible (¶5). Both are consistent with ¶6’s conclusion: “Whatsoever God has created, He has created good.”

The scorpion image had a long life in Persian. Rúmí’s Masnavi (Book IV) says, in the same terms of relation (nisbat): “So there is no absolute evil in the world; evil is relative ... The snake’s venom is life to the snake; in relation to man it is death” (this article’s literal translation of pas bad-i muṭlaq nabáshad dar jahán / bad bi-nisbat báshad ... zahr-i már án már rá báshad ḥayát / nisbatash bá ádamí báshad mamát). Chapter 77 has a second scorpion: the Abbasid caliph Mutavakkil, who “would summon his ministers, deputies, and trustees to his presence, have a box full of scorpions let loose among them, and, forbidding anyone to move, would burst into boisterous laughter whenever one of them was stung” (ch. 77 ¶9). Read together, the two passages separate two kinds of evil: the scorpion’s sting, which is evil to us in relation, and the caliph’s cruelty, which is a human quality used in “an unlawful way” (ch. 57 ¶11).

¶7. Conclusion

“It is thus evident that all evil is mere non-existence. Good has a positive existence; evil is merely its absence.” (¶7)

Chapter 75: “Two Kinds of Torment”

¶1. Subtle torment: vice is its own punishment

“KNOW THAT THERE are two kinds of torment: subtle and palpable. For example, ignorance is itself a torment, but it is a subtle torment; indifference to God is itself a torment; falsehood is itself a torment; iniquity and treachery are torments. Indeed, all the human imperfections are torments, but they are subtle torments. A person endowed with a conscience will certainly prefer to be killed rather than to sin, and to have his tongue cut out rather than to slander and lie.” (¶1)

The claim is that a vice is a torment in itself (nafs-i jahl ‘adháb ast, “ignorance itself is torment”). Ignorance, indifference to God, falsehood, iniquity and treachery are not punished by torment; they are torment, but a subtle one, which their possessor may not feel. The last sentence describes a person “endowed with a conscience” (shu’úr, which also means awareness or discernment). For such a person, in the Persian, “being killed is better than error (khaṭá), and a cut-out tongue is better than lying and slander” (nazd-i ú qatl bihtar az khaṭást va lisán-i burídih bihtar az kadhib va iftirá’st). The sentence describes what that person values; it is not a command. It does not say that anyone ought to be killed, or to die, rather than sin.

The sentence had a concrete meaning for Bahá’ís of 1904. In the persecutions of those years many were offered their lives if they would recant, which would have meant saying something false about their faith, and some refused (this specific fact is not documented in the corpus for 1903 and is stated here from general histories). The Universal House of Justice, writing to the Bahá’ís of Iran on 1 October 2014, describes the same choice today: “For the sake of upholding the truth and not uttering a simple false statement about your belief that would open up to you every social and economic opportunity, you maintain your integrity and refuse to deny or dissimulate your faith.”

¶2. Palpable torment, and the worst torment

“The other kind of torment is palpable and consists in physical punishments such as imprisonment, beating, expulsion, and banishment. But for the people of God, to be veiled from Him is still more grievous than all these torments.” (¶2)

The palpable torments are the penalties of the time: imprisonment, beating, expulsion, banishment. For “the people of God” (ahl-i Alláh) the greatest torment is “to be veiled from Him” (iḥtijáb az Ḥaqq). Chapter 60 says the same in fuller form: “For such souls, there is no greater torment than to be veiled from God, and no worse punishment than selfish qualities, evil attributes, baseness of character, and engrossment in carnal desires” (ch. 60 ¶3), and it calls this “existential” torment, found “in all the worlds of God, whether in this world or in the heavenly realms of the spirit” (ch. 60 ¶2). Chapter 77 draws the practical consequence for society: the aim of education is that people “regard the crime itself as the greatest chastisement and the most grievous torment and punishment” (ch. 77 ¶3).

How the three chapters fit together

Read as one answer to “where does evil come from?”, the chapters say:

  1. Nothing God created is evil. The innate nature of every human being is good, and so is every created thing in its own reality (57 ¶3, ¶9–11; 74 ¶6).
  2. Evil is either the absence of a good (ignorance, illness, death) or the harm one good thing does to another to which it is opposed (venom) (74 ¶3–6).
  3. In human beings, moral evil comes from the misuse of good capacities and from what is acquired through habit and bad education (57 ¶9–11). That is why the wicked can be blamed, and why education matters so much (57 ¶8).
  4. People differ in innate capacity and in inherited constitution, but those differences are of degree, not of good and evil, and they do not determine whether someone is counted good (57 ¶3–7).
  5. A vice is its own punishment, a “subtle torment”, whether or not it is felt; outward punishments are a second and lesser kind (75).

Three things the chapters do not do should be stated plainly. They do not explain why God permits the evils that come from privation and relation (illness, earthquakes, venom), beyond saying that what God created is good; chapter 70 ¶3 adds only that “sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, and material loss” are matters where man “is not accountable”, “for he is compelled to endure them”. They do not say that suffering is unreal or should be ignored. And they do not say that suffering is deserved.

The same themes elsewhere in the Writings

Bahá’u’lláh

  • Created good; evil from ourselves. “Know thou that all men have been created in the nature made by God, the Guardian, the Self-Subsisting. ... All that which ye potentially possess can, however, be manifested only as a result of your own volition. ... Every good thing is of God, and every evil thing is from yourselves.” (Gleanings, LXXVII). The phrase “the nature made by God” corresponds to chapter 57’s fiṭrat, and “every evil thing is from yourselves” to its ¶9.
  • Calamity. “O SON OF MAN! My calamity is My providence, outwardly it is fire and vengeance, but inwardly it is light and mercy.” (Hidden Words, Arabic 51)
  • One substance. “O CHILDREN OF MEN! Know ye not why We created you all from the same dust? That no one should exalt himself over the other.” (Hidden Words, Arabic 68). This is the principle against which chapter 57 ¶5–6 should be read.
  • Medicine. “Resort ye, in times of sickness, to competent physicians; We have not set aside the use of material means, rather have We confirmed it through this Pen” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶113). However illness is defined, it is to be treated.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: other chapters of Some Answered Questions

  • Ch. 29 (Sin and Atonement) places the source of sin in man’s material nature: “The material nature is the source of every imperfection, and the spiritual nature is the source of all perfection” (¶2), and “All sin is prompted by the dictates of nature. These dictates of nature ... are not sins with respect to the animal but are sins with regard to man” (¶6). This is the same teaching as chapter 57 from another side: the animal qualities are not evil in the animal; they become sins in a being that can rise above them.
  • Ch. 29 ¶9 and ch. 30 ¶10 reject inherited guilt: the doctrine that Adam’s descendants “through no fault or sin of their own, and for no other reason than their descent from Adam, became guilty sinners” is “irrational and clearly mistaken” and “far from the justice of God”. Chapter 30 reinterprets the story: “the tree is the material world and the serpent is attachment to it” (¶11).
  • Ch. 60 on existential rewards and torments (quoted above under ch. 75).
  • Ch. 66 ¶7–8, on children who die young: “These children abide under the shadow of the Divine Providence, and, as they have committed no sin and are unsullied by the defilements of the world of nature, they will become the manifestations of divine bounty”.
  • Ch. 70 ¶3, on what is not chosen: “sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, and material loss: These are not subject to the will of man and he is not accountable for them”. See also free-will-and-fate.
  • Ch. 72–73 on healing (quoted above under ch. 57 ¶12 and ch. 74 ¶4).
  • Ch. 77 on crime, education and the caliph’s scorpions (quoted above).

The debate these chapters entered

The chapters were given in years when Europeans and Americans were arguing about four related questions: whether evil is a real thing or the absence of good; whether a good God is compatible with the suffering of the innocent; how far character is inherited; and whether a doctor may lie to a patient. The details and exact quotations are in the episode’s context dossier; this section summarises them.

Evil as privation: an old answer

The teaching that evil is the absence of good is old. Augustine (354–430) is its best-known Christian source: “For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good? In the bodies of animals, disease and wounds mean nothing but the absence of health” (Enchiridion, ch. 11, tr. J. F. Shaw). In the Confessions he describes coming to see “that evil was nothing but a privation of good” (Book III) and concludes “whatsoever is, is good” (Book VII). Boethius’s Philosophy says “evil is nothing” (Consolation, Book III). Thomas Aquinas defines evil as “the absence of good” and adds the relational case: “fire would not be generated if air was not corrupted, nor would the life of a lion be preserved unless the ass were killed” (Summa Theologiae I, q. 48, a. 1 and a. 2 ad 3). In Islamic philosophy, Avicenna treated evil as non-existence (’adam) and distinguished evil in itself from things that are evil only in relation to what they harm; the vocabulary of chapter 74 (’adam, vujúdí, nisbat) belongs to that tradition, and Rúmí put the relational case into verse (quoted above). Leibniz’s Theodicy (1710) restated the privation view and noted that a privation can still have force: “Evil itself comes only from privation ... We see that water in freezing is capable of breaking a gun-barrel wherein it is confined; and yet cold is a certain privation of force” (§153).

So chapter 74 does not introduce a new doctrine. What is distinctive is its pairing of privation with relation (¶5) and its place beside chapters 57 and 75, which put the weight on capacity, education and conscience.

Suffering and a good God: the objection

The objection to the privation answer was made most sharply in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: Hume’s restatement of “EPICURUS’s old questions” (Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, Part X, 1779), Voltaire’s poem on the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 (“Come, ye philosophers, who cry, ‘All’s well,’ / And contemplate this ruin of a world”), John Stuart Mill’s essay “Nature” (1874: “nearly all the things which men are hanged or imprisoned for doing to one another, are nature’s every day performances”), Darwin’s letter to Asa Gray of 22 May 1860 (“I cannot persuade myself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of Caterpillars”), and Ivan Karamazov’s refusal of any harmony bought with “the tears of that one tortured child” (Dostoevsky, 1879–80; English tr. 1912).

Christian Science and the “sick soul” (1875–1906)

In the same years an American religious movement drew the opposite practical conclusion from a teaching that sounds like chapter 74. Mary Baker Eddy’s Science and Health says: “Evil is a negation, because it is the absence of truth. It is nothing, because it is the absence of something”, and “Man is never sick, for Mind is not sick and matter cannot be.” Christian Science was growing fast: on 10 June 1906 its Boston “Mother Church Extension” was dedicated, with more than 30,000 people at the day’s services by the church’s own count. William James, in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), described it as “the most radical branch of mind‐cure in its dealings with evil. For it evil is simply a _lie_, and any one who mentions it is a liar”, and judged that such “healthy‐mindedness is inadequate as a philosophical doctrine, because the evil facts which it refuses positively to account for are a genuine portion of reality”.

The difference from the Bahá’í texts is practical and clear. Chapter 74 calls illness “the want of health”, but chapter 73 describes illness as a real disturbance of the body, and Bahá’u’lláh commands, “Resort ye, in times of sickness, to competent physicians”. The House of Justice adds that the privation teaching “does not mean that there is no power in evil”. Privation in the Bahá’í texts is an account of what evil is, not a denial that it happens.

Heredity and character (1900–1906)

The talks were given at the moment heredity became a public cause. Karl Pearson’s Huxley Lecture (16 October 1903; printed in Biometrika in March 1904) concluded: “We inherit our parents’ tempers, our parents’ conscientiousness, shyness and ability, even as we inherit their stature, forearm and span”, and that such qualities “are bred and not created”. Francis Galton defined “eugenics” before the Sociological Society in London on 16 May 1904 and proposed that it “must be introduced into the national conscience, like a new religion”. Against them, the British Inter-Departmental Committee on Physical Deterioration (1904) found that “the influence of heredity in the form of the transmission of any direct taint is not a considerable factor in the production of degenerates”, and blamed poverty, food and housing; and the Atlanta University conference led by W. E. B. Du Bois (1906) found no “adequate scientific warrant for the assumption that the Negro race is inferior to other races in physical build or vitality”, the differences in mortality being “sufficiently explained by conditions of life”. Cesare Lombroso’s theory of the “born criminal”, first set out in 1876, was honoured at the sixth international congress of criminal anthropology in Turin in 1906.

Chapter 57 takes a position in this debate without naming it. It accepts that constitution is inherited (¶5), but denies that evil is innate (¶3, ¶9), makes descent subordinate to character (¶7), and says the effects of education are “enormous” (¶8). The eugenicists drew the opposite conclusion from the same breeders’ analogy: Pearson wrote that “the education is of small service, unless it be applied to an intelligent race of men”.

Truth-telling in medicine

Chapter 57 ¶12 matches the medical ethics of its time. The Hippocratic Decorum advises “concealing most things from the patient while you are attending to him ... revealing nothing of the patient’s future or present condition” (ch. XVI, tr. W. H. S. Jones). The American Medical Association’s Code of 1847 said “the physician should be the minister of hope and comfort to the sick” and that “The life of a sick person can be shortened not only by the acts, but also by the words or the manner of a physician” (Art. I, §4). In 1961, 90 per cent of the American physicians who answered a survey said they preferred not to tell a patient that he had cancer (Oken, JAMA); by 1979, 97 per cent of respondents preferred to tell (Novack et al., JAMA). Current law and guidance in the United Kingdom require doctors to share information patients want, with only narrow exceptions (the Supreme Court’s Montgomery judgment, 2015; General Medical Council guidance). This history belongs to the field and is not authority for any Bahá’í teaching.

Objections a physician would raise

Each item gives the objection as an experienced doctor might put it, what the texts say, and what is left open. The responses are drawn from the texts and from outside sources where stated; they are not the only possible ones.

1. A tumour is not an absence. It is very much a thing.

The objection. “Illness is the want of health” (74 ¶4) might fit blindness or deafness. It does not fit a tumour, an infection or a toxin, which are positive things that grow, invade and kill.

What the texts say. Chapter 74 has two categories, not one. Paragraph 5 is written for exactly this case: a thing with “a positive existence” that harms us. It is evil “in relation to us”, while “in their own reality” both it and we are good. A cancer cell does what living cells do (it divides) in a way that is incompatible with the body around it. And chapter 73 defines health as “a particular state of equilibrium” that can be lost by “a surfeit” as well as by “a deficiency”; what is missing in illness is the body’s order. Cancer biology describes the disease in similar terms: cells that have lost the controls that normally limit their growth and gained signals that drive it (Hanahan and Weinberg, “The Hallmarks of Cancer”, Cell, 2000 and 2011). The match is an observation by this article, not a claim that the text anticipated oncology.

What is left open. Whether describing a tumour as good in its own reality is more than a choice of words. A doctor may say that the classification changes nothing about the patient’s suffering, and that is true; chapter 74 is an account of what evil is, not a treatment or a consolation.

2. Cruelty is more than an absence.

The objection. A person who tortures a child is not merely lacking kindness. Ivan Karamazov: “a beast can never be so cruel as a man, so artistically cruel.”

What the texts say. Chapter 57 locates moral evil not in a missing faculty but in good capacities “used in an unlawful way” (¶11) and in dispositions “completely subverted” by habit (¶9). The cruel person’s intelligence, energy and will are real; what is missing is their right direction. The House of Justice says that evil “has no existence in its own right, but that does not mean that there is no power in evil” (1984), and One Common Faith calls the negation of the good “severely crippling in its effect” and names “spiritual disease” as what modern explanations of atrocity leave out. Shoghi Effendi: evil “exerts a very strong influence both in this world and in the next” (1934).

What is left open. Philosophers still debate whether privation can account for malice that seems to aim at harm for its own sake. The texts’ answer is that such malice is a disordered use of real powers. A sceptic may accept the description and still say the word “absence” understates it.

3. Pain is a positive experience, not a lack.

What the texts say. Chapter 74 does not list pain among the privations. On its own terms, pain is a sensible reality that is good in its function: it warns of injury. People born unable to feel pain (congenital insensitivity to pain) injure themselves repeatedly and often die young. Pain becomes evil “in relation” when it no longer serves that function, as in intractable pain at the end of life. This application of ¶5 is this article’s, not the text’s.

4. Calling evil the absence of good sounds like Christian Science, and comforts no one at a bedside.

What the texts say. The wording overlaps with Eddy’s (“Evil is a negation ... It is nothing, because it is the absence of something”), but the conclusions are opposite: Eddy denied that disease is real (“Man is never sick”); Bahá’u’lláh commands recourse to “competent physicians”, and the House of Justice says there is “power in evil”. On comfort: the chapter does not present itself as consolation. Chapter 74 begins “TO EXPLAIN THE truth of this matter is difficult indeed”, and the texts on consolation are elsewhere (the letter on the loss of a son in Selections 171, which begins: “although the loss of a son is indeed heartbreaking and beyond the limits of human endurance”).

5. “Bloodline” and “superior stock”: this is the language that led to eugenics.

What the texts say. See ¶5–7 above. The sentence uses the same breeders’ analogy as Pearson and Galton. But in context it is about the parents’ health, it names no race or class, it is followed by a paragraph that makes descent count for nothing without character (¶7), and the chapter’s verdict is that education’s effects are “enormous” (¶8). Elsewhere: “Know ye not why We created you all from the same dust? That no one should exalt himself over the other” (Hidden Words); “God does not look at colors; He looks at the hearts” (Howard University, 1912).

What is left open. Whether the 2014 translation’s “excellence of the bloodline” is the best English for ṭahárat-i khún. And whether, in an age that has seen what eugenics did, the sentence can be read without that history. An honest presentation names the history and the overlap and shows where the chapter parts from it.

6. The blessing on Abraham’s lineage: is this a chosen bloodline?

What the texts say. Paragraph 6 says prophets came from that lineage; it does not say its members are better. The following paragraph says a descendant without the character of the line is not counted in it. Bahá’u’lláh’s descent is through Keturah, a line outside both the Israelite and the Arab lines.

7. Innate differences in capacity: what about disability?

What the texts say. Differences of innate capacity are differences of degree, “not a matter of good or evil” (¶4); every human being “possesses intelligence and capacity” (¶3). The text does not discuss disability, and should not be stretched to do so. Modern behavioural genetics finds that most measured traits are partly heritable: a meta-analysis of fifty years of twin studies found an average heritability of 49 per cent across 17,804 traits (Polderman et al., Nature Genetics, 2015). Eric Turkheimer’s “laws” (2000) include that “The effect of being raised in the same family is smaller than the effect of genes” and that “A substantial portion of the variation in complex human behavioral traits is not accounted for by the effects of genes or families.” Heritability is a statistic about variation in a population, not a measure of how fixed a trait is in a person; it changes with environment.

What is left open. Chapter 57 ¶4 and modern genetics agree on the observation (children of the same family differ). They differ on its meaning: the text treats innate capacity as a gift of God that carries no moral weight; genetics treats it as variation with causes. The two are not in conflict unless one claims that heritable traits determine moral worth, which the text denies.

8. The nursing child: does a baby have a temperament?

What the texts say. Paragraph 10 grants the observation and changes its meaning: the infant’s “greed” is the capacity to “demand ever more”, which will be good or bad according to its object. Developmental psychology agrees that infants differ in temperament from birth (studies since the 1950s on “easy” and “difficult” babies); it does not treat temperament as moral. The contrast with Augustine (the infant’s envy as evidence of sin) and with the Anglican Article IX (man “is of his own nature inclined to evil”) is exact.

9. The physician’s lie: modern medicine forbids it.

The objection. A palliative-care doctor today is trained to tell patients the truth they want to know. False reassurance (“you are doing better”) is a breach of trust and of law, and research suggests it does not even protect hope: parents of children with cancer who received more prognostic information reported more hope, not less (Mack et al., Journal of Clinical Oncology, 2007).

What the texts say. Paragraph 12 calls lying “the very foundation of all evil” and then says that a physician’s hopeful words “sometimes” help and are “not blameworthy”. It does not oblige anyone to say them. Chapter 72 limits what such words can do (“these means have no sway over grave illnesses unless assisted by the constitution of the patient”). The paragraph’s purpose in the chapter is to show that even the worst quality is not evil in every use.

What is left open. Whether ¶12 should be read as a rule of medical practice at all. It describes the ethics of its own time, which were the ethics of the Hippocratic Decorum and the 1847 American code. A Bahá’í doctor today works under the duty of truthfulness and under the law of informed consent; nothing in the Writings held here requires deception. A reasonable modern reading keeps the principle (a word chosen for the patient’s good, not the speaker’s convenience, may be judged differently from a self-serving lie) and drops the specific practice. That reading is this article’s; the corpus does not settle it.

10. Patients tormented by conscience at the end.

What the texts say. Chapter 75 describes exactly this: the “subtle torment” of one’s own faults, felt by a person “endowed with a conscience”. Palliative medicine has a name for suffering of this kind: Cicely Saunders’s “total pain”, which includes spiritual and emotional pain alongside physical pain. The texts’ remedy for subtle torment is not denial but change: in chapter 60, souls “delivered from the darkness of these vices through the light of faith ... reckon this as the greatest reward”. The Bahá’í teaching on forgiveness and on the progress of the soul after death is outside this page; see TheSoulAndLifeAfterDeath.

The sentence that needs care. “A person endowed with a conscience will certainly prefer to be killed rather than to sin” (75 ¶1) must not be read as approving of death, or of a dying person’s despair. It describes the scale of values of a conscientious person, in a period when Bahá’ís were being killed for refusing to recant.

11. The suffering of children.

What the texts say. Chapter 66 says children who die young “abide under the shadow of the Divine Providence”. The talks say suffering can perfect the soul (“Men who suffer not, attain no perfection”, Paris Talks 14). Darwin had answered that view in advance: “Some have attempted to explain this with reference to man by imagining that it serves for his moral improvement. But ... they often suffer greatly without any moral improvement” (Autobiography, in Life and Letters, 1887).

What is left open. None of these chapters explains why an infant suffers. Chapter 74 says what evil is; it does not claim to say why God permits each instance of it. Ivan Karamazov’s objection is not answered in these chapters, and a presentation should not pretend that it is.

12. Natural disasters.

What the texts say. An earthquake is a sensible reality; in ch. 74’s terms it is evil “in relation” to those it kills, and good “within the limits of its own being”. Chapter 70 ¶3 classes “misfortune, and material loss” among things man is “compelled to endure” and is “not accountable for”. This is a classification, not a justification; the texts held here do not offer a theodicy of disasters.

What could not be verified

  • Persian readings. The Persian words cited (akhláq-i fiṭrí/irthí/iktisábí, ṭahárat-i khún, nuṭfih-yi ṭayyibih, jins-i a’lá, yak dirham afyún, ḥakím, gáhí, ’adam, amr-i vujúdí/’adamí, bi’n-nisbih, shu’úr, khaṭá, ’adháb-i laṭíf/ghalíẓ, ahl-i Alláh, iḥtijáb) were read from a machine-read scan of the 1908 edition stored in reversed order and normalised for this article. Transliterations are this article’s own and should be checked against the bahai.org Persian text before being shown on screen.
  • “Excellence of the bloodline.” The reading of ¶5 as referring mainly to the parents’ health is this article’s, based on the example that follows. The text does not define the term.
  • Canaan. The identification of ¶7’s Canaan with the Qur’anic son of Noah is an observation from the Persian wording and from Islamic commentary (which commonly, but not unanimously, names the son Kan’án); the 2014 footnote points to Genesis 9.
  • Recantation in 1903. That Bahá’ís in the 1903 persecutions were offered their lives for recanting is stated from general histories; this article did not find it in the corpus for those specific events.
  • Opium in Iran. The statement that opium use was widespread in Iran around 1900 is general historical knowledge, not checked in a source for this article.
  • Afroukhteh’s figure of about three hundred killed in 1903 is his; other accounts give different numbers.
  • Pilgrims’ notes. Rosenberg (1904) and Lucas (1905) are cited only to show that the subject was discussed at the time; their wording is not authenticated.
  • Avicenna. The summary of Avicenna’s treatment of evil (privation, and evil in relation) is from general knowledge of his Metaphysics of The Healing, IX.6; the text was not consulted for this article.
  • Rúmí. The Persian couplets were checked on Ganjoor (Masnavi IV, section 2); the English is this article’s working translation, not Nicholson’s.
  • Medical sources (Hanahan and Weinberg; Oken; Novack; Mack; Polderman; Turkheimer; congenital insensitivity to pain; Montgomery) are cited from abstracts and summaries, not read in full for this article, and describe the field only.
  • No historical connection between ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s wording and Augustine, Aquinas, Avicenna, Leibniz, Eddy, James, Pearson, Galton or the Fitzroy Report is claimed. The parallels and contrasts are observations.
