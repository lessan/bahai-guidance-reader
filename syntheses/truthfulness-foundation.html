Truthfulness, the Foundation of All Human Virtues — and the Practices Attached to It

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The sentence everyone knows — “Truthfulness is the foundation of all human virtues” — is ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s, and it is not a free-standing aphorism. It is the fourth item in a Tablet enumerating seven qualities requested by a believer, sitting between steadfastness and trustworthiness, and it reached English through Shoghi Effendi’s own translation in The Advent of Divine Justice. That placement matters, because it tells you what kind of claim is being made. Truthfulness is not being praised. It is being positioned as load-bearing — the quality without which the others cannot be built.

My position on the central question: the Faith attaches to truthfulness a body of practices that is unusually specific, and the practices share one design signature. They do not add surveillance. They remove the machinery that makes dishonesty profitable, and then leave the individual alone with the obligation. Ḥuqúqu’lláh is self-computed, unsolicited, and cannot be demanded by any institution. Bahá’í elections have no nominations, no candidates, and no campaigning — nothing to be honest about except the elector’s own conscience. Everyone must write a will. Confession to another human being is forbidden, while daily self-accounting is commanded. Backbiting and calumny appear in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas in the same verse as murder and adultery. And the very first unit of the very first institute course operationalises the sentence above as an exercise: list five virtues, then decide whether any of them can be acquired without truthfulness.

Two further claims I will defend. First, the Writings refuse the trade-off between truthfulness and unity that most communities quietly accept. What people usually offer to sacrifice for unity — frank speech about facts — is precisely what unity is made of; what people usually mistake for honesty — candid commentary on persons — is prohibited outright. The line runs between facts and people, not between comfort and truth. Second, the hardest contemporary application is not personal ethics but institutional reporting: statistics, self-assessment, and what a community says about itself. Here the Bahá’í record contains both the model and the unfinished work, and the Riḍván 2021 report — which named, in the same paragraph as its largest achievements, the reason a higher total was not reached — is the model.

The single highest-leverage practical change, for an individual: report the number you actually have, with its date and its method attached. For an institution: never punish an accurate low number. Everything else in this article is downstream of those two.

Where the famous sentence actually comes from

Attribution first, because this is a sentence that circulates without one. It is ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s, from a Tablet answering a request to enumerate seven qualities. The list runs: “First is knowledge: man must attain to the knowledge of God. Second is faith. Third is steadfastness.” Then:

“Fourth is truthfulness, for truthfulness is the foundation of all human virtues. Without truthfulness progress and success, in all the worlds of God, are impossible for any soul. When this holy attribute is established in man, all the divine qualities will also be acquired.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks

The Tablet continues: “Fifth is trustworthiness, a quality which ranketh among the greatest of all divine bestowals. Sixth is faithfulness… Seventh is utter self-effacement.” The published text carries a footnote recording that the sentence beginning “Truthfulness is the foundation” was translated by Shoghi Effendi in The Advent of Divine Justice. So the English formulation everyone quotes is the Guardian’s rendering of a passage he chose to lift out of a longer Tablet and place at the climax of his 1938 letter to North America.

Two things follow. Truthfulness is ranked among qualities, not above them — knowledge and faith precede it — and it is immediately followed by trustworthiness, which is not a synonym. In the Arabic and Persian of the Writings the pair is ṣidq and amánat: truthfulness is the correspondence between what you say and what is; trustworthiness is the correspondence between what is entrusted to you and what you do with it. Bahá’u’lláh keeps them in orbit around each other, and the image He uses is astronomical:

“Say: trustworthiness is the sun of the heaven of My commandments, truthfulness is its moon, and praiseworthy attributes are its stars.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Trustworthiness (from a Tablet, translated from the Arabic)

The same pairing appears in the image the compilation opens with — “Regard thou faith as a tree. Its fruits, leaves, boughs and branches are, and have ever been, trustworthiness, truthfulness, uprightness and forbearance” — and again in a commercial register that is startling in a scripture: “Commerce is as a heaven, whose sun is trustworthiness and whose moon is truthfulness.” Bahá’u’lláh is not decorating a moral exhortation. He is saying that a market is a sky, and that these two qualities are the bodies by which it is lit.

A note on the “Tablet of Trustworthiness”

Readers looking for a Lawḥ-i-Amánat — a Tablet of Trustworthiness — will not find one as a separately published English work, and it is worth saying plainly why. In a Tablet to His Trustee, Ḥájí Amín, Bahá’u’lláh indicates that a Tablet of Trustworthiness had been revealed in AH 1296, around 1879. Adib Taherzadeh, in The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, volume 4, identifies the celebrated trustworthiness passage — the vision of a Maid of Heaven in the Garden of Riḍván outside ‘Akká — as appearing “in the Tablets of Ishráqát, Ṭarázát and also in a Tablet revealed in honour of Ḥájí Mírzá Buzurg-i-Afnán,” one of the custodians of the House of the Báb in Shíráz, and suggests the last of these may be the Tablet of Trustworthiness itself.

The internal evidence supports the dating. In Ṭarázát, Bahá’u’lláh introduces the passage with the words “Not long ago these sublime words were revealed from the Pen of the Most High” — He is quoting Himself, from a recent Tablet. So the material a reader knows as “the Tablet of Trustworthiness” is available, in English, inside Ṭarázát and Ishráqát, and again in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf. What is not available is an identified, separately translated Tablet under that title; and Taherzadeh’s identification is a scholar’s inference, not an institutional determination. This is exactly the sort of thing a synthesis should get right rather than smooth over.

Trustworthiness is given a station, not a recommendation

The passage itself is one of the strangest and most emphatic in the Writings. Bahá’u’lláh describes walking on the Green Island — the Garden of Riḍván outside ‘Akká, where He was permitted to go in the later years of His confinement — and seeing a figure on a pillar of light:

“By God, the True One! I am Trustworthiness and the revelation thereof, and the beauty thereof. I will recompense whosoever will cleave unto Me, and recognize My rank and station, and hold fast unto My hem. I am the most great ornament of the people of Bahá, and the vesture of glory unto all who are in the kingdom of creation. I am the supreme instrument for the prosperity of the world, and the horizon of assurance unto all beings.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Ishráqát

A virtue is here given a face and a voice and permitted to speak in the first person. Nothing else in the Bahá’í Writings is personified quite this way. And the claim it makes about itself is civilisational rather than personal — the supreme instrument for the prosperity of the world. That framing is repeated in the surrounding prose of Ṭarázát, where trustworthiness is the fourth of the Ornaments:

“The fourth Ṭaráz concerneth trustworthiness. Verily it is the door of security for all that dwell on earth and a token of glory on the part of the All-Merciful… Trustworthiness is the greatest portal leading unto the tranquillity and security of the people. In truth the stability of every affair hath depended and doth depend upon it.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Ṭarázát

The stability of every affair. Not of religious affairs. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá draws the consequence with an arithmetic that ought to unsettle anyone who thinks of honesty as one virtue among many:

“If a man were to perform every good work, yet fail in the least scruple to be entirely trustworthy and honest, his good works would become as dry tinder and his failure as a soul-consuming fire. If, on the other hand, he should fall short in all his affairs, yet act with trustworthiness and honesty, all his defects would ultimately be righted… in the sight of God, trustworthiness is the bedrock of His Faith and the foundation of all virtues and perfections.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Trustworthiness (from a Tablet, translated from the Persian)

The same Tablet says that to act otherwise “would be to take an axe to the root of the Cause of God.” And in a Tablet addressed to a believer named Ṣádiq — the name means “truthful” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the strongest statement in the corpus about the relative weight of falsehood:

“If the sum of all sins were to be weighed in the balance, falsehood would, on its own, countervail them; nay, its evils would even outweigh them and its detriment prove greater. It were better for thee that thou shouldst be a blasphemer and tell the truth than that thou shouldst mouth the formulas of faith and yet be a liar.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Trustworthiness (from a Tablet, translated from the Persian)

That sentence is the doctrinal core of everything that follows. A religion which prefers an honest unbeliever to a lying believer has committed itself, structurally, to arrangements in which lying gains nothing.

Rectitude of conduct: the Guardian converts a virtue into a list of acts

The move from exhortation to practice happens on 25 December 1938, in the letter to the believers of North America published as The Advent of Divine Justice. Shoghi Effendi names three spiritual prerequisites for the success of the first Seven Year Plan; the first is rectitude of conduct, and he refuses to leave it abstract:

“This rectitude of conduct, with its implications of justice, equity, truthfulness, honesty, fair-mindedness, reliability, and trustworthiness, must distinguish every phase of the life of the Bahá’í community.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice

Seven nouns, and then — after pages of quotation from Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá — an enumeration of the places where those nouns must show up. It is worth reading as an audit list rather than as prose. Rectitude must appear “in every verdict which the elected representatives of the Bahá’í community… may be called upon to pronounce”; “constantly reflected in the business dealings of all its members, in their domestic lives, in all manner of employment, and in any service they may, in the future, render their government or people”; “exemplified in the conduct of all Bahá’í electors, when exercising their sacred rights and functions”; and — the item most often skipped —

“It must be demonstrated in the impartiality of every defender of the Faith against its enemies, in his fair-mindedness in recognizing any merits that enemy may possess, and in his honesty in discharging any obligations he may have towards him.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice

Owing a debt to someone attacking your religion, and paying it. That is a testable standard, and it is the point at which rectitude stops being a disposition and becomes a practice. Six years earlier, writing to the believers of North America on 21 March 1932, Shoghi Effendi had set the same standard in its negative form — a requirement about what must never be alleged: “Let them so shape their lives and regulate their conduct that no charge of secrecy, of fraud, of bribery or of intimidation may, however ill-founded, be brought against them.” The clause however ill-founded is the demanding part. The standard is not innocence; it is conduct arranged so that the accusation cannot get purchase.

The Guardian closes the section by observing that the institution which crowns the Administrative Order takes its name from this quality: the House of Justice, “whose very name and title… symbolizes that rectitude of conduct which is its highest mission to safeguard and enforce.”

Seventy-two years later the Universal House of Justice picked the passage up again and re-aimed it. Writing to the Conference of the Continental Boards of Counsellors on 28 December 2010, it rehearsed the Guardian’s three prerequisites and then made the comparative judgement explicit:

“If indeed great effort was required for the friends to uphold the high standards of the Faith then, how much greater must be the exertion in a world that rewards dishonesty, that encourages corruption, and that treats truth as a negotiable commodity.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (Framework for Action)

The addressees are named, and the naming is the practice. Not “the friends” in general, but “the coordinators of every training institute, the members of every Area Teaching Committee, every Auxiliary Board member and every one of his or her assistants, and all members of every local, regional and national Bahá’í body, whether elected or appointed” — with the instruction that they act “lest the slightest trace of self-interest becloud their judgment.” Rectitude, in the 2010 restatement, is primarily an institutional discipline. It attaches to the people who hold information, allocate resources, and write reports.

The practices that only work if you are truthful

Here is the answer to the central question, and it is more concrete than the exhortations suggest. The Faith builds obligations that cannot be enforced from outside, and then declines to build the enforcement. Each one is a standing test of the individual’s truthfulness, and each is designed so that dishonesty gains the dishonest person nothing an institution could ever detect.

An unauditable tax

Ḥuqúqu’lláh — the Right of God, nineteen mithqáls of gold in every hundred, payable on what remains once yearly expenses and specified exemptions are deducted — is the clearest case. Bahá’u’lláh forbids the obvious enforcement mechanism outright:

“Time and again have We written and commanded that no one should solicit such payment. The offering of every person that voluntarily tendereth the Ḥuqúqu’lláh with the utmost joy and pleasure may be accepted, otherwise acceptance was not and is not permissible.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Ḥuqúqu’lláh: The Right of God

The Universal House of Justice has restated the consequence without softening it: “The payment of Ḥuqúqu’lláh is a personal obligation on each Bahá’í, and it is for him to meet this obligation in accordance with his own conscience; it cannot be demanded from him by any of the institutions of the Faith.” The computation itself is left open at exactly the point where self-interest operates. Bahá’u’lláh exempts the principal residence and such household and professional equipment as are needful — and “it is left to the individual to decide which items are necessary and which are not.”

The failure mode is not left to the reader’s imagination. In a letter of 26 February 1973 the House of Justice names it:

“The same principle applies to those friends who spend lavishly on their families, who purchase or build residences and furnish them far in excess of their needs, and rationalize these expenditures in their desire to avoid payment of Ḥuqúqu’lláh.” — The Universal House of Justice, 26 February 1973 (Ḥuqúqu’lláh: The Right of God)

Rationalisation, not evasion. The law is constructed so that the only person who can cheat it is the one keeping the accounts, and the only lie available is the one told to oneself. That is a practice attached to truthfulness — the practice is the annual computation, and its integrity has no external referee. The mechanics are treated in huqququllah-not-a-tax.

An election with nothing to be dishonest about

Bahá’í elections remove, by design, almost every surface on which electoral dishonesty normally forms. There are no nominations, no candidates, no platforms, no canvassing, and the ballot is secret. Shoghi Effendi’s rejection of nomination is not procedural preference but doctrine: the practice is “in fundamental disaccord with the spirit which should animate and direct all elections held by the Bahá’ís,” and it has “the great disadvantage of killing in the believer the spirit of initiative, and of self-development.” The House of Justice is blunt about what remains prohibited:

“Let them exercise the utmost vigilance so that the elections are carried out freely, universally and by secret ballot. Any form of intrigue, deception, collusion and compulsion must be stopped and is forbidden.” — The Sanctity and Nature of Bahá’í Elections

What is left is one instruction to the elector, from the Guardian’s letter of 3 June 1925 to the delegates and visitors at the North American convention: to consider “without the least trace of passion and prejudice, and irrespective of any material consideration, the names of only those who can best combine the necessary qualities of unquestioned loyalty, of selfless devotion, of a well-trained mind, of recognized ability and mature experience.” That is an entirely interior standard. No observer can verify that a ballot was cast that way.

And the system’s trust in the elector’s conscience goes further than most Bahá’ís realise. The guidance states that “a believer has the right to vote for himself during the election time, if he conscientiously feels the urge to do so,” adding that this “does not necessarily imply that he is ambitious or selfish.” A system anxious about honesty would have forbidden it. This one leaves it to the voter and declines to police the motive.

A document that speaks after you cannot

Bahá’u’lláh makes will-writing universal: “Unto everyone hath been enjoined the writing of a will. The testator should head this document with the adornment of the Most Great Name, bear witness therein unto the oneness of God in the Dayspring of His Revelation” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶109). A Bahá’í will is therefore a truth-telling instrument that operates when its author can no longer be questioned — and the law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh runs straight into it: a believer is obliged to provide in the will for whatever balance of the Right of God remains unpaid at death, and under the law of intestacy “division of the estate should take place only after the Ḥuqúqu’lláh hath been paid.” The self-assessed obligation is carried past the point where self-interest can operate. See bahai-wills-and-estate-planning and inheritance-and-family-wealth.

A daily audit that may not become a performance

Bahá’u’lláh commands the internal accounting — “O SON OF BEING! Bring thyself to account each day ere thou art summoned to a reckoning” (The Hidden Words, Arabic 31) — and then closes the exit that other traditions leave open. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶34 states that “to none is it permitted to seek absolution from another soul.” The notes explain that in the Tablet of Bishárát Bahá’u’lláh gives the reason: “such confession before people results in one’s humiliation and abasement,” and God “wisheth not the humiliation of His servants.” Shoghi Effendi’s secretary sets the boundary precisely — believers are forbidden to confess sins to a person or in public, “however, if we spontaneously desire to acknowledge we have been wrong in something, or that we have some fault of character, and ask another person’s forgiveness or pardon, we are quite free to do so.” The House of Justice has further clarified that the prohibition “does not prevent an individual from admitting transgressions in the course of consultations held under the aegis of Bahá’í institutions,” nor from seeking advice from a friend or a professional counsellor.

Read the two provisions together and a design becomes visible. Rigorous self-examination is required; its conversion into a social performance is forbidden. Apology is free; confession as ritual is not. A community in which people compete to disclose their faults would be producing something other than truthfulness, and the law forecloses it.

Debts, and the honesty of the creditor

The compilation includes a Tablet in which Bahá’u’lláh addresses debt from both directions at once: “anyone who hath the ability to settle his debts, and yet neglecteth to do so, hath not acted in accordance with the good pleasure of the one true God,” and then immediately — “these matters, however, depend on the existence of ability, for the making of a demand is contingent upon ability to meet it,” citing the Qur’ánic verse “Respite thy debtor till he findeth means to pay.” The Báb’s example in The Dawn-Breakers holds the same balance: rejecting an overpriced purchase of honey, He instructs, “You must neither defraud your neighbour nor allow him to defraud you.” Honesty in a transaction is a two-sided obligation; being cheated is not a form of virtue. This register is developed in bahai-owned-business-conduct and debt-loans-and-mortgages.

The curriculum

The most underrated practice is educational, and it is the one that reaches the largest number of people. The very first unit of Ruhi Book 1, Reflections on the Life of the Spirit — the entry point to the institute process for every participant worldwide — presents the sentence for memorisation and then converts it into an exercise: “Truthfulness is the foundation of all human virtues. List five virtues… Can we acquire these virtues without truthfulness?” The tutor’s guidance in Book 7 specifies that this is not an opinion question: participants “are encouraged to name five virtues and then decide whether it is possible to acquire any of them in the absence of truthfulness.”

The same sentence anchors a Grade 1 children’s-class lesson in Ruhi Book 3, with a script that trades entirely in concrete cases — a broken glass admitted to, unfinished homework declared to a teacher who will be displeased — and a definition of the foundation image drawn from a father laying rocks and cement before the walls go up. And in the junior-youth text Walking the Straight Path, written for twelve-to fourteen-year-olds, the memorisation passage is preceded by a single discussion question that is the whole subject in miniature: “Why is it necessary to have courage in order to always tell the truth?”

Three age bands, one sentence, and in each case an operation rather than an admiration. That is what it looks like when a community attaches a practice to a principle.

What truthfulness forbids: the law of speech

Truthfulness in the Writings has a prohibitive half, and it is legislated at the highest level of severity. In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶19, backbiting is not placed among counsels of refinement:

“Ye have been forbidden to commit murder or adultery, or to engage in backbiting or calumny; shun ye, then, what hath been prohibited in the holy Books and Tablets.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶19

Four prohibitions, one verse. The notes gather the reinforcements: “How couldst thou forget thine own faults and busy thyself with the faults of others? Whoso doeth this is accursed of Me” and “Breathe not the sins of others so long as thou art thyself a sinner” (The Hidden Words, Arabic 26 and 27) — and then the Kitáb-i-‘Ahd, Bahá’u’lláh’s last work: “Verily I say, the tongue is for mentioning what is good, defile it not with unseemly talk… everyone should utter that which is meet and seemly, and should refrain from slander, abuse and whatever causeth sadness in men.” Elsewhere Bahá’u’lláh gives the reason in terms of the speaker rather than the target: “backbiting quencheth the light of the heart, and extinguisheth the life of the soul” (Gleanings).

Against this sits a second body of guidance that is often misused. Bahá’u’lláh does place a governor on speech:

“Human utterance is an essence which aspireth to exert its influence and needeth moderation… Every word is endowed with a spirit, therefore the speaker or expounder should carefully deliver his words at the appropriate time and place… One word may be likened unto fire, another unto light.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd

The pastoral caveat is necessary here, because the counterfeit is close by. Moderation of utterance governs timing, manner and audience. It does not license accuracy to be shaded. A believer who softens a figure, omits an unwelcome fact, or lets a false impression stand, and calls it wisdom, has borrowed the language of one teaching to break another. The fifth Ṭaráz settles the direction of the rule in a single line before it turns to abuse: “One should not ignore the truth of any matter, rather should one give expression to that which is right and true… should not defile their tongues with abuse.” Speak the truth of the matter; do not abuse the person. Both halves, in one sentence, in that order.

The community’s actual failure mode, in my observation, runs opposite to the guidance on both counts: blunt about people, vague about facts. The texts ask for the reverse.

Truthfulness and unity are not in tension — the texts say so structurally

The intuition that honesty threatens unity is old and, in most organisations, correct. The Bahá’í texts deny it, and they deny it by specifying where the candour goes.

Consultation is the designated arena, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s instruction gives frankness the strongest possible warrant:

“The members thereof must take counsel together in such wise that no occasion for ill-feeling or discord may arise. This can be attained when every member expresseth with absolute freedom his own opinion and setteth forth his argument. Should anyone oppose, he must on no account feel hurt for not until matters are fully discussed can the right way be revealed. The shining spark of truth cometh forth only after the clash of differing opinions.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá

Note the order of the clauses. Freedom of expression is presented not as a risk to be managed for the sake of harmony but as the means by which harmony without ill-feeling is attained. The prerequisites ‘Abdu’l-Bahá attaches — “purity of motive, radiance of spirit, detachment from all else save God… humility and lowliness amongst His loved ones, patience and long-suffering in difficulties” — are conditions on how one speaks, not permissions to withhold. The full treatment belongs to consultation; what matters here is the architecture, and the architecture is: maximum candour about the matter, inside a bounded forum, followed by unity in execution, with the deliberations held confidential.

The complementary provision is the one that makes the candour survivable. In a letter of 19 April 2013 the House of Justice describes the community as an environment “in which mistakes are treated with tolerance and fear of failure is diminished; in which criticism of others is avoided and backbiting and gossip give way to mutual support and encouragement.” Read as social engineering rather than as sentiment, that is a precise claim: you cannot get honest reporting out of a community that punishes the reporter. Remove the fear of failure and the incentive to misreport goes with it. Keep the fear, and no amount of exhortation to truthfulness will produce accurate numbers.

The same letter defines the two opposite failures with unusual sharpness — believers are to “steer clear of the pitfalls of hypocrisy, on the one hand—that is, saying one thing yet doing another—and heedlessness, on the other—that is, disregard for the laws, ignoring or explaining away the need to follow them,” while avoiding “paralysis engendered by guilt.” Hypocrisy is defined operationally, not psychologically: the gap between word and deed. It is the same gap Bahá’u’lláh names in the passage the Guardian chose for The Advent of Divine Justice: “Beware, O people of Bahá, lest ye walk in the ways of them whose words differ from their deeds… It is through your deeds that ye can distinguish yourselves from others.”

Finally, the link the Writings make between trustworthiness and the Covenant closes the circle: “Whoso hath remained faithful to the Covenant hath been steadfast in his adherence to trustworthiness, whilst those who have repudiated it have erred grievously.” Fidelity to a promise and accuracy in speech are, in this framing, the same disposition operating on different objects — which is why ‘Abdu’l-Bahá can say that a believer whose conduct is distinguished by “his truthfulness and loving-kindness towards all the peoples of the world” has thereby “been faithful to the Covenant and Testament of God,” and that one whose conduct is not, has not. The unity of the community rests on the same quality that makes an individual’s report reliable.

The hardest case: the community’s own numbers

If truthfulness is the foundation, the load-bearing test in an era of plans and cycles is institutional reporting. Here the guidance is specific, the practice is real, and the honest account includes an unfinished piece.

The instrument. The Universal House of Justice wrote to the National Spiritual Assembly of India on 23 July 2013 on the collection and utilisation of statistics, and the letter is notable for what it refuses to claim. Data gathering is described as “a natural part of the everyday activities that take place as the friends carry out their work” — coordinators who accompany children’s-class teachers “would be aware of the number of such classes and the approximate number of children attending each one.” The Statistical Report Program is named as the instrument, and reports are pooled at points in the three-month cycle. Then the limiting sentence:

“It is apparent that the collection of data is the result of the organic process of growth in each cluster and constitutes only one means, albeit a critical one, for assessing the progress of the Cause and the community-building process under way.” — The Universal House of Justice, 23 July 2013 (Messages 2001–2022)

An instruction to collect numbers that contains, in the same paragraph, a statement of what numbers cannot tell you. That is honest measurement culture in one sentence — and note the word approximate. The guidance does not ask for a precision the situation cannot bear.

The forum. The reflection meeting is where the numbers meet the community, and the House of Justice specified the temper required as early as 27 December 2005: “To arrive at a unified vision of growth based on a realistic assessment of possibilities and resources, to develop strategies that lend structure to it, to devise and implement plans of action commensurate with capacity, to make necessary adjustments while maintaining continuity, to build on accomplishments—these are some of the requisites of systematization that every community must learn and internalize.” Realistic and commensurate with capacity are the operative words, and they cut against the two standard distortions of a reflection gathering: the inflated report and the demoralised one. See reflection-meeting-planning.

The model. The Riḍván 2021 message reporting the close of the 2016–2021 Five Year Plan is, in my judgement, the best available example of the standard being met. The figures are exact and dated. At the Plan’s start, the capacity existed for conducting “just over 100,000 core activities at a given time”; by its close, “300,000 core activities are being sustained at once,” with participation “risen above two million.” There were 329 national and regional training institutes in operation; three-quarters of a million people had completed at least one book of the sequence; the number of courses completed stood at two million. Intensive programmes of growth “more than doubled and now stands at approximately 4,000.” Clusters past the third milestone rose from “around 200, spread across some 40 countries” to “1,000 in nearly 100 countries.” Over 30 clusters were sustaining more than 1,000 core activities each, in places involving “the participation of more than 20,000 people in a single cluster.”

And then, without prompting and in the middle of the good news:

“Difficulties involved in opening up new villages and neighbourhoods to the Faith in the midst of a global health crisis, or expanding activities that were at an early stage when the pandemic began, prevented an even higher total from being reached during the Plan’s final year.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2021 (Messages 2001–2022)

The shortfall is named in the same breath as the achievement, with its cause attached. No institution that wished to flatter itself would have written that sentence. It is the practice of truthfulness performed at the level of a global report, and it is the template any Assembly, committee or coordinator can copy directly: state the number, state the gap, state why.

The unfinished piece. Against that, the community’s public-facing headline figure is weaker than its internal reporting. The Bahá’í World News Service statistics page states that “there are more than 5 million Bahá’ís in the world,” that Bahá’ís “reside in well over 100,000 localities,” that there are “currently 188 councils at the national level,” and that “about 2,100 indigenous tribes, races, and ethnic groups are represented.” No date, no method, and no source is given for any of them; the five-million formulation has been in circulation since the early 1990s, while external demographic databases — the World Religion Database, ARDA, and the projections derived from them — have for years placed the figure higher, in the seven-to-eight-million range, using methods of their own that no one can check against Bahá’í rolls, because no unified global roll exists.

I want to be precise about what is and is not a problem here. The decision not to manage the community by headcount is defensible and probably right, and it is deliberate: the House of Justice publishes participation, clusters, milestones and institute capacity rather than membership, because those are the quantities its plans actually act upon. Enrolment rolls in a Faith with no mechanism for lapsing would be the less honest number. The companion analysis in how-many-bahais-worldwide works this through, and ridvan-2021-headline-statistic traces what the modern figures do and do not say. The problem is narrower and fixable: a round number repeated for three decades without a date or a stated method is a weak instrument, and the standard the community applies to its own cluster statistics — approximate, dated, method attached — is the standard it should apply to the sentence that most outsiders will ever read about it.

The strongest countermove available. The practice that most decisively demonstrates honest self-assessment is submitting your own claims to someone else’s measurement. The Kimanya-Ngeyo Foundation for Science and Education in Uganda — a Bahá’í-inspired agency running the Preparation for Social Action curriculum — did exactly that, opening its teacher-training programme to a randomised controlled trial run by Vesall Nourani, Nava Ashraf and Abhijit Banerjee, pre-registered and reported in February 2025. The intervention raised the pass rate on the national exam determining progression from primary to secondary school from 51% to 75%, placing it in the top five percentile of rigorously evaluated education interventions on learning-adjusted years of schooling per dollar, with effects persisting four years after the first cohort of teachers was trained. The authors’ acknowledgements record the reason the study was possible: the Foundation’s “commitment to systematically learning about their own activities.”

This is field description, not Bahá’í authority, and the finding is one programme in one country. But as evidence about practice it is exactly on point. An organisation confident in its own honesty hands the measuring instrument to an outsider.

The world this standard now meets

The corpus supplies the principle; it cannot describe the condition of the field in 2026, and the external picture is worth stating precisely — as description only, never as ground for a Bahá’í teaching.

Transparency International’s Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 covers 182 countries and territories on a 0–100 scale drawn from 13 independent data sources. The global average fell to 42, the first decline in more than a decade; 122 countries score below 50; and the number of countries scoring above 80 has fallen from 12 a decade ago to five. The World Economic Forum’s Global Risks Report 2026, its twenty-first edition, ranks misinformation and disinformation second among severe global risks on the two-year horizon — the third consecutive year they have appeared near the top — and fourth on the ten-year horizon. The 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer, based on 30-minute online interviews with nearly 34,000 respondents across 28 countries between 23 October and 18 November 2025, reports that seven in ten are unwilling or hesitant to trust someone with different values, background or information sources, with insularity highest in developed markets (Japan 90%, Germany 81%); “my employer” is the most trusted institution at 78%, against 53% for government.

Set the 1938 letter beside those figures. Shoghi Effendi wrote of “the demoralizing influences which a corruption-ridden political life so strikingly manifests” and asked for a rectitude “which, in all its manifestations, offers a striking contrast to the deceitfulness and corruption that characterize the political life of the nation.” The House of Justice’s 2010 phrase — a world that treats truth as a negotiable commodity — was written before generative models made synthetic text and video cheap. What was a moral diagnosis in 1938 and a warning in 2010 is now a measured condition with an index number attached.

If you build software or work with data, the leverage here is unusual and specific. Nearly every reporting tool in the world is optimised for the person being reported about; its defaults flatter. The needed instrument is the opposite: a system whose defaults record what happened rather than what was hoped, which timestamps and versions every figure so that a revision is visible rather than silent, which carries the collection method alongside the number, and which makes “we don’t know” a first-class value rather than a blank that gets filled with an optimistic estimate. Provenance and verification tooling — for images, documents, and increasingly for text — is the same discipline applied to the incoming direction. If you are a Bahá’í with these skills, the Statistical Report Program’s descendants, cluster reflection materials, and any Assembly’s record-keeping are all within reach, and the design principle is one line long: make the honest report the easy one.

The non-technical paths are equally real and much less glamorous. Treasurer of a Local Spiritual Assembly. Secretary, keeping minutes that record what was decided rather than what one wished had been decided. Tutor of a Book 1 study circle, where the sentence at the head of this article is first put to work. These are the roles where the standard is actually kept or lost.

Taking a position: what to do on Monday

Attach a date and a method to every number you report. “Fourteen participants, counted at the reflection meeting on 12 July, from the coordinator’s list” is a truthful statement. “About twenty” is not, unless it is genuinely an estimate and says so. The 23 July 2013 letter’s word is approximate — use it when it is true, and don’t use it as camouflage.

Say the shortfall in the same sentence as the achievement. Riḍván 2021 is the template. If the number is lower than last cycle, the report says so and says why, before anyone asks. An institution that learns this once never has to manage a credibility problem again.

Separate facts from persons, in that order. Full candour about what happened; nothing about who is to blame. This is not diplomacy — it is ¶19 on one side and the fifth Ṭaráz on the other. In practice it means the sentence “the children’s class stopped in March” is required, and the sentence “because she lost interest” is forbidden.

Compute your Ḥuqúqu’lláh yourself, and be suspicious of your own exemptions. The 26 February 1973 letter named the mechanism: lavish provision followed by rationalisation. The annual computation is the most private test of truthfulness the Faith administers, and it is the one nobody will ever check.

Vote without consulting anyone. No lists, no “who are you thinking of”, no group messages before a convention. The election’s honesty is not protected by rules — there are almost none — but by every elector’s individual refusal to confer.

Write the will. ¶109 makes it universal, and it is the one act of truthfulness that operates entirely after you have lost the ability to explain yourself.

Bring yourself to account daily, and do not turn it into a performance. The account is owed to God, apology is owed to the person wronged, and disclosure in consultation is permitted where it serves the matter. Public self-accusation is none of those things, and ¶34 forbids it.

If you serve on an institution: never punish an accurate low number. This is the single highest-leverage institutional practice on the list, and everything else depends on it. A community that receives a disappointing report with tolerance will keep getting true reports. A community that receives it with disappointment will, within two cycles, stop receiving reports that are true. The 19 April 2013 letter describes the environment required — “mistakes are treated with tolerance and fear of failure is diminished” — and it is describing a precondition for accurate data, not merely a pleasant atmosphere.

The measure of all of it is not scrupulosity. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s account of what truthfulness does is protective rather than punitive: “A truthful person will be protected from all moral afflictions, will shrink from every evil deed, and be preserved from every wicked act, inasmuch as all vices and misdeeds are the very antithesis of truthfulness.” The claim is that the other virtues become available once this one is fixed — which is what “foundation” meant all along. You do not decorate a foundation. You build on it.

Open questions / honest limits

  • The Lawḥ-i-Amánat is not available as an identified, separately published English text. What circulates as “the Tablet of Trustworthiness” is the Green Island passage, which appears in Ṭarázát, in Ishráqát, and in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf. The dating — AH 1296, around 1879, indicated in a Tablet to Ḥájí Amín — and the identification with the Tablet revealed for Ḥájí Mírzá Buzurg-i-Afnán come from Adib Taherzadeh’s The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, volume 4. That is a scholar’s reconstruction, not an institutional determination, and this page treats it as such.
  • Provenance in the Trustworthiness compilation is thin by design. A large majority of its extracts from Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá are marked only “From a Tablet—translated from the Persian” or “…from the Arabic”, with no recipient, date, or published location. They are authenticated by the Research Department of the Universal House of Justice, which is sufficient for citation; they are not locatable to a named Tablet, which means a reader cannot check them in context. Quotations in this article carry the identification the compilation itself provides, and no more.
  • The Writings do not settle the hard disclosure cases. What to say when telling the truth endangers a third party; whether and when silence constitutes deception; how far “moderation” in utterance may bound frankness before it becomes evasion — none of these is resolved in the corpus, and this page has not invented an answer. The one adjacent ruling that is unambiguous is situational: the House of Justice’s letter of 1 October 2014 to the Bahá’ís of Iran commends the Bahá’ís of Iran for refusing “to deny or dissimulate” their faith even when a single false statement “would open up to you every social and economic opportunity” — a refusal to lie about one’s own belief under coercion. It does not generalise into a rule governing all disclosure.
  • The community’s headline population figure has no published methodology. “More than 5 million” appears on the Bahá’í World News Service statistics page without a date or a source; external estimates run higher and use methods that cannot be reconciled against Bahá’í records because no unified global roll exists. This page treats the discrepancy as a reporting-practice question, not as evidence of misstatement, and takes no position on the true number.
  • The external data will age, and each source has a known weakness. The Corruption Perceptions Index measures perceptions of public-sector corruption, not corruption; the Edelman Trust Barometer is an online panel commissioned by a communications firm; the WEF risk rankings are an opinion survey of its own network. The Uganda randomised trial is a single evaluation of a single programme, and its authors, not this page, are responsible for its claims. None of it grounds a Bahá’í principle.
  • This page does not cover consultation in depth — see consultation — nor covenant-breaking, nor the detailed computation of Ḥuqúqu’lláh, nor the professional cases of disclosure treated in whistleblowing-workplace-integrity and bahai-conduct-in-workplace.
