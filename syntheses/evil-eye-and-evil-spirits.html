The Evil Eye, Evil Spirits, and the Will of Others: What the Bahá’í Writings Say

Last updated 12 September 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í writings address the fear of the evil eye, curses, and malevolent spirits by distinguishing between supernatural claims and psychological reality.

The writings reject the belief that glances, curses, or disembodied spirits possess occult power. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá stated directly that “the eye possesseth no such influence” and characterized the belief as “mere superstition.” He explained that scriptural references to evil spirits, demons, and Satan are symbolic representations of the lower nature in human beings, not independent malevolent entities. Similarly, departed souls do not assume physical bodies or exercise power over the living: “Good is stronger than evil and even when alive they had very little power.”

At the same time, the writings explain why people experience genuine distress and illness. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá noted that mutual impressions between people are real, and described the psychological mechanism in detail: when an individual believes in the evil eye and encounters someone reputed to possess it, the resulting anxiety and expectation of calamity cause sleeplessness and distress. “These are the impressions that are caused, and these the occurrences which result.” The Universal House of Justice applied this same explanation to witchcraft, ju-ju, and voodoo, stating that “the influence of such ‘arts’ is dependent on the conviction, even the sub-conscious conviction, of the person affected.” While evil has no independent substantive existence, its effects are real, just as darkness is the absence of light.

Protection consists of spiritual steadfastness, prayer, medical treatment, and practical service. The Bahá’í teachings do not offer counter-charms, exorcisms, or magical rituals. Bahá’u’lláh stated: “Man is the supreme Talisman.” The primary safeguard is an internal condition of trust in God, supported by revealed prayers, the remembrance of God, competent medical care for physical and psychological symptoms, and active involvement in service. When ‘Abdu’l-Bahá faced twenty-four years of persistent hostility from an Afghan resident in ‘Akká who publicly denounced Him and avoided looking at Him in the street, He responded by providing the man with food, clothing, medical care, and financial assistance until the man expressed regret and became His friend. The hostility produced no harm, while practical goodwill resolved the enmity.

The nature of the fear

Belief in the evil eye and malevolent spirits typically links several distinct experiences.

The first is the association between praise and subsequent misfortune. When an admired child falls ill, a business fails, or a marriage ends, observers often conclude that admiration or envy caused the loss. This leads to the assumption that receiving attention or praise attracts harm.

The second involves acute physical sensations and nighttime distress. Individuals may awaken unable to move, feeling pressure in the chest and sensing a threatening presence in the room. Others experience distressing nightmares or sudden episodes of panic that they interpret as attacks by unseen entities.

These emotional and physical experiences are real to the people who undergo them. The critical question is what causes these phenomena and how to respond to them. The Bahá’í writings separate the physical and psychological facts from the supernatural interpretations attached to them.

The Islamic and scriptural context

In Islamic tradition, belief in the evil eye and occult forces is grounded in specific passages of the Qur’án and the hadith collections.

The Qur’án concludes with two súrahs of refuge (al-mu’awwidhatayn). In Súrat al-Falaq, believers seek refuge “from the evil of the envier when he envies” (113:5). Classical commentators such as al-Ṭabarí and al-Qurṭubí defined ḥasad (envy) as the desire that another person be deprived of a blessing, and commentators such as al-Sa’dí interpreted the verse to encompass the eye (al-’ayn) as the instrument of envy. Súrat al-Qalam similarly states: “those who disbelieve would almost make you slip with their eyes” (68:51). The hadith literature contains explicit statements. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records the tradition al-’aynu ḥaqq (“The evil eye is real. If anything could precede the divine decree, it would be preceded by the evil eye,” Muslim 2188); a related narration from Abū Hurayra appears in al-Bukhārī. The recitation of protective prayers (ruqyah) was permitted for this purpose. The Qur’án also mentions jinn as a distinct creation (súrah 72) and refers to sorcery (siḥr) in the account of Babylon (2:102).

Two points clarify how the Bahá’í teachings approach these texts:

First, Bahá’ís accept the Qur’án as revealed scripture and Muḥammad as a Manifestation of God. The Báb describes the Qur’án as the Book “which is free from error.” The Bahá’í approach to these passages is interpretive rather than dismissive. Bahá’í hermeneutics treats scriptural references to demons, devils, and occult forces as symbolic. When asked about Gospel accounts of individuals possessed by devils, Shoghi Effendi explained through his secretary that these descriptions should be understood figuratively, with Satan symbolizing the lower nature and evil inclinations. The same symbolic understanding applies to scriptural references to malevolent spirits across religious traditions.

Second, the Qur’án itself subordinates any effect of sorcery to the will of God. Súrat al-Baqarah states that practitioners of magic “could not harm anyone except by God’s permission” (2:102). Even within this scriptural framework, occult harm is not an autonomous power. In addition, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá pointed out that accusations of sorcery and demonic possession were consistently made by deniers against the Prophets themselves—such as claiming that the Prophet was “a man enchanted,” had “a jinn in him,” or was taught by a sorcerer. In scripture, the vocabulary of the occult was frequently used by opponents to dismiss divine revelation.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablets on the evil eye

‘Abdu’l-Bahá addressed the evil eye by name in two Tablets. The Persian originals are published in Ganjíniy-i-Ḥudúd va Aḥkám (p. 454) and Makátíb-i-Ḥaḍrat-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá (vol. 3, pp. 257–58); the English translations below are provisional renderings by Adib Masumian.

In the first Tablet, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá responds to a direct inquiry about whether the evil eye has any effect:

“Thou hadst written about the malevolent glare, which is to say afflictions that proceed from the eye … to ask whether or not ‘the evil eye’ can exert an effect. The eye possesseth no such influence, but there is a consummate wisdom in the impressions which people give and receive. This applieth generally. However, souls that are firm and steadfast are protected from such misfortune. Should such a one feel affected somehow by this superstition, let them recite ‘Yá Alláhu’l-Mustagháth’ ninety-five times.”

In this passage, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá denies that the human eye possesses any power to inflict harm. He affirms that mutual impressions between individuals occur, identifies spiritual steadfastness as protection, and recommends reciting “Yá Alláhu’l-Mustagháth” (“O God, the Invoked”) ninety-five times for anyone troubled by feelings arising from the belief.

In the second Tablet, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá explains the psychological process in detail:

“O servant of God! Thou hadst written about the benevolent gaze and the malevolent glare—in other words, afflictions that proceed from the eye. This is mere superstition, but there are feelings that result from this superstition which take hold in people. Those feelings, in turn, can give rise to certain impressions. Suppose, for example, there is someone who becomes reputed to have ‘the evil eye,’ and another person who believeth, with certainty, in such influences of the eye. When the person infamous for having ‘the evil eye’ looketh at this hapless soul, he, being superstitious, will grow anxious; he will have no peace of mind, waiting constantly for a calamity to befall him. These are the impressions that are caused, and these the occurrences which result. It is not the case that some misfortune or other proceeded from the eye of the former and affected the latter. If, therefore, such a thought as this should cross one’s mind: ‘So-and-so is evil-eyed and hath glared at me,’ let that one engage immediately in the remembrance of God, in order that this superstition may fade away from their heart.”

In this explanation, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá outlines a clear sequence: an individual who believes in the evil eye becomes anxious when looked upon by someone reputed to have it; this anxiety produces constant dread and expectation of misfortune; this mental state leads to real physical and psychological consequences. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá explicitly rejects the idea that harm passes between the two individuals: “It is not the case that some misfortune or other proceeded from the eye of the former and affected the latter.” His instruction is to turn immediately to the remembrance of God when such a thought arises, preventing the superstition from taking hold.

Mind, body, and the limits of interpersonal influence

The Bahá’í writings describe how psychological states produce physical symptoms. In Some Answered Questions, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá explains the relationship between emotional shock and physical health:

“when a terrifying event suddenly comes to pass, such an excitement may be produced in the nerves of a healthy person that he immediately falls ill. The cause of the illness is not a material thing, for that person has not ingested or come into contact with anything: The nervous excitement alone has brought about the illness.” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 72)

Intense fear and nervous excitation can directly cause physical illness. When someone believes they have been targeted by an evil eye or a curse, the resulting anxiety and dread can produce real physical ailments, even though no supernatural influence was transmitted.

In the same chapter, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá defines the limits of what one person’s spiritual focus can do for another’s physical condition. Even when a healer directs strong, deliberate concentration toward a patient who expects to be healed, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states that “this is effective only to a point and not in all cases,” and that “these means have no sway over grave illnesses.” If intentional, cooperative spiritual effort cannot heal severe illness, an envious glance from another person cannot inflict serious illness or physical harm.

Regarding the concern that other people can read or control one’s thoughts, a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 18 January 1951 states:

“You should not be afraid any one can affect your mind. Even when we want to catch the thoughts of those we love most we cannot do so, how much less can other people succeed in penetrating our minds.”

Human beings cannot telepathically read or manipulate another person’s mind. When troubled by intrusive or negative thoughts, believers were advised not to concentrate on them or struggle against them directly, but to redirect their attention toward God. A pilgrim’s note records ‘Abdu’l-Bahá advising that when unwanted thoughts occur, one should turn the heart toward God rather than fighting the thoughts, which only increases mental distress (Daily Lessons Received at Akka, p. 86). While pilgrims’ notes do not constitute authoritative text, this counsel matches ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablet recommending immediate remembrance of God to dispel superstitious thoughts.

Evil spirits, Satan, and the deceased

The Bahá’í writings state that malevolent disembodied entities do not exist.

In a 1972 letter to a National Spiritual Assembly concerning traditional beliefs in ju-ju, the Universal House of Justice quoted ‘Abdu’l-Bahá:

“As to the question of evil spirits, demons and monsters, any references made to them in the Holy Books have symbolic meaning. What is currently known among the public is but sheer superstition.” (18 July 1972)

In The Promulgation of Universal Peace, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá clarifies the meaning of these scriptural terms:

“The reality underlying this question is that the evil spirit, Satan or whatever is interpreted as evil, refers to the lower nature in man. … God has never created an evil spirit; all such ideas and nomenclature are symbols expressing the mere human or earthly nature of man.” (p. 295)

The concept of an independent evil power opposed to God is replaced by the concept of the lower nature, which requires moral and spiritual education. In a 1974 letter, the Universal House of Justice reiterated this principle:

“the concept of Satan or the Devil as an actual being opposed to God is rejected by the Bahá’í teachings but … the term is understood by Bahá’ís to mean the promptings of self and desire and the dark side of human nature.” (13 February 1974)

The Bahá’í writings also reject the idea that deceased souls can return to haunt, harm, or possess the living. First, departed souls do not take on physical bodies: “When the souls leave the bodies, they do not assume elemental bodies. Whatever man thinks regarding this is but his own imagination” (Daily Lessons Received at Akka, p. 78). Second, departed souls have no power to harm the living. In a pilgrim’s note, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is recorded as stating: “There is no power exercised over the people by those evil souls that have passed away. Good is stronger than evil and even when alive they had very little power. How much less have they after they are dead” (Daily Lessons Received at Akka, p. 43). Third, attempts to communicate with or summon departed spirits do not achieve contact. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states in Some Answered Questions that “no concrete result or outcome ever follows from this supposed compelling or summoning of spirits,” characterizing such claims as “mere fables and fictions” (ch. 71).

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 1 November 1934 summarizes both the non-existence of evil spirits and the reality of evil itself:

“what is generally called evil spirit is a purely imaginary creation and has no reality whatever. But as to evil, there is no doubt that it exerts a very strong influence both in this world and in the next.”

Although evil does not exist as an independent substance, evil choices, corrupting influences, and destructive actions produce real consequences.

Nocturnal experiences and medical research

In Some Answered Questions, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá distinguishes between two kinds of subjective spiritual experiences. The first comprises authentic spiritual perceptions, such as prophetic visions. The second comprises psychological illusions:

“The other kind of spiritual disclosures is mere illusion, but these illusions take such a tangible form in the mind that many simple-hearted people imagine them to be real.” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 71)

The subjective vividness of an experience does not prove that an external entity caused it. When a believer reported distress from hearing voices, Shoghi Effendi advised through his secretary that she should treat it as “a phenomenon which might be your subconscious mind, might be some psychic influence, but whatever it was you should not let it disturb you and certainly not place much importance on it” (quoted in Lights of Guidance, §1736). In another letter, his secretary wrote that reported psychic phenomena “in most cases are an indication of a deep psychological disturbance” and that believers “should avoid as much as possible giving undue consideration to such matters” (20 November 1937).

Contemporary medical research describes the physiological basis of nighttime experiences that are often attributed to spirits. (This medical research describes empirical findings; it provides scientific context, not Bahá’í scriptural authority.) Sleep paralysis occurs when an individual awakens during the REM stage of sleep while temporary muscle atonia persists. The person wakes unable to move or speak, frequently experiencing hallucinations, chest pressure, and a strong sensation of an intruder in the room. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 76 studies covering 167,133 participants across 25 countries estimated the prevalence of sleep paralysis at 30 percent in the general population, 34 percent among students, and 35 percent among psychiatric patients (Hefnawy et al., Cureus, 2024).

Cross-cultural studies indicate that while the physiological event is universal, the explanation given to it depends on cultural background. In a comparative study of sleep paralysis in Egypt and Denmark, 71 percent of Egyptian participants attributed the episodes to supernatural causes and 48 percent attributed them specifically to jinn, with 41 percent using protective recitations (ruqyah); in contrast, Danish participants rarely attributed the episodes to supernatural entities (Jalal et al., Transcultural Psychiatry, 2014). Similar cultural attributions exist worldwide, such as the karabasan in Turkey and the phi am in Thailand.

The physiological sensations are genuine, but the supernatural attribution is culturally learned. For persistent sleep disturbances, nightmares, or physical pain, the Bahá’í writings direct individuals to medical science. Bahá’u’lláh writes in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas: “Resort ye, in times of sickness, to competent physicians; We have not set aside the use of material means, rather have We confirmed it through this Pen” (¶113). Consulting a physician for sleep disorders or anxiety disorders is in accordance with this explicit guidance.

Traditional practices, witchcraft, and institutional guidance

Questions regarding black magic, ju-ju, and witchcraft have arisen in many Bahá’í communities. Guidance on these subjects is collected in the compilation Aspects of Traditional African Culture (Research Department of the Universal House of Justice, December 1998, §4.5).

In a letter dated 30 August 1984 written on its behalf, the Universal House of Justice explained the mechanism underlying psychic arts and traditional curses:

“The House of Justice fully appreciates that in Trinidad and Tobago there are many instances of individuals being affected adversely by the psychic arts of other people. This is an observable phenomenon in many parts of the world. … The important thing for Bahá’ís to understand is that the influence of such ‘arts’ is dependent on the conviction, even the sub-conscious conviction, of the person affected and, similarly, the power of the ‘priests’ to overcome the influence is likewise an outcome of the sufferer’s conviction that it is from the ‘priest’ that he or she will be able to obtain help.”

This analysis explains both why individuals suffer from curses and why traditional practitioners appear able to remove them: both the harm and the cure depend entirely on the conviction of the person affected. Because the effect stems from belief rather than occult power, Bahá’í institutions do not provide alternative rituals or counter-practitioners. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi state that “there is no such thing as Bahá’í healers or a Bahá’í type of healing” (8 June 1948), that individuals with healing abilities should not present their work as a Bahá’í practice, and that practices involving communication with departed spirits are incompatible with the teachings.

The same letter from the Universal House of Justice clarifies that although evil has no independent substantive existence, evil forces exert real influence:

“Bahá’ís recognize that evil is negative and has no existence in its own right, but that does not mean that there is no power in evil. Do not Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá warn us repeatedly of the spiritual infection of Covenant-breaking?”

The letter quotes a Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá recommending practical spiritual steps:

“…if you seek immunity from the sway of the forces of the contingent world, hang the Most Great Name in your dwelling, wear the ring of the Most Great Name on your finger, place the picture of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in your home and always recite the prayers that I have written. Then you will behold the marvellous effect they produce. Those so-called forces will prove but illusions and will be wiped out and exterminated.”

Similarly, a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 26 November 1939 states: “Evil forces do take control of our life, but it is within our power to free ourselves from falling under their subjection.”

The Universal House of Justice emphasizes that physical objects such as rings or symbols do not function as independent protective charms. The primary remedy is education and deepening in the Bahá’í writings:

“The principal means of overcoming such problems is through deepening the knowledge of the friends in the teachings of the Faith, so that they will come to recognize the lack of any true reality to such negative forces.” (30 August 1984)

Subsequent letters highlight active participation in community life, prayer, and consultation with Bahá’í institutions (24 March 1988). The House of Justice also notes that overcoming long-standing cultural traditions and fears is a gradual process requiring time and education, achieved by providing “a greater spiritual attraction” rather than abrupt condemnation (28 October 1990).

Free will, moral agency, and real harm

Rejecting the supernatural power of the evil eye does not mean that other people’s actions cannot cause harm. The Bahá’í writings recognize real social, moral, and physical harms resulting from the misuse of human free will.

These harms include slander, backbiting, prejudice, oppression, and active persecution. In the Tablet of Protection, Bahá’u’lláh asks God to shield the believer “from every calamity and pestilence, and from every wicked man and woman; from the evil of the evil-doers, and from the scheming of the unbelievers.” Prayers in the Bahá’í prayer book similarly seek protection from the malice and scheming of opponents. In these texts, human ill will causes harm through tangible actions, speech, and social influence, not through occult forces or glances.

In Some Answered Questions, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá outlines the boundaries of human free will:

“Certain matters are subject to the free will of man, such as acting with justice and fairness, or injustice and iniquity—in other words, the choice of good or evil actions. … But there are certain matters where man is forced and compelled, such as sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, and material loss: These are not subject to the will of man.” (ch. 70)

Under this distinction, sickness, physical death, and material accidents are not directly subject to human will. An individual has the free will to choose between acting justly or unjustly, but cannot project sickness or misfortune onto another person through will or admiration alone.

At the same time, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá distinguishes between decreed fate and conditional fate. Using the example of a lamp, He notes that while the oil will eventually be consumed (decreed fate), a sudden wind can extinguish the flame prematurely if the lamp is not sheltered (conditional fate). Regarding conditional fate, He writes: “It is expedient to avoid this fate, to guard oneself against it, and to be cautious and prudent” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 74). Practical caution—such as avoiding unhealthy environments and safeguarding one’s physical wellbeing—is appropriate. But constant suspicion toward others is contrary to Bahá’í ethics. In Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, He advises:

“Let them see no one as their enemy, or as wishing them ill, but think of all humankind as their friends; regarding the alien as an intimate, the stranger as a companion.” (§1)

Viewing admiration or goodwill as a covert threat undermines personal peace and damages social relationships.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá and the Afghan resident in ‘Akká

A historical example of responding to prolonged ill will appears in accounts of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s life in ‘Akká. The account was recorded by Myron Phelps in 1903 and corroborated by Bahíyyih Khánum in the same volume.

During ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s exile in ‘Akká, an Afghan Muslim living in the city publicly opposed Him as a heretic. The man denounced ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in public gatherings and in the mosque, warning people not to speak to Him or associate with Him. When passing ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in the street, he covered his face with his robe to avoid looking at Him.

In response, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá provided him with regular support. The man was poor and lived at the mosque; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá sent him food and clothing, which the man accepted without acknowledgment. When the man fell ill, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá sent a physician, paid for his medicine, and provided food and financial assistance. Phelps records that while extending one wrist for the physician to examine his pulse, the man used his other hand to cover his face so he would not see ‘Abdu’l-Bahá.

This pattern continued for twenty-four years until the man came to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s residence:

“Then at last one day the Afghan came to the Master’s door, and fell down, penitent and weeping, at his feet. ‘Forgive me, sir!’ he cried. ‘For twenty-four years I have done evil to you, for twenty-four years you have done good to me. Now I know that I have been in the wrong.’” (Phelps, Life and Teachings of Abbas Effendi, p. 101)

The two men remained on friendly terms thereafter.

This historical account illustrates how ‘Abdu’l-Bahá responded to sustained hostility. The opposition was public, deliberate, and continued over decades within a small city. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá neither displayed anxiety nor retaliated. Instead, He practiced the principle He articulated in Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: to bestow assistance upon opponents and show kindness to those who express enmity (§1). The animosity of another person caused Him no spiritual or psychological harm, while persistent material assistance and goodwill eventually altered the opponent’s conduct.

Bahá’í practices for protection and assurance

For individuals accustomed to relying on amulets, protective rituals, or counter-charms, the Bahá’í writings provide specific constructive practices:

  1. Immediate remembrance of God. When fear or suspicion of the evil eye arises, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá instructs the individual to engage immediately in the remembrance of God so that the superstition does not take root. For someone who feels troubled by these impressions, He recommends reciting “Yá Alláhu’l-Mustagháth” (“O God, the Invoked”) ninety-five times.
  2. Recitation of prayers for protection. The Bahá’í prayer book includes a dedicated section of prayers for protection. In one prayer, Bahá’u’lláh writes: “Armed with the power of Thy name nothing can ever hurt me, and with Thy love in my heart all the world’s afflictions can in no wise alarm me.” In another prayer, He asks God to protect His servants “from the darts of idle fancy and vain imaginings.” The Báb revealed a prayer for protection in the shape of a pentacle that asks God for protection from all directions: “Protect us from what lieth in front of us and behind us, above our heads, on our right, on our left, below our feet and every other side to which we are exposed.” Regarding the Tablet of Aḥmad, Bahá’u’lláh states: “Should one who is in affliction or grief read this Tablet with absolute sincerity, God will dispel his sadness, solve his difficulties and remove his afflictions.”
  3. Use of the Greatest Name. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá recommended displaying the Greatest Name in one’s home, wearing the ringstone symbol, and reciting prayers as means of protection from negative influences. However, the Universal House of Justice explains that these items are not amulets whose material form exerts power. Rather, their efficacy is spiritual, and their primary function is to foster faith and confidence in God: “Man is the supreme Talisman” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, p. 161). The true safeguard is spiritual steadfastness rather than reliance on a physical object.
  4. Medical evaluation for physical and sleep symptoms. Chronic sleep paralysis, severe night terrors, panic attacks, and physical pain require medical diagnosis and treatment. Bahá’u’lláh explicitly commanded believers to consult competent physicians in times of sickness (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶113). Seeking professional medical care for sleep or psychiatric disorders is an application of this law.
  5. Prudent association. In a letter written on his behalf on 4 October 1950, Shoghi Effendi advised that while believers should seek to overcome evil with good, if an individual proves persistently malicious and uninfluenceable, “we should shun his company for it is unhealthy.” Setting practical boundaries with hostile individuals is sensible conduct.
  6. Focusing attention on service and community life. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi consistently advise believers not to dwell on occult phenomena, psychic impressions, or fear of negative forces. One letter states: “If Bahá’u’lláh had attached the slightest importance to occult experiences, to the seeing of auras, to the hearing of mystic voices … He, Himself, would have mentioned all of these things in His Teachings. The fact that He passed over them in silence shows that to Him, they had either no importance or no reality. … We must turn our faces away from these things, and toward the actual practice of His Teachings in our everyday life” (quoted in Lights of Guidance, §1741). Another letter notes that of paramount importance is to leave such concerns aside and engage in service to humanity (20 November 1938). Participating in community building, teaching, and practical service redirects attention away from self-absorbed fears.

Summary of the Bahá’í approach

The Bahá’í teachings resolve the issue of the evil eye and malevolent spirits through three primary principles:

  1. Separating subjective experience from supernatural explanation. The emotional distress, anxiety, and physical sensations that accompany superstitious fear are real, but they do not stem from external occult forces. They arise from internal belief, psychological suggestion, and natural physiological reactions. Recognizing this distinction eliminates the fear of external malevolent agencies while validating the individual’s need for relief.
  2. Replacing ritual counter-measures with education and prayer. The Bahá’í Faith does not provide rituals for exorcism, curse-breaking, or magical counter-attacks. As the Universal House of Justice noted, seeking ritual deliverance from curses reinforces the false assumption that curses have occult power. Instead, the Bahá’í approach addresses the underlying conviction through education, spiritual deepening, and prayer, helping individuals recognize that these forces have no substantive existence.
  3. Locating protection in spiritual steadfastness and practical action. True protection does not depend on physical amulets or magical formulas. It is achieved by cultivating trust in God, maintaining moral integrity, seeking qualified medical care for physical and mental disorders, and engaging in constructive service to others.

  • Compilation sources. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi cited from Lights of Guidance and the 1998 Research Department compilation Aspects of Traditional African Culture represent correspondence with individual believers and national institutions.
  • Scriptural scope on the jinn. The authoritative Bahá’í writings interpret references to evil spirits, demons, and Satan symbolically, but the corpus does not contain a dedicated treatise addressing the Qur’ánic concept of jinn specifically by name.
  • Evaluation of hadith traditions. Bahá’u’lláh quotes Islamic traditions in the Kitáb-i-Íqán and other works, but individual traditions concerning the evil eye (such as al-’aynu ḥaqq) are not individually analyzed in available Bahá’í texts.
  • External medical research is descriptive. Scientific findings regarding sleep paralysis provide empirical and cross-cultural context. They describe physical and psychological mechanisms, but are not cited as authoritative theological sources.
  • Medical non-diagnosis. Discussion of sleep paralysis and anxiety mechanisms provides general information and does not constitute a clinical diagnosis for any individual. Clinical evaluation requires consultation with a qualified physician.
  • Historical nature of the Phelps account. The account of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and the Afghan resident in ‘Akká rests on Myron Phelps’s 1903 biography and the narrative of Bahíyyih Khánum. It is an observational historical source rather than revealed scripture.
