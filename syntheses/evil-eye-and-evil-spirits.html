The Evil Eye, Evil Spirits, and the Will of Others: What the Bahá’í Writings Say

Last updated 11 September 2026

Executive summary

Someone who fears that other people’s ill will can reach them — through the evil eye, through a curse, through spirits that follow the envious gaze — is usually described by the Bahá’í literature in two moves rather than one, and the second move is the one that is usually missed.

The first move is a flat denial of the mechanism. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, asked directly whether “the evil eye” can exert an effect, answered: “The eye possesseth no such influence.” Asked again, in another Tablet, He called the whole idea “mere superstition.” On evil spirits He was equally plain: references to “evil spirits, demons and monsters” in scripture “have symbolic meaning,” and what circulates among the public “is but sheer superstition.” Satan, in the Bahá’í reading, names the lower nature in the human being, not a rival power in the universe. The souls of the dead do not linger in rooms; they do not take on bodies; “Good is stronger than evil and even when alive they had very little power.”

The second move is an acknowledgement that something real is being observed. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá did not say nothing happens. He said the causal story is wrong. In the same breath as “the eye possesseth no such influence” He added: “there is a consummate wisdom in the impressions which people give and receive.” He then described the actual mechanism with unusual precision: a person who believes themselves looked upon with the evil eye “will grow anxious; he will have no peace of mind, waiting constantly for a calamity to befall him. These are the impressions that are caused, and these the occurrences which result.” The Universal House of Justice has applied exactly this analysis to ju-ju, witchcraft, and voodoo: “the influence of such ‘arts’ is dependent on the conviction, even the sub-conscious conviction, of the person affected.” And Shoghi Effendi would not let believers shrug evil off as a mere word: “no one would assert darkness was not a fact. So evil exists too, and we cannot close our eyes to it, even though it is a negative existence.”

My position, stated plainly: the Bahá’í answer to this fear is neither “it is all in your head” nor a counter-charm. It is a transfer of the thing relied upon. The Writings do not abolish the vocabulary of protection — they keep it, intensely; the Báb wrote a prayer for protection in His own hand in the shape of a pentacle, Bahá’u’lláh revealed a Tablet asking God to guard its bearer “from every wicked man and woman,” and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá told a troubled correspondent to hang the Greatest Name in the house. What changes is where the power is located. Bahá’u’lláh’s reversal is the sentence to hold onto: “Man is the supreme Talisman.” The shield is not an object, and it is not a counter-spell; it is a condition of the soul, and it is reachable by remembrance, by prayer, by competent medical help where the trouble is medical, and by redirecting attention toward service. The demonstration that this works is not a doctrine but a story: an Afghan cleric in ‘Akká spent twenty-four years cursing ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in the mosque and holding his robe before his face in the street, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá sent him food, clothing, money, and a physician until the man came weeping to His door. The hostility was real and organised. It had no purchase.

The shape of the fear

The worry rarely arrives as one proposition. It arrives as a cluster, and each piece of the cluster reinforces the others.

It begins with envy. Someone admires a child, a marriage, a run of good fortune — and then the thing admired breaks. The inference is almost irresistible: the admiration did it. Attention itself becomes dangerous, and the danger is worst when the attention is warm, because praise draws the gaze. From there the cluster extends. A room feels wrong on entering it. Waking in the dark, a person is certain that something malevolent is present and close, sometimes with physical pain. Friends report being hunted through their dreams. Blessing and disaster keep arriving together, until the two look causally linked, and the conclusion forms that well-wishing itself attracts something that means harm.

Every element of this is a recognisable human experience, and a person reporting it is reporting accurately. The question a synthesis has to answer is not whether the experience occurred. It is what the experience is, and what to do about it. Those are two separate questions, and the Bahá’í texts answer them separately.

The tradition being read from

Someone raising this from a Muslim background is not inventing a folk anxiety. They are reading a textual tradition, and the texts are specific.

The Qur’án ends with two short súrahs of refuge-seeking, the mu’awwidhatayn. In Súrat al-Falaq the believer seeks refuge “from the evil of the envier when he envies” (113:5) — and classical commentators, al-Qurṭubí and al-Ṭabarí among them, read ḥasad there as the desire that another be stripped of a blessing, with later exegetes such as al-Sa’dí extending the verse to cover the ‘ayn, the eye, as the outward channel of an envious soul. Súrat al-Qalam is read the same way: “those who disbelieve would almost make you slip with their eyes” (68:51). In the hadith collections the statement is direct. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records, on the authority of Ibn ’Abbās: al-’aynu ḥaqq — “The evil eye is real. If anything could precede the divine decree, it would be preceded by the evil eye” (Muslim 2188, Kitāb al-Salām); a parallel report from Abū Hurayra appears in al-Bukhārī’s Book of Medicine. Ruqyah, recitation over the afflicted, is permitted for precisely this; one report in al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd restricts ruqyah to “the evil eye or fever.” Jinn are a created order in the Qur’án with a súrah of their own (72), and sorcery, siḥr, appears at 2:102, in the account of Hārūt and Mārūt at Babylon.

Two things are worth noticing before going further, because they decide whether this conversation is a collision or not.

The first is that the Bahá’í Faith does not approach the Qur’án as an outsider’s document. Bahá’ís hold Muḥammad to be a Manifestation of God and the Qur’án to be revealed Scripture; the Báb, in the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’, calls the Qur’án the Book “which is free from error.” A Bahá’í reading of 113:5 is therefore not a dismissal of the verse but a question about its meaning — and the Bahá’í hermeneutic of scripture is heavily, consistently symbolic. When the Guardian was asked about the people in the Gospels “described as being possessed of devils,” the reply was that “this should be interpreted figuratively; devil or satan is symbolic of evil and dark forces yielding to temptation.” The same reading is applied to the Qur’ánic material. That is an interpretive claim about scripture, not a denial of it.

The second is that the Qur’án itself attaches a limit that the folk version drops. At 2:102, on the magic learned at Babylon, the text states that its practitioners “could not harm anyone except by God’s permission.” Even inside the Islamic frame, the harm is not an autonomous force. It is contingent, permitted, subordinate. And there is a sharper irony in the Qur’ánic record that Bahá’u’lláh’s own writings dwell on: the accusations of jinn-possession and sorcery were levelled at the Prophets. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, reviewing the pattern, quotes the deniers of every age — “Ye follow none other but a man enchanted”; “He deviseth a lie about God, or there is a jinn in him”; Pharaoh’s “He, in sooth, is your Master who hath taught you magic.” In scripture, the language of the occult is most often the vocabulary of rejection, spoken by those who could not see what was in front of them.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá answers the question directly, twice

The Bahá’í corpus contains two Tablets of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá responding to believers who asked about the evil eye by name. They are not in the authorised English translations; they are available in provisional translation by Adib Masumian from Persian originals published in Ganjíniy-i-Ḥudúd va Aḥkám (p. 454) and Makátíb-i-Ḥaḍrat-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá (vol. 3, pp. 257–58). The English below is therefore provisional and not authorised, though the Persian is the Master’s own. Their value is that nowhere else does the authoritative literature treat the question so exactly.

The first is compact:

“Thou hadst written about the malevolent glare, which is to say afflictions that proceed from the eye … to ask whether or not ‘the evil eye’ can exert an effect. The eye possesseth no such influence, but there is a consummate wisdom in the impressions which people give and receive. This applieth generally. However, souls that are firm and steadfast are protected from such misfortune. Should such a one feel affected somehow by this superstition, let them recite ‘Yá Alláhu’l-Mustagháth’ ninety-five times.”

Read the sentence structure, because it carries the whole argument. A denial: the eye has no such power. Then a concession that is not a retreat: the giving and receiving of impressions between people is real, and there is wisdom in it. Then a qualification: steadfastness is itself protection. Then — and this is the part that a purely debunking answer could never produce — a remedy is prescribed, not for the eye, but for the feeling. Someone who feels affected is not told they are being foolish. They are given something to say.

The second Tablet sets out the mechanism in full:

“O servant of God! Thou hadst written about the benevolent gaze and the malevolent glare—in other words, afflictions that proceed from the eye. This is mere superstition, but there are feelings that result from this superstition which take hold in people. Those feelings, in turn, can give rise to certain impressions. Suppose, for example, there is someone who becomes reputed to have ‘the evil eye,’ and another person who believeth, with certainty, in such influences of the eye. When the person infamous for having ‘the evil eye’ looketh at this hapless soul, he, being superstitious, will grow anxious; he will have no peace of mind, waiting constantly for a calamity to befall him. These are the impressions that are caused, and these the occurrences which result. It is not the case that some misfortune or other proceeded from the eye of the former and affected the latter. If, therefore, such a thought as this should cross one’s mind: ‘So-and-so is evil-eyed and hath glared at me,’ let that one engage immediately in the remembrance of God, in order that this superstition may fade away from their heart.”

This is a causal chain, laid out in order: belief → dread → sleeplessness and expectancy → events that appear to confirm the belief. The chain runs entirely inside the person who fears, and it genuinely produces outcomes — “these the occurrences which result.” Nothing crosses from the other person’s eye. And the intervention is placed at the first link, at the instant the thought arrives: immediately, remembrance of God, so that the conviction dissolves before the dread can build on it.

Note what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not do. He does not tell the correspondent to find out who the evil-eyed person is, or to neutralise them, or to obtain a counter-measure. The entire prescription is addressed to the heart of the person asking.

Why the tradition’s observation is not stupid

The Bahá’í explanation would be weak if it stopped at “you are imagining it,” because the observed correlation between fear and misfortune is not imaginary. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s longest treatment of how minds act on bodies explains why.

In Some Answered Questions, asked how some heal without medicine, He distinguishes four kinds of healing — two material, two spiritual — and in describing the third He states the principle that underwrites the whole evil-eye analysis:

“when a terrifying event suddenly comes to pass, such an excitement may be produced in the nerves of a healthy person that he immediately falls ill. The cause of the illness is not a material thing, for that person has not ingested or come into contact with anything: The nervous excitement alone has brought about the illness.”

Terror makes people ill. That is asserted as fact, by the same authority that denies the evil eye, in the same body of teaching. A person who becomes convinced they have been cursed and then falls sick has not misremembered the sequence of events. They have misidentified the agent.

The same chapter sets a ceiling on what soul-to-soul influence can accomplish, and the ceiling matters here as much as the effect. Where a strong connection of attention and expectation exists between two people, health can be affected — “but this is effective only to a point and not in all cases.” Grave illness and physical injury lie outside its reach altogether: “these means have no sway over grave illnesses.” If the honest reach of deliberate, cooperative, mutually attentive spiritual effort is limited to minor conditions, then the reach of a stranger’s unwelcome glance is not somewhere beyond it.

And on the specific fear that another person’s thoughts can get inside one’s mind, the Guardian’s secretary wrote, on 18 January 1951, with an argument from ordinary experience: “You should not be afraid any one can affect your mind. Even when we want to catch the thoughts of those we love most we cannot do so, how much less can other people succeed in penetrating our minds.” Anyone who has sat with a beloved person and been unable to read them knows this is true. The channel the fear depends on is one nobody has ever been able to open, even with every motive to.

Shoghi Effendi put the underlying advice about evil thoughts arriving unbidden in an image that works with the mechanism rather than against it — one should not become a mirror reflecting them, and should not fight them either, “for this is impossible; it only aggravates the difficulty, causing more to appear”; instead the mirror of the heart is turned squarely toward God, face to the light and back to the thoughts. (This passage comes from Daily Lessons Received at Akka, a pilgrim’s record of answers given in ‘Akká; such notes carry no authority, and it is cited here for its practical usefulness rather than as doctrine. The companion statement in the same volume — “Evil spirits are deprived of eternal life. How then can they exercise any influence?” — is a Tablet to Ella Goodall Cooper and stands on firmer ground.)

Evil spirits, Satan, and the dead

On the existence of malevolent disembodied beings, the Bahá’í texts are not ambiguous.

The Universal House of Justice, writing in 1972 to a National Spiritual Assembly whose communities were troubled by ju-ju, supplied quotations gleaned from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Writings for the Assembly to share. The first was this:

“As to the question of evil spirits, demons and monsters, any references made to them in the Holy Books have symbolic meaning. What is currently known among the public is but sheer superstition.”

In The Promulgation of Universal Peace, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names what the symbol stands for:

“The reality underlying this question is that the evil spirit, Satan or whatever is interpreted as evil, refers to the lower nature in man. … God has never created an evil spirit; all such ideas and nomenclature are symbols expressing the mere human or earthly nature of man.”

This is more demanding than it first appears, not less. An external tempter can be warded off; one’s own lower nature cannot be, and has to be educated. The Bahá’í reading does not make the moral landscape safer — it relocates the danger inward, where responsibility lies. When the House of Justice set out the Bahá’í position on the concept of the Devil in 1974, it was in these terms: “the concept of Satan or the Devil as an actual being opposed to God is rejected by the Bahá’í teachings but … the term is understood by Bahá’ís to mean the promptings of self and desire and the dark side of human nature.”

On the dead, three statements close off the three things usually feared. They do not come back in bodies: “When the souls leave the bodies, they do not assume elemental bodies. Whatever man thinks regarding this is but his own imagination.” They do not exert power over the living; a Western pilgrim who asked ‘Abdu’l-Bahá about the power of evil souls who had passed on recorded the reply as: “There is no power exercised over the people by those evil souls that have passed away. Good is stronger than evil and even when alive they had very little power. How much less have they after they are dead.” And summoning them does not work; on “this supposed compelling or summoning of spirits,” ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s test in Some Answered Questions is empirical — “no concrete result or outcome ever follows” — and His verdict is blunt: “these are mere fables and fictions.”

Shoghi Effendi’s secretary, writing on 1 November 1934, states the conclusion and immediately guards it from being over-read:

“what is generally called evil spirit is a purely imaginary creation and has no reality whatever. But as to evil, there is no doubt that it exerts a very strong influence both in this world and in the next.”

That second sentence is the whole reason this article has two halves instead of one.

What the night-time experiences actually are

If no spirit is in the bathroom, what is in the bathroom? The Bahá’í literature answers this in a way that neither flatters the experience nor insults the person having it.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá divides “spiritual disclosures” into two kinds. One is genuine — the visions of Isaiah, Jeremiah, John; perception not mediated by the five senses, which “exert a marvellous effect upon minds and thoughts and produce powerful attractions in the hearts.” The other is different, and the sentence describing it is the important one for someone frightened in the dark:

“The other kind of spiritual disclosures is mere illusion, but these illusions take such a tangible form in the mind that many simple-hearted people imagine them to be real.”

“Take such a tangible form in the mind.” The vividness of an experience is not, on this account, evidence of its external reality — and the person who finds it vivid is not lying, exaggerating, or weak-minded. The Guardian’s guidance to a believer disturbed by a voice she had heard takes the same line without a trace of contempt: treat it as “a phenomenon which might be your subconscious mind, might be some psychic influence, but whatever it was you should not let it disturb you and certainly not place much importance on it,” and do not let something “the origin and purpose of which you have no way of knowing” influence you or set any store on its observations. Elsewhere his secretary wrote that reported psychic phenomena “in most cases are an indication of a deep psychological disturbance,” and that believers “should avoid as much as possible giving undue consideration to such matters” — advice that simultaneously withdraws credence from the content and directs attention to the person’s actual condition.

Here the corpus stops and the contemporary field begins; what follows is a description of the state of research, not authority for a Bahá’í principle. The specific experience of waking unable to move, certain of a malevolent presence in the room, often with pressure or pain in the chest or abdomen, is one of the best-documented phenomena in sleep medicine. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 76 studies from 25 countries and 167,133 participants put the overall prevalence of sleep paralysis at 30 per cent, rising to 34 per cent among students and 35 per cent among psychiatric patients; fear and a sensed presence are among the commonly reported features, and hallucinations, when they occur, present “as paranormal experiences, dreams, or strange phenomena related to supernatural powers or evil spirits” (Hefnawy et al., Cureus, 2024).

What the cross-cultural work adds is the decisive part. The physiology is the same everywhere; the interpretation is supplied locally. Jalal, Simons-Rudolph, Jalal and Hinton, comparing samples in Cairo and Copenhagen in 2014, found that 71 per cent of Egyptian experiencers interpreted the episode as supernatural and 48 per cent attributed it specifically to the jinn, with 41 per cent turning to ruqyah to prevent further attacks; Danish participants were markedly less likely to reach for a supernatural explanation of the identical phenomenon. The same experience is the phi am ghost in Thailand, the karabasan in Turkey, the Old Hag in Newfoundland, and in Cambodian accounts a ghost pressing down on the chest. A person who wakes terrified in Cairo and a person who wakes terrified in Copenhagen have had the same night. They have inherited different explanations of it.

Which is exactly what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s second evil-eye Tablet describes, two generations before the research existed: the experience is genuine, the feelings that follow are genuine, the consequences are genuine, and the causal story attached to it by the surrounding culture is not. And the Bahá’í response to a recurring, distressing physical event has a clear precedent. Bahá’u’lláh’s own law in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas is “Resort ye, in times of sickness, to competent physicians; We have not set aside the use of material means, rather have We confirmed it through this Pen.” A sleep disorder belongs to a sleep physician as surely as a fever belongs to a doctor. Treating it that way is obedience, not faithlessness.

Ju-ju, voodoo, amulets — and the House of Justice’s two-sided answer

This question is not peculiar to one culture, and the Bahá’í institutions have dealt with it at scale. The most substantial body of guidance is gathered in Aspects of Traditional African Culture, compiled by the Research Department at the Bahá’í World Centre in December 1998, whose section 4.5 addresses “Supernatural Forces, Evil Spirits, Witchcraft and Other Practices” across communities where black magic, ju-ju, and voodoo are long established. It is worth reading precisely because the guidance refuses the easy position in both directions.

On the mechanism, the 1984 letter written on behalf of the House of Justice is unflinching, and begins by conceding the phenomenon:

“The House of Justice fully appreciates that in Trinidad and Tobago there are many instances of individuals being affected adversely by the psychic arts of other people. This is an observable phenomenon in many parts of the world. … The important thing for Bahá’ís to understand is that the influence of such ‘arts’ is dependent on the conviction, even the sub-conscious conviction, of the person affected and, similarly, the power of the ‘priests’ to overcome the influence is likewise an outcome of the sufferer’s conviction that it is from the ‘priest’ that he or she will be able to obtain help.”

The second half of that is the sentence people skip. It is the trap the whole system runs on: the practitioner who lifts the curse is powered by the same conviction as the curse. Paying to have it removed confirms that it was there. That is why the guidance does not propose a Bahá’í counter-practitioner. The Faith declines to take over the job. “There is no such thing as Bahá’í healers or a Bahá’í type of healing,” the Guardian’s secretary wrote; believers with a genuine gift for healing are told not to attribute it to the Faith; and practices “involving communication with departed spirits” are named as incompatible with the teachings, to be left behind.

And then the same letter refuses the reductive conclusion:

“Bahá’ís recognize that evil is negative and has no existence in its own right, but that does not mean that there is no power in evil. Do not Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá warn us repeatedly of the spiritual infection of Covenant-breaking?”

It goes on to quote a Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá that a strictly rationalist reading of the Faith would not predict:

“…if you seek immunity from the sway of the forces of the contingent world, hang the Most Great Name in your dwelling, wear the ring of the Most Great Name on your finger, place the picture of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in your home and always recite the prayers that I have written. Then you will behold the marvellous effect they produce. Those so-called forces will prove but illusions and will be wiped out and exterminated.”

And a letter written for the Guardian on 26 November 1939: “Evil forces do take control of our life, but it is within our power to free ourselves from falling under their subjection.”

Taken together these do not describe a Faith that has explained the problem away. They describe a Faith that says: the forces you fear have no independent reality; evil nonetheless has power; you have the capacity to get free; here are things to do. The principal means, the letter insists, is not any of the objects but deepening in the teachings “so that they will come to recognize the lack of any true reality to such negative forces” — the object supports a conviction, it does not substitute for one. A 1988 letter adds the communal side: prayer, consultation with the institutions and obedience to their instructions, and “wholehearted participation in Bahá’í community life.”

On pace, the guidance is patient rather than purist. Where such beliefs have been held for centuries, “weaning the Bahá’ís from them is a gradual process and takes time and patience,” and “any bad habits of ancient cultural traditions are overcome by the offering of a greater spiritual attraction” (28 October 1990). Nobody is asked to argue themselves out of a lifelong fear in an afternoon.

Other people’s free will really can harm you — just not that way

A response that left the matter at “no one can touch you” would be false, and the Writings do not say it. They relocate the danger rather than deleting it.

The harms the Bahá’í texts take seriously from other people’s free will are moral and social, and they are grave: calumny and backbiting, the “spiritual infection” of Covenant-breaking, oppression, persecution, the scheming of enemies. Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablet of Protection asks God to shield its bearer “from every calamity and pestilence, and from every wicked man and woman; from the evil of the evil-doers, and from the scheming of the unbelievers.” His own prayer in the Protection section of the prayer book asks God to “send down … upon me and upon my loved ones that which will protect us from the mischief of those that have repudiated Thy truth.” These are not the petitions of a theology in which human malice is inert. Ill will is a real force in the world; it operates through tongues, institutions, courts, and crowds. It does not operate through glances.

The Bahá’í account of agency draws the boundary where the fear most needs one. In Some Answered Questions, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá distinguishes what is subject to a person’s free will from what is not:

“Certain matters are subject to the free will of man, such as acting with justice and fairness, or injustice and iniquity—in other words, the choice of good or evil actions. … But there are certain matters where man is forced and compelled, such as sleep, death, sickness, failing powers, misfortune, and material loss: These are not subject to the will of man.”

Sickness, misfortune, loss sit in the compelled category — outside the domain that anyone’s will governs, including one’s own. The domain another person’s free will actually reaches is their own conduct: whether they act justly or unjustly toward me. That is a real and sometimes terrible power. It is not the power to make my child ill by admiring them.

What free will is given instead is room for prudence. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s distinction between irrevocable and conditional fate — the lamp whose oil must eventually burn away, and the lamp a sudden wind may blow out — carries an explicit instruction about the second kind: “It is expedient to avoid this fate, to guard oneself against it, and to be cautious and prudent.” Ordinary caution is endorsed. Vigilance against an invisible enemy is not caution; it is the dread that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá identified as the actual source of harm.

There is also a cost to the fear that is easy to overlook, and it falls on the person who holds it. If others’ good wishes must be regarded as dangerous, then warmth becomes a threat, admiration becomes a risk, and every kindness has to be inspected. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s counsel in the first of His Selections points in precisely the opposite direction: “Let them see no one as their enemy, or as wishing them ill, but think of all humankind as their friends; regarding the alien as an intimate, the stranger as a companion.” Read beside the evil-eye Tablets, that is not naivety. It is the posture that the dread makes impossible — and the dread, not the glance, is what does the damage.

The Afghan of ‘Akká

Doctrine settles what is true. A life settles what is possible. The episode that answers this fear at the level it is actually felt was recorded by Myron Phelps, who spent the winter of 1902 in ‘Akká, and is confirmed in the same volume by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s sister Bahíyyih Khánum in her own narrative. It is a Western observer’s account rather than an authoritative text, and is offered as history.

When ‘Abdu’l-Bahá came to ‘Akká there lived in the city a man from Afghanistan, “an austere and rigid Mussulman,” to whom the Master was a heretic. He did not keep the enmity private. He worked it up in others, and in public gatherings, including the mosque, denounced ‘Abdu’l-Bahá by name: “This man is an impostor. Why do you speak to him? Why do you have dealings with him?” Passing Him in the street, “he was careful to hold his robe before his face that his sight might not be defiled.”

What ‘Abdu’l-Bahá did in return, Phelps records in a list of plain items. The Afghan was poor and lived in a mosque, often short of food and clothing; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá sent him both, and he accepted them without thanks. He fell sick; ‘Abdu’l-Bahá brought him a physician, food, medicine, money. He accepted those too — and as he held out one hand for the physician to take his pulse, “with the other he held his cloak before his face that he might not look upon the Master.”

For twenty-four years.

“Then at last one day the Afghan came to the Master’s door, and fell down, penitent and weeping, at his feet. ‘Forgive me, sir!’ he cried. ‘For twenty-four years I have done evil to you, for twenty-four years you have done good to me. Now I know that I have been in the wrong.’”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá told him to get up, and they became friends. Bahíyyih Khánum, recounting how deep-rooted the prejudices against the family had been, says of her brother that “in time his love for others has won all hearts,” and reports the question people in the city had taken to asking about him: “What does he do to his enemies that he makes them his friends?”

Several things in that story bear on the fear under discussion, and the first is the one most often missed. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s freedom was not the freedom of someone whose enemy was imaginary. The hostility was public, organised, sustained, and aimed at Him by a man with religious standing in a city where the exiles were already prisoners. He did not dispute its reality. He declined to grant it a channel. Twenty-four years of concentrated ill will, in a small city, with daily proximity, produced no effect on Him whatsoever — while the same twenty-four years of food, medicine, and money eventually dismantled the hatred entirely.

And the method was not endurance. It was the specific conduct ‘Abdu’l-Bahá prescribed in Selections, where He describes the person favoured at the Threshold of the Lord as the one “who bestoweth, even upon his enemies, the jewel of bounty, and lendeth, even to his fallen oppressor, a helping hand; it is he who will, even to the fiercest of his foes, be a loving friend.” In ‘Akká that was not a sentiment. It was a physician, a loaf, and a coat, repeated for a quarter of a century.

What replaces the amulet

The practical question remains, and it deserves a practical answer rather than a reassurance. Someone who has relied for years on protective practices is not helped by being told to stop and put nothing in their place. The Bahá’í texts supply a full set of replacements, and they are more active, not less.

Remembrance at the moment of the thought. This is ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s own prescription, and its timing is the point: when the thought “so-and-so has glared at me” crosses the mind, “let that one engage immediately in the remembrance of God, in order that this superstition may fade away from their heart.” For a person who feels already affected, the first Tablet gives something countable — “Yá Alláhu’l-Mustagháth,” O God, He Who is invoked, ninety-five times. The remedy is aimed at the conviction, which is where the damage is being done.

The prayers revealed for protection, which are many. The Protection section of Bahá’í Prayers is not a token. Bahá’u’lláh’s prayer there contains the sentence that states the entire principle in eighteen words: “Armed with the power of Thy name nothing can ever hurt me, and with Thy love in my heart all the world’s afflictions can in no wise alarm me.” Another of His prayers asks God to “keep safe Thy servants and Thy hand-maidens, O my Lord, from the darts of idle fancy and vain imaginings” — the protection sought is from the fancy itself. The Báb’s prayer for protection preserves the ancient all-directions formula without apology: “Protect us from what lieth in front of us and behind us, above our heads, on our right, on our left, below our feet and every other side to which we are exposed. Verily, Thy protection over all things is unfailing.” The original of that prayer is in the Báb’s own hand, in the form of a pentacle. And of the Tablet of Aḥmad, Bahá’u’lláh promises: “Should one who is in affliction or grief read this Tablet with absolute sincerity, God will dispel his sadness, solve his difficulties and remove his afflictions.”

The Greatest Name — understood correctly. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s counsel to hang the Most Great Name in the home, wear the ring, recite the prayers is authentic guidance and not a concession to weakness. But the 1972 letter pairs it with a statement that fixes its meaning: “That the ’Most Great Name’ exercises influence over both physical and spiritual matters is sure and certain” — and then the House of Justice immediately directs the Assembly’s attention to “the necessity of deepening the friends in their knowledge of the teachings, reinforcing their trust and faith in the Cause of God.” The difference between this and an amulet is not the object; it is what the object is understood to be doing. Bahá’u’lláh’s own sentence forecloses the confusion: “Man is the supreme Talisman.” And ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, writing of the Greatest Name, says that His Name “is indeed the healing medicine for every illness … the sovereign remedy and the supreme talisman … a shield for all mankind.” The vocabulary of the talisman is not abolished in the Bahá’í Writings. It is taken over, and its referent is changed: from an object that acts on the world to a Name that acts on the heart, and to the human being who bears it.

Competent medical help for what is medical. Recurrent night terror, sleep paralysis, nocturnal pain, chronic dread: the Aqdas instruction to resort to competent physicians applies without qualification. Guiding the community through the coronavirus pandemic, the House of Justice counselled that on the advisability and efficacy of the options available “the friends should follow the counsel of medical and other scientific experts.” Seeking a diagnosis is not a failure of faith; it is the law.

The company one keeps. The Guardian’s secretary, in the same 1950 letter that insists evil is a fact, draws a practical conclusion: “We must seek to supplant it by good, and if we see an evil person is not influenceable by us, then we should shun his company for it is unhealthy.” Ordinary distance from people who mean harm is sound. It is not a charm; it is prudence of the kind ‘Abdu’l-Bahá commended in discussing conditional fate.

And finally, redirection. The most repeated instruction in this entire body of guidance is to stop looking at the subject. “We must use the Writings of the Prophets as our measurement,” the Guardian’s secretary wrote to a believer troubled by exactly these questions. “If Bahá’u’lláh had attached the slightest importance to occult experiences, to the seeing of auras, to the hearing of mystic voices … He, Himself, would have mentioned all of these things in His Teachings. The fact that He passed over them in silence shows that to Him, they had either no importance or no reality.” And then: “We must turn our faces away from these things, and toward the actual practice of His Teachings in our everyday life.” Another letter is sharper still: spiritual experiences hold great personal interest, “but of paramount importance in this day is to forget them and go out into the world trying to save the human race from its threatening condition.”

That is not avoidance. An attention that has been organised for years around detecting threats has to be given something else to organise itself around, and the guidance names it: service, community, the teaching work, the practice of the teachings in daily life. A mind occupied in building something is not available for surveillance.

Where the Bahá’í answer actually differs

Three claims are worth stating as conclusions, because they are what distinguish this from both a folk answer and a debunking one.

The experience is granted; the causal story is refused. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá never tells anyone that they did not feel what they felt. He tells them that the feeling originated on their own side of the exchange, and that illusions can “take such a tangible form in the mind” that sincere people take them for reality. This is a more respectful answer than either scepticism or agreement, because it neither calls the person a liar nor leaves them in the power of a fiction.

The remedy is dissolution, not counter-force. No Bahá’í exorcism exists, no Bahá’í curse-lifting, no Bahá’í healer. This is not an oversight. The House of Justice’s analysis shows why it would be self-defeating: the practitioner who removes the curse draws their power from the same conviction that made the curse effective, so that relief purchased this way deepens the dependency it relieves. The Bahá’í intervention is aimed one level down, at the conviction itself — immediate remembrance, deepening in the teachings, the prayers, and enough understanding of the Writings “to recognize the lack of any true reality to such negative forces.”

The deepest protection is a condition, not a shield. “Souls that are firm and steadfast are protected from such misfortune,” ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote — and that is the same claim as Bahá’u’lláh’s “with Thy love in my heart all the world’s afflictions can in no wise alarm me,” and the same claim the Afghan story demonstrates. What made twenty-four years of public hatred powerless against ‘Abdu’l-Bahá was not a charm or a denial. It was an attachment so complete that the hostility had nothing to grip. That condition is not instantly available to a frightened person, which is why the texts also give countable recitations, specific prayers, a Name to hang on the wall, a physician to consult, and patient institutions that expect the process to take time. But it is the direction everything else points in, and it is the reason the answer to this fear is finally not an argument at all. It is a way of living that makes the fear unnecessary.

Open questions / honest limits

  • The two evil-eye Tablets are provisional translations. The Persian originals are published in Ganjíniy-i-Ḥudúd va Aḥkám and Makátíb vol. 3, and the renderings used here are Adib Masumian’s, explicitly not authorised. The substance is corroborated by the authoritative guidance quoted throughout, but the exact English wording should not be treated as canonical or quoted as an authorised translation.
  • Pilgrims’ notes are used twice and flagged both times. The “mirror turned toward God” passage and the reply about evil souls who have passed away come from pilgrims’ records. They carry no authority. They are included because the authoritative material says the same things less concretely.
  • “Spiritualism, Psychic Phenomena and Related Subjects” — several Guardian-era letters here are quoted as they appear in Lights of Guidance and in the Research Department’s 1998 compilation. Lights of Guidance is a privately compiled work; where a letter’s date is given it is given, and the 1998 compilation is the more reliable of the two.
  • I have not found a Bahá’í text that addresses the jinn directly. The Qur’ánic order of jinn is not, so far as this corpus shows, taken up and either affirmed or denied as such in the authoritative Bahá’í Writings. The statements available address “evil spirits, demons and monsters” in scripture as symbolic and the popular beliefs as superstition; whether and how that bears on the Qur’ánic jinn specifically is not settled here, and a serious Muslim interlocutor is entitled to notice the gap.
  • The status of hadith in Bahá’í thought is not resolved on this page. Bahá’u’lláh quotes Islamic traditions freely and authoritatively in the Kitáb-i-Íqán, so the Faith plainly does not discard the hadith literature wholesale; but no general Bahá’í rule for weighing individual traditions has been stated, and al-’aynu ḥaqq is not addressed by name in any Bahá’í text located here.
  • The external research is field description and will age. The sleep-paralysis prevalence and attribution figures are from 2014 and 2024 publications; prevalence estimates vary widely with sampling, and the Egyptian and Danish samples are small (n = 89, 59, 44). They establish that supernatural attribution of a physiological event is common and culturally patterned. They do not and cannot adjudicate a metaphysical question, and they are not offered as authority for a Bahá’í principle.
  • This page does not diagnose anyone. Pointing out that sleep paralysis exists is not a claim about any particular person’s experience. That determination belongs to a competent physician, per the Kitáb-i-Aqdas.
  • The Afghan episode rests on a Western observer’s account (Phelps, 1903), corroborated in the same volume by Bahíyyih Khánum. The twenty-four-year span and the closing words are as recorded there; no independent documentary confirmation has been sought for this page.
