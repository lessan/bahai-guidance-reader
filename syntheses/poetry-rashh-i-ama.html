Rashḥ-i-‘Amá (The Clouds of the Realms Above)

Last updated 17 October 2026

Rashḥ-i-‘Amá is a Persian poem of twenty couplets by Bahá’u’lláh. Bahá’í sources date it to 1852, when He was imprisoned in the Síyáh-Chál (the “Black Pit”) of Ṭihrán, and treat it as the earliest of His writings that survives. In the authorised English translation it is the first text in The Call of the Divine Beloved, where it is titled “The Clouds of the Realms Above”. Every line ends with the same idea: something is “raining down” on the world. This page is part of a series on poetry in the Writings; see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poem

When and where. The preface to The Call of the Divine Beloved says that “the first, the poem known as ‘Rashḥ-i-‘Amá’, was written in 1852 in the Síyáh-Chál and is among the few He revealed while in His native land of Persia, and in verse” (CDB Preface 2). Shoghi Effendi gives the setting. After the attempt on the Sháh’s life on 15 August 1852, Bahá’u’lláh was arrested at Níyávarán, taken to Ṭihrán and “cast into the Síyáh-Chál”, where His imprisonment “lasted for a period of no less than four months” (GPB VII.1). Shoghi Effendi describes the dungeon, the chains and the denial of food and sleep in God Passes By V.14.

What Shoghi Effendi says of it. In his account of the months of seclusion that followed the Síyáh-Chál, Shoghi Effendi writes of the “initial and impassioned outpourings of a Soul struggling to unburden itself” and says that these, “with the Tablet of Kullu’ṭ-Ṭa‘ám and the poem entitled Rashḥ-i-‘Amá, revealed in Ṭihrán, the first fruits of His Divine Pen” (GPB VII.36). He thus names it, with the Tablet of Kullu’ṭ-Ṭa‘ám, as among the first writings of Bahá’u’lláh. He does not quote or analyse the poem in that passage.

The experience behind it. The preface of The Call of the Divine Beloved calls the poem “the poetic reflection of that experience”, meaning the experience Bahá’u’lláh describes in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, which Shoghi Effendi also quotes in God Passes By (VI.27). The words are Bahá’u’lláh’s:

“During the days I lay in the prison of Ṭihrán, though the galling weight of the chains and the stench-filled air allowed Me but little sleep, still in those infrequent moments of slumber I felt as if something flowed from the crown of My head over My breast, even as a mighty torrent that precipitateth itself upon the earth from the summit of a lofty mountain. Every limb of My body would, as a result, be set afire. At such moments My tongue recited what no man could bear to hear.”

(CDB Preface 2, quoting the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf; also in GPB VI.27)

The preface adds that this poetic reflection “can perhaps never be adequately rendered into another language, yet the present translation is an initial attempt to impart a glimpse of its power and momentous themes.” Neither the preface nor Shoghi Effendi says that the poem was recited in one of these moments of slumber. The link between the poem and the experience is the preface’s statement, not a claim made in the poem.

Language and form. The Persian text kept in this repository ([source]) is a transcription from The Utterance Project, which says it is the text supplied by the Research Department of the Universal House of Justice, based on a version transcribed by Zaynu’l-Muqarrabín. It has twenty couplets. Each couplet ends with the verb mī-rīzad (“rains down”, “pours down”), preceded by a rhyming syllable. From the eleventh couplet, the first line of each couplet is made of two short halves that rhyme with each other and begin with the same command, “behold” (bīn), which is why the authorised translation repeats “Behold” in stanzas 11 to 19. Several words in the poem are written as single letters or abbreviations (for example “Há’”, “Bá’” and “Ṭá’” in the English), which the translators read as allusions to names; see the translators’ notes below.

The word ‘Amá. The authorised translation gives a note on the title word from a Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “‘Amá is defined as an extremely thin and subtle cloud, seen and then not seen” (CDB n. 8). In that Tablet the word names “the Universal Reality without individuations as such”, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá connects it with the “Hidden Treasure” of the Ḥadíth. The authorised translation attaches this note to the first line, which it renders “the clouds of realms above”.

The authorised translation, in full

This text is the authorised translation. The numbers are those of the stanzas in the printed volume (paragraph addresses CDB 1.1 to CDB 1.20).

1.

’TIS FROM Our rapture that the clouds of realms above are raining down; ’Tis from Our anthem that the mysteries of faith are raining down.

2.

Upon the Eastern wind Cathay’s entrancing musk doth waft; This sweetly scented breeze from Our curling locks is raining down.

3.

The day-star of adornment hath dawned forth above the face of God; Behold that mystic truth which from His Countenance is raining down.

4.

The sea of purity hath from the wave of true reunion surged; This precious, rare bestowal from Our rapture is raining down.

5.

The treasuries of love lay hid within the very heart of Fárs; From out this treasure trove the pearls of faithfulness are raining down.

6.

The splendour of the rose doth bring the ecstasy of choicest wine; This subtle music from the ringing tones of Lordship is raining down.

7.

The trumpet-blast of Judgement Day, the joyful bliss of heaven’s call— Both at a single breath are from the firmament now raining down.

8.

The Day of “I am He” is made to shine resplendent from Our face; The Age of “He is He” from out Our flowing cup is raining down.

9.

From out the fountain of Our heart hath God’s celestial river flowed; This cup of honeyed nectar from Our ruby lips is raining down.

10.

The Day of God hath been fulfilled, for lo, the Lord hath been unveiled; This wondrous message from the melody of Ṭá’ is raining down.

11.

Behold Bahá’s outpouring grace, the bounty of the clouds above, Which, merged into a single song, in God’s own voice is raining down.

12.

Behold the Lord’s leviathan, behold His sacred countenance; Behold the blessings of the heart that from His throne are raining down.

13.

Behold the Palm of Paradise, behold the warbling of the Dove; Behold the glorious hymns that in the purest light are raining down.

14.

Behold the soul-entrancing song, behold the beating of the drum, Behold the sacred rhythms that from Our hand are raining down.

15.

Behold the Countenance Divine! Behold the Maid of Paradise! Behold the grace upon the world from Our own presence raining down.

16.

Behold the everlasting Face! Behold the chalice-bearer’s charm! Behold the crystal draught that from Our brimming cup is raining down.

17.

Behold the fire of Moses, see His hand that shineth white; Behold the heart of Sinai—from Our hand all raining down.

18.

Hear ye the sotted lovers’ sighs, behold the garden blooming fair; Behold the bliss that from His presence in your midst is raining down.

19.

Behold the radiant face of Há’, behold the beauteous robe of Bá’; Behold the Lordly grace that from Our Pen is raining down.

20.

The vessel of the Advent this, the clouds of limpid waters these; The trill of songbirds this, from Our fleeting Wellspring raining down.

The two provisional translations (short extracts)

Both translators say they made the translation for study, and both explain their choices. The extracts below are for comparison only.

Lambden (provisional translation by Stephen Lambden, 1984/1995) keeps close to the words of the Persian and capitalises key terms. His opening couplet reads: “On account of Our Rapture the Sprinkling of the Cloud of Unknowing raineth down; The Mystery of Fidelity poureth forth from Our Melody.” His title and this line render rashḥ as “sprinkling” and ‘amá as “Cloud of Unknowing”.

Neshati (provisional translation by Ramin Neshati, 2000) rhymes his couplets and reorders some phrases. His opening couplet reads: “Our charm bids waft the Mist of Unknown / Mystery of fidelity thus flows from Our tone”. He renders ‘amá as “Mist of the Unknown”.

The three versions agree on the structure (twenty or nineteen couplets, a refrain of pouring or raining, a turn to “behold” in the second half) and differ in wording and in what is made explicit. The authorised translation says “raining down” in almost every line; Lambden varies between “raineth down”, “poureth forth” and “wafteth down”; Neshati uses “flows”, “pours” and “streams” and does not keep the refrain in every couplet.

What the translators say (as scholarship)

Scholars and translators are not authorities on the Writings. Their remarks help in reading the poem.

  • Number of couplets. Lambden’s version has nineteen couplets and says the Persian text he used (from Má’ida-yi-Ásmání, vol. 4) is “not a critical edition”. Neshati says his text, taken from the Research Department of the Universal House of Justice, “differs from the published version … in Ishraq Khavari’s Maida-yi Asmani, volume 4. The Maida text is deficient by one couplet.” The text saved here and the authorised translation both have twenty. The couplet that Lambden’s version lacks is the one about the treasuries of love in Fárs (stanza 5 of the authorised translation).
  • Abbreviated letters. Neshati reads the single letters in the Persian as abbreviations: fá’ as Fárs, the province of the Báb; ṭá’ as Ṭihrán; há’ as huwíyyat (essence) in one couplet; rá’ as rubúbíyyat (lordship). The authorised translation prints “Fárs” and “Ṭá’” and, in stanza 6, “Lordship”, so it agrees with these readings for the first and the last of these. Lambden keeps the letters as letters (“the letter ‘R’”, “the letter ‘T’”).
  • “I am He” and “He is He”. Neshati’s note says that in Shí‘í tradition the Mahdi was expected to speak a word that would make believers reject Him, and that in later writings Bahá’u’lláh explains this word as the change from “He is He” to “I am He”. He refers readers to Adib Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1. The copy of that book in the repository is only a catalogue page, so this reference could not be checked against the text of the book.
  • What the poem claims. Lambden writes that “it seems likely, as most Bahá’í writers have maintained, that the Rashh-i-‘Ama’ is expressive of Bahá’u’lláh’s Own assumption of a leading role within the Bábí community”, and adds that how far it alludes to the claim Bahá’u’lláh later made openly “is not entirely clear”. Neshati writes that it “records, in awe-inspiring language, Bahá’u’lláh’s earliest intimation of His prophet hood.” These are two scholars’ judgments; the Writings and Shoghi Effendi in the sources above say only that the poem is among the first fruits of His pen.
  • Date. Lambden places it in the latter half of 1852 (early 1269 AH). Neshati gives “sometime between the months of August and November 1852” and says that “not much more is known about the circumstances of its revelation.”

Themes and images

Pouring from a source. The refrain says that what is being given comes down from above, from “Our rapture”, “Our anthem”, “Our locks”, “Our cup”, “Our Pen”. The speaker is not described as the poem’s author but as the source: the pouring is “from Our face” and “from His presence in your midst”. The shift from “Our” to “His” in stanzas 3, 12 and 18 is in the authorised translation; the poem moves between the two without comment.

Senses and sources of delight. Scent (“Cathay’s entrancing musk”, “sweetly scented breeze”), wine (“the ecstasy of choicest wine”, “honeyed nectar”, “crystal draught”), sound (“the warbling of the Dove”, “the beating of the drum”, “the trill of songbirds”) and sight (“the day-star of adornment”, “the garden blooming fair”) run through the poem. Each is said to come from the same source.

The Day. Stanza 7 sets the “trumpet-blast of Judgement Day” beside “the joyful bliss of heaven’s call” and says that both come “at a single breath”. Stanza 8 names two ages by two phrases, “I am He” and “He is He”. Stanza 10 says: “The Day of God hath been fulfilled, for lo, the Lord hath been unveiled”. The last stanza calls what is being poured out “the vessel of the Advent”. The poem therefore speaks of the arrival of the promised Day, in the setting of Bábí expectation. For the Writings on that Day, see The greatness of this Day and The Day foretold.

Earlier Revelations named. Stanza 17 names Moses and Sinai: “Behold the fire of Moses, see His hand that shineth white”. Stanza 5 names Fárs, the province of the Báb, as the place where “the treasuries of love lay hid”.

The Maid of Paradise. Stanza 15 says “Behold the Maid of Paradise!” In the same period Shoghi Effendi says that Bahá’u’lláh sang “the praises of that Maiden that personified the Spirit of God within Him” (GPB VII.36). He does not connect that passage to this line of the poem, and this page does not either.

Reunion. Stanza 4 speaks of “the wave of true reunion”; stanza 9 of a “celestial river” flowing “from out the fountain of Our heart”.

What is not settled

  • The exact date. The sources agree on 1852 and on the Síyáh-Chál. Within that, only a range (August to November 1852, or the latter half of the year) is given.
  • The Persian text. The published text and the text supplied by the Research Department differ by one couplet and by several words. Neshati lists the differences and says some are unresolved. The authorised translation does not say which readings it follows beyond the preface.
  • The abbreviated letters. The readings of the single letters are given by Neshati as notes. The Writings and Shoghi Effendi, in the sources used here, do not explain them.
  • The meaning of “I am He” and “He is He” in this poem. Neshati and Lambden both read it in the light of later writings, with different degrees of confidence. A lyric should not state more than the text says.
  • The title in English. The authorised translation uses “The Clouds of the Realms Above”; the provisional translators use “Sprinkling of the Cloud of Unknowing” and “Mist of the Unknown”. These are three renderings of the same two Persian words.
  • Taherzadeh and other scholarship. The repository copy of The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1 does not contain the text, so what Taherzadeh says of the poem is not reported here.
  • A machine-drafted translation. None was made for this poem, because an authorised translation exists.

For songwriting

Notes, not lyrics. Phrases are copied from the authorised translation (licence `ok-with-credit`: credit “Bahá’u’lláh, The Call of the Divine Beloved, CDB 1.n”) and are 5 to 15 words long.

Singable exact phrases

  • “the clouds of realms above are raining down” (CDB 1.1)
  • “Upon the Eastern wind Cathay’s entrancing musk doth waft” (CDB 1.2)
  • “The sea of purity hath from the wave of true reunion surged” (CDB 1.4)
  • “The splendour of the rose doth bring the ecstasy of choicest wine” (CDB 1.6)
  • “From out the fountain of Our heart hath God’s celestial river flowed” (CDB 1.9)
  • “The Day of God hath been fulfilled, for lo, the Lord hath been unveiled” (CDB 1.10)
  • “Behold the soul-entrancing song, behold the beating of the drum” (CDB 1.14)
  • “Behold the fire of Moses, see His hand that shineth white” (CDB 1.17)
  • “The vessel of the Advent this, the clouds of limpid waters these” (CDB 1.20)

Refrain. The poem’s own refrain, “raining down”, can serve as a chorus word. “Behold”, repeated at the start of lines in stanzas 11 to 19, is the poem’s second device and fits a verse that builds.

Concrete images in the text. Rain and clouds; musk on the east wind; a sea and a wave; a rose and wine; a trumpet; a cup and a cupbearer; a river from a fountain; a palm tree and a dove; a drum; fire and a white hand (Moses); songbirds.

A possible arc, taken from the order of the poem. Stanzas 1 to 6: scent, sea, treasure, wine, music coming down from above. Stanzas 7 to 10: the Day arrives (trumpet, “I am He”, “The Day of God hath been fulfilled”). Stanzas 11 to 19: a run of “Behold” lines, each naming something poured out. Stanza 20: a closing summary of vessel, clouds and birdsong.

What a lyric must not claim

  • Do not present a provisional translation (Lambden, Neshati) as the text of the Writings, and do not print lines from them without permission.
  • Do not say the poem was recited in a particular moment of the Síyáh-Chál, or that the speaker is any named figure, beyond what the preface and Shoghi Effendi say. The preface links the poem to the experience described in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf; the poem itself does not describe a dungeon.
  • Do not say the poem makes the explicit claim to be the Manifestation of God that Bahá’u’lláh made later. The sources here leave the degree of that claim open, and some scholars say it is not explicit.
  • Do not give a meaning to the single letters (“Ṭá’”, “Há’”, “Bá’”) in the lyric as if it were settled. Quote them as they stand.
  • Do not add a rhyme or line that changes a phrase. If rhyme is needed, the lyric should say it is inspired by the poem rather than quoting it.
