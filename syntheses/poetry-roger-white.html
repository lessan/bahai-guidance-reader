Roger White (1929–1993): A Bahá’í Poet of the Heroes, the Martyrs and the Life of Service

Last updated 17 October 2026

Roger White was a Canadian Bahá’í who wrote poetry, a novel and history while serving at the Bahá’í World Centre in Haifa. His poems are not scripture and carry no authority in matters of the Faith. They are one believer’s response to the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, written in English in the second half of the twentieth century. This page describes the poet, then goes through eight poems from his first major collection, Another Song, Another Season (1979), which bear on the greatness of this Day, on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, on the heroes and martyrs, and on the life of service. It is part of a series; see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets. Related pages: The greatness of this Day and The hour of dawn.

Copyright status: White’s books are copyrighted. The corpus copy of Another Song, Another Season carries the notice “Posted with permission of current copyright holder.” Licence for a published lyric: `permission-needed`. This page therefore quotes only short extracts and describes the rest.

The poet

The facts below come from an obituary by Robert Weinberg (Bahá’í Studies Review 7, London, 1997) and a book review by Hilda Phillips in Dialogue (1987). Both are scholarship and memorial writing, not authoritative texts.

Early life. John Roger White was born in Toronto on 2 June 1929 into a Roman Catholic family of Irish ancestry. According to Weinberg, he told a close friend in his early twenties that he doubted the existence of God. He was introduced to the Faith by Gary Rea-Airth and became a Bahá’í in August 1951. In 1952 he married Helen Owens in a Bahá’í ceremony; both served on the first Spiritual Assembly of Belleville and later on the Assembly of Ottawa. The marriage did not last. Weinberg says Roger moved to Vancouver in 1962.

Work and service. White worked as Clerk to the County Court in Belleville, then as assistant editor of Hansard in Ottawa, and from 1962 for the Supreme Court of British Columbia. Between 1966 and 1969 he was secretary in Kenya to the Hand of the Cause William Sears and, in that period, to the Hands of the Cause in Africa. He then spent two more years with Sears in Palm Springs. In 1971 he went to the Bahá’í World Centre on a loan of six months. Weinberg writes that “twenty years later” he was still serving there. His work included secretarial duties for the Universal House of Justice, work with David Hofman, management of the publishing department, and responsibility for compiling and publishing volumes XIV to XIX of The Bahá’í World and the compendium of volumes I to XII (1981).

Writing. Weinberg reports that while in Haifa he took up creative writing again, encouraged by David Hofman. His books were:

  • Another Song, Another Season (George Ronald, 1979)
  • The Witness of Pebbles (George Ronald, 1981)
  • A Sudden Music, a novel in letters about the first Bahá’í centre in Europe (George Ronald, 1983)
  • One Bird, One Cage, One Flight, over 100 poems on Emily Dickinson (Naturegraph, 1983)
  • The Shell and the Pearl, a short account of the martyrdom of ‘Alí-Aṣghar of Yazd (George Ronald, 1984)
  • Occasions of Grace (George Ronald, 1992)
  • Notes Postmarked the Mountain of God and The Language of There (New Leaf, 1992, published by himself)
  • the text of Forever in Bloom, a book of photographs of the Bahá’í House of Worship in New Delhi (1992)

Weinberg also says White published a book of poems, Summer Window, at his own expense in his early twenties. He was an associate editor of the Israeli English-language journal Voices Israel. Weinberg reports that the persecution of the Bahá’ís in Iran after 1979 led White to write some of his strongest poems, collected in The Witness of Pebbles and Occasions of Grace.

Reception. Weinberg quotes H. M. Balyuzi, from an unpublished review, as saying White wrote “with an elegance and fluency reminiscent of Hafiz.” He quotes the scholar Geoffrey Nash, also from an unpublished review, who called White “the first Bahá’í poet” in many respects. Weinberg’s heading calls him the “poet laureate” of the Bahá’í community; this is a description by the obituary writer, not an office. David Hofman wrote the foreword to Another Song, Another Season. Ron Price’s online study The Emergence of a Bahá’í Consciousness in World Literature: The Poetry of Roger White (2002) is a book-length analysis; the corpus holds its table of contents but not enough of its text to quote.

Views on the arts. Weinberg quotes White addressing Bahá’í youth in Haifa in 1990: committed artists will be “a source of rejuvenation,” and the community should treat its artists “as a gift rather than a problem.”

Last years. White retired in 1991 after heart surgery and returned to Canada, where terminal cancer was diagnosed. Weinberg says he started a poetry group, spoke at firesides and taught the Faith to his surgeon and hospital staff. He died on 10 April 1993 in Richmond, British Columbia.

There is no mention of White in God Passes By or Memorials of the Faithful; the corpus has no authoritative text about him.

The collection used here

Hofman’s foreword to Another Song, Another Season says the “Portrayals” are “of real people, heroes and martyrs and servants of the Bahá’í Faith,” and that “The unifying spirit is the response of widely varied individuals to the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh.” The book has four parts: Portrayals; Glimpses of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (adapted from the diary of Juliet Thompson); a third part of dramatic poems on the early heroes; and Songs and Sonnets. The corpus text is an uncorrected OCR scan ([source]), so some words are garbled. Quotations below are limited to lines the scan reproduces cleanly.

The poems

1. “Martha Root”

The poem is headed 1872–1939 and carries an epigraph attributed to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. The epigraph in the book is abridged and gives no source. It resembles a passage of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá to the American believers that Shoghi Effendi quotes in God Passes By, which includes the words “consider how exalted and lofty is the station you are destined to attain” (GPB XVI.7).

The poem describes Martha Root as outwardly unremarkable, “a dowdy girl” in a contemporary’s words, and sets this against what the poet sees in her: a life spent as a warning against darkness. It ends with a wish for the reader: “Be patient: we may yet ourselves become God’s gadabouts,” and, a line later, “meteoric, expire Martha-like, in conflagrant holy urgency.” (Another Song, Another Season, “Martha Root”)

Shoghi Effendi calls her “the incomparable Martha Root” in his cable on Marion Jack (CF 101.1).

2. “Marion Jack”

The epigraph is “Let them remember Marion Jack . . .” attributed to Shoghi Effendi; that sentence was not located in the corpus. His cable on her death is: “Mourn loss of immortal heroine, Marion Jack, greatly loved and deeply admired by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, a shining example to pioneers of present and future generations of East and West” (CF 101.1).

White’s poem is spoken by a group of art critics who dismiss her painting and her appearance, then fragments of the cable’s words break into the text. The poem ends with a prayer: “Marion! Guide us as we seize the brush!” and “Teach us the colours of immortality!”

3. “Louis G. Gregory”

The poem is headed 1874–1951 and has an epigraph from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, “He is like pure gold; that is why he is acceptable in any market” (the sentence was not found in the reference library, so it is cited only as White prints it). The poem is short. It asks that words be found to honour “this gentleman of colour” and says “the alchemy of service and obedience mints coin of purest gold.” It then recalls the Master’s instruction to travel so that the believers could see him.

4. “The Pioneer”

The epigraph is attributed to Bahá’u’lláh and addresses women as “the soft-flowing waters” and “the breezes of spring”; the scanned text is badly garbled, so it is not quoted. The poem describes an unnamed older woman who can be met “in Carcross, Nairobi, Liverpool, Duluth.” The speaker warns the reader that she is a danger to despair: “I warn you, she is dangerous.” The poem lists her knowledge, her care for people of every background and her refusal to accept evil. It ends: “she tinkers with souls.”The refrain uses the language of threat for the work of teaching and healing.

5. “The Courier”

The epigraph is a passage from the Hidden Words: “Thou art but one step away from the celestial tree of love.” The poem is a series of rhymed couplets addressed to a courier named Salman, who walks from “Land of Ta” to ‘Akká carrying letters. The refrain asks “How many steps, Salman, Salman, To world of God from world of man?” The poem says he “brought the lovers Love’s Own Word,” concealed in his hat. It does not itself say who Salman was or which Tablets he carried. The poem treats the unlettered messenger’s simplicity as a form of discernment.

6. “Rubabih” (Yazd, 1903)

Headed “Yazd 1903”, the poem describes a woman with a bad reputation who watches from her window as a man is dragged through the street by a mob and his wife is beaten. The poem then states: “Even stereotypes make choices.” She takes the children and the wounded woman into her house and heals her. The poem’s closing line says outcasts “have, in every age, come highly recommended.” The poem is a dramatisation, and this page has not checked its details against a historical account.

7. “A Crimson Rain”

This is a sequence of dramatic monologues, each dated and placed: Fort Tabarsí, May 1849; a second fort in autumn (the date is garbled in the scan); and the fort of ‘Alí-Mardán Khán at Zanjan, December 1850. In the first, a young defender holds his dead friend and refuses to let the world forget him: “God keeps his name!” The speaker notes that “Husayn felled, Quddus injured” and that the number of defenders is dwindling. In the second, a townsman speaks scornfully of a martyr and then notes that his head “looked peaceful,” and that the pomegranates are strangely bitter. In the Zanjan letter a young wife writes to her mother from the besieged fort, asking “Am I not still your child, your jigar-gushih?” and says she has cut her hair to bind the muskets. A Dylan Thomas epigraph and a line from God Passes By on Zanjan open parts of the sequence; the second is garbled in the scan.

The poems are imagined voices; they do not cite a historical source.

8. “Glimpses of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá” (Part Two)

A sequence adapted from the diary of Juliet Thompson, published separately in The Bahá’í World volume 15 as well as in the book. It records meetings from ‘Akká in July 1909, Thonon-les-Bains in August, and New York in April 1912. The Master’s sayings in the poem are Thompson’s reports, not authenticated Tablets. In ‘Akká He “bursts upon us like the sun” with a greeting. At Thonon He buys violets from children; when the smallest has not been given anything He says “To this little one I have not given . . .” and, in the diary’s words, “And the Master gave.” The diary entry for 11 April 1912 describes his arrival in New York. This part has no theory of the Day: it shows what the writer saw and records the effect.

Others in the book

Part Three also contains “The Salt”, which addresses Badí‘ by name and asks him about pain and courage: “Tell, tell, Badi’, before fiend stills your tongue.” In Part Four, “Suppliant: Bahjí” is a poem of two stanzas set in the garden at Bahjí, in which the writer sets the hero’s and saint’s hearts against his own: “But mine lies shattered.” The book’s other poems are comic, domestic or satirical, including addresses to Ogden Nash and T. S. Eliot.

Themes and images

  • The unremarkable servant. Martha Root, Marion Jack, Louis Gregory, Rúbábih and the pioneer woman are all drawn as people the world overlooks. The poems take the Guardian’s and the Master’s verdicts as the corrective.
  • Voice from inside. Several poems speak in the first person of a fighter, a wife or a townsman. White chooses the point of view of a witness who does not yet understand.
  • Everyday speech for high themes. Hofman’s foreword says White presents “nobility, dedication, the beauty of sacrifice” in “modes of common speech and everyday concepts.”
  • The Master’s presence. In “Glimpses” the writer reports a force “born of the energy of God” that “spills from Him,” but in plain domestic scenes (tea, children, a waterfall).
  • Not a statement of doctrine. None of these poems states the greatness of the Day. They show its effect on the people who respond.

What is not settled

  • The corpus text is uncorrected OCR. Several lines are garbled and quotations should be checked against the printed book before any use.
  • Only Another Song, Another Season and “Glimpses of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá” are available as poem text. The other books are known only from abstracts and reviews.
  • The date of “Rubabih” in the poem is “Yazd 1903”; the historical basis is not given.
  • Authors of the epigraphs are as White prints them. The Marion Jack and Louis Gregory epigraphs could not be matched to a source in the corpus.
  • The Weinberg and Phillips pieces differ in small ways on White’s publication list (Phillips lists three collections of poems, Weinberg more books); this page follows Weinberg for completeness.
  • Permission to reproduce White’s poems in a published work must come from the current copyright holder; the corpus notice does not name them.

For songwriting

Short, singable phrases:

  • “Marion! Guide us as we seize the brush!” (Marion Jack)
  • “Teach us the colours of immortality!” (Marion Jack)
  • “God keeps his name!” (A Crimson Rain)
  • “How many steps, Salman, Salman” (The Courier)
  • “she tinkers with souls” (The Pioneer)
  • “And the Master gave.” (Glimpses of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá)

Images: a painter’s brush; the hat that hides a letter; pomegranates; hands dyed with henna and with blood; a waterfall; violets sold by children.

A possible arc: begin with the overlooked person, move to the narrow view of the world (the critic, the townsman, the neighbour), and end with the Guardian’s or the Master’s judgement. The Marion Jack and Louis Gregory poems already follow this shape.

What a lyric must not claim:

  • that White’s poems are scripture or that the Master’s words in “Glimpses” are authenticated Tablets;
  • that the historical details in “A Crimson Rain” or “Rubabih” are documented fact;
  • that Shoghi Effendi said “Let them remember Marion Jack” in those words (his cable is the text cited above);
  • that White held an office as “poet laureate”.

The authoritative sentences these poems can point to are Shoghi Effendi on Marion Jack (CF 101.1) and the passage addressed by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá to the American believers (GPB XVI.7). For the Day itself see The greatness of this Day.
