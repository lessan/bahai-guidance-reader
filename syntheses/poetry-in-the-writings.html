Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í Poets

Last updated 17 October 2026

What this series is

Bahá’u’lláh wrote poems in Persian and Arabic, from the Rashḥ-i-‘Amá, written in the Síyáh-Chál in 1852, to the odes and refrains of the Baghdad and Constantinople years. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá also wrote verse. Ṭáhirih was a poet before and after she became a Letter of the Living, and later Bahá’ís, among them Rúḥíyyih Khánum, Roger White and Robert Hayden, wrote poetry in English.

This page lists one page per poem of Bahá’u’lláh, one page for His shorter poems, one page for the poems of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, and one page per later poet. Each page says what the poem is, which English translations exist and of what kind, what is not settled, and ends with notes for songwriting. The passages are also in the shared passage bank (`kb/library/passage-bank/poetry.json`), tagged for other uses.

Translations: authorised, provisional, machine-drafted

Only a few of Bahá’u’lláh’s poems have an authorised English translation: the Rashḥ-i-‘Amá in The Call of the Divine Beloved; the Tablet of the Wondrous Maiden, the Tablet of the Bell and the Tablet of the Immortal Youth in Days of Remembrance; and the Arabic part of the Tablet of the Holy Mariner in Bahá’í Prayers. For the rest, the English texts are provisional translations by individual scholars. The pages label each translation.

The Universal House of Justice describes who may make provisional translations and how they are to be labelled:

Individual believers are free to translate into English any of the Holy Writings for their own use. Such translations may also be issued electronically, and may be published in circulars or journals without review by anyone other than the editors of the publications, but they must, in all cases, be clearly labeled as “provisional translations.” (The Universal House of Justice, Policies Governing Authorized and Provisional Translations, 4 July 1999)

Translations to be included in books are sent to the Bahá’í World Centre for clearance first. A translation is authorised when it is approved by a translation committee appointed by the House of Justice.

For several poems with no authorised translation, this series also has machine translations, made with an AI language model (Gemini 4 Argon, 17 October 2026). No human translator has reviewed them. Each is published as its own page, says at the top that it is a machine translation, and gives a table of how key terms were rendered, a literal version aligned with the original, a readable version and the readings the model was unsure of. They are provisional translations made by a machine, not the text of the Writings, and the pages on the poems do not quote them:

  • The Ode of the Dove
  • The Mathnaví-i-Mubárak
  • Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá
  • Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat
  • The Tablet of the Holy Mariner, Persian part
  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, “Ey gulrukh-i-abhá-yi-man”
  • Four poems attributed to Ṭáhirih, listed on her page

Setting the Writings to music

Shoghi Effendi encouraged musical settings of the revealed text and allowed small changes for metre:

Slight alterations in the text of the prayers are permissible, and I would advise you to give a musical form to the revealed word itself which I feel will be exceedingly effective. (Shoghi Effendi, in his own hand, appended to a letter of 8 April 1931 written on his behalf; The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith)

A letter written on his behalf added a limit on shortening a passage:

No doubt prayers and parts of the Tablets, “Hidden Words”, etc., will be suitable, but he does not feel it would be advisable to abridge any given part, in other words to leave out parts of a paragraph or a meditation and foreshorten it in this manner. (Letter of 3 July 1949 written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi; The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith)

Each page’s notes for songwriting give a licence for every phrase: authorised translations may be used with credit; provisional translations by living translators need the translator’s permission; translations published before 1929 are in the public domain.

The pages

Poems of Bahá’u’lláh

  • Rashḥ-i-‘Amá (The Clouds of the Realms Above): twenty couplets written in the Síyáh-Chál in 1852, the earliest of His writings that survive. Authorised translation, given in full.
  • The Ode of the Dove (Qaṣídiy-i-‘Izz-i-Varqá’íyyih): an Arabic ode of 127 verses written in Kurdistán at the request of the Sufis of Sulaymáníyyih. Two provisional translations, and one passage translated by Shoghi Effendi.
  • Sáqí az ghayb-i-baqá: a Persian ghazal to the Cupbearer, signed “Darvísh”, from the Kurdistán years. One couplet is in authorised English in The Seven Valleys; the rest is in provisional translation.
  • The Mathnaví-i-Mubárak: His longest poem, Persian rhyming couplets of 1863, ending with a lament for Baghdád. Provisional translations only.
  • The Tablet of the Bell (Lawḥ-i-Náqús): calls and a refrain, “Praise be to Thee, O Thou Who art ‘He’”, revealed in Constantinople for the anniversary of the Báb’s Declaration in 1863. Authorised translation.
  • The Wondrous Maiden (Ḥúr-i-‘Ujáb): thirty verses with the refrain “How wondrous … how wondrous indeed!”. Authorised translation.
  • The Immortal Youth (Lawḥ-i-Ghulámu’l-Khuld): “Rejoice! This is the immortal Youth …”, placed with the Declaration of the Báb. Authorised translation.
  • The Tablet of the Holy Mariner (Lawḥ-i-Malláḥu’l-Quds): revealed on the fifth day of Naw-Rúz 1863 with the refrain “Glorified be my Lord, the All-Glorious!”. The Arabic part is in authorised translation; the Persian part is not.
  • Hálih Hálih yá Bishárat: a Persian poem of glad tidings from the last months in Baghdád. Provisional translations only.
  • Other poems and poetic Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh: Az Bágh-i-Iláhí, the Nightingale Tablets, Shikkar-Shikan Shavand, and the ghazals of the Majmú‘ih-yi-Mubárak.

Poems of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá

  • The poems of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: a prayer in eight rhyming couplets with an authorised translation, “O Lord so rich in bounty”, and a Persian love poem in provisional translation.

Bahá’í poets

  • Ṭáhirih: Letter of the Living and poet. Browne’s translations of 1918 are in the public domain; the attribution of her best-known ghazal is disputed.
  • Amatu’l-Bahá Rúḥíyyih Khánum: “This Is Faith”, her elegy for Enoch Olinga, and Poems of the Passing.
  • Roger White: Canadian poet of the heroes, martyrs and pioneers, who served at the Bahá’í World Centre.
  • Ná’ím of Sidih: Persian poet of the time of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, with notes on Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam and other early poets.
  • Robert Hayden: American poet and Bahá’í, Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress.

The poems of the later poets are not scripture. Their pages say so and treat them as literature.

What the run did not find

  • Poems of the Báb. No translated poem of the Báb was found in the corpus; see the last section of Other poems of Bahá’u’lláh.
  • Shorter poems of Bahá’u’lláh. Several poems of the Sulaymáníyyih and Baghdád years are known only by title in the sources, among them Báz-áv-u-bidih-jámí.
  • Turkish poems of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. A scholarly paper says He wrote “a few poems in Persian and in Turkish”; none was found.
  • Originals. The original texts of the Wondrous Maiden, the Tablet of the Bell and the Immortal Youth were not available, so their authorised translations could not be compared with the original here.
  • Machine translations. Their quality has not been checked by a translator.
