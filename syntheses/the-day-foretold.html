The Day Foretold: What Each Tradition Was Promised

Last updated 16 October 2026

The idea

The Bahá’í Writings say that the Day in which Bahá’u’lláh appeared is the Day promised in the scriptures of every religion, and that He is the One each tradition awaited, under the name that tradition used. Bahá’u’lláh writes:

“Great indeed is this Day! The allusions made to it in all the sacred Scriptures as the Day of God attest its greatness.” (Gleanings, GWB VII.2)

and, in the Lawḥ-i-Dunyá:

“In this Day a great festival is taking place in the Realm above; for whatsoever was promised in the sacred Scriptures hath been fulfilled.” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, TB 6.56)

This page is the hub for a series of pages, one for each tradition, that set the tradition’s own scripture beside the Bahá’í texts that speak of its promise. The series is planned as a series of songs, one tradition per song. For the wider theme of what the Writings foretell, see promises-of-the-future.

Shoghi Effendi’s summary

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists the titles under which the traditions awaited the Promised One:

“To Israel He was neither more nor less than the incarnation of the “Everlasting Father”; the “Lord of Hosts” come down “with ten thousands of saints”; to Christendom Christ returned “in the glory of the Father,” to Shí‘ah Islám the return of the Imám Ḥusayn; to Sunní Islám the descent of the “Spirit of God” (Jesus Christ); to the Zoroastrians the promised Sháh-Bahrám; to the Hindus the reincarnation of Krishna; to the Buddhists the fifth Buddha.” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

How the Writings read prophecy

The Prophets return. Bahá’u’lláh writes in the Kitáb-i-Íqán that the Manifestations of God share one station and one Faith, so that any of them may say:

“Wherefore, should one of these Manifestations of Holiness proclaim saying: “I am the return of all the Prophets,” He verily speaketh the truth.” (Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 162)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá explains that “return” in the Holy Books means a return of attributes and not of the person:

“For what the Prophets of God meant by “return” is not the return of the essence but of the attributes; it is not the return of the Manifestation Himself but of His perfections.” (Some Answered Questions, SAQ 81.14)

The titles in the table, such as “the reincarnation of Krishna” or the return of the Imám Ḥusayn, can be read with this explanation in mind. The sources gathered do not apply it to each title one by one.

Each Prophet announces the next. From notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, New York, 5 July 1912:

“The divine Prophets are conjoined in the perfect state of love. Each One has given the glad tidings of His successor’s coming” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.5)

In the same talk He says the glad tidings of the Promised One “are recorded in the Torah, or Old Testament, in the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta, and the books of Buddha” (PUP 80.1).

The state of earlier scriptures. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (22 June 1943) says:

“We cannot be sure of the authenticity, word for word, of any of the past Holy Scriptures except the Qur’án, as they were either not written down during the Prophet’s lifetime or have been changed in the course of time and the originals lost; what we can be sure of is that when Bahá’u’lláh or the Master stated that Zoroaster foretold a Promised One’s coming, it is correct.” (Compilation, Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster, no. 20)

The series therefore quotes each scripture in the translation named on its page, and the Bahá’í reading as the Bahá’í reading.

The Revelation does not claim to be final. Shoghi Effendi writes that this Revelation “emphatically repudiates the claim to be regarded as the final revelation of God’s will and purpose for mankind” (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, WOB 6.44). It claims to be “the culmination of a prophetic cycle and the fulfillment of the promise of all ages” (WOB 6.42), not the end of revelation.

Books by Bahá’ís on the prophecies

Many Bahá’ís have written books on the prophecies of earlier religions. These books quote the scriptures and propose readings of particular verses. They are commentary, not authoritative interpretation: where a reading is also in the Writings, the tradition pages cite the Writings; where it goes beyond them, the pages say so and give it as the author’s reading. Each tradition page has a section, “How Bahá’í authors have read the prophecies”, that sets these readings out verse by verse. The books used include:

  • Christian prophecy: William Sears, Thief in the Night (1961) and The Wine of Astonishment; George Townshend, The Promise of All Ages (1934), The Heart of the Gospel (1939) and Christ and Bahá’u’lláh (1957); Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání, The Brilliant Proof (1912); Ruth Moffett, New Keys to the Book of Revelation; Robert Riggs, The Apocalypse Unsealed; Yamartino, The Second Coming; Michael Sours, The Prophecies of Jesus, with Christopher Buck’s response.
  • The Hebrew Bible: J. E. Esslemont, Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era (1923), chapter 13; Riggs, I, Daniel; Sears, Thief in the Night; the compilation Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Bible.
  • The Qur’án and the traditions: Gulpáygání, The Bahá’í Proofs; Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Qur’án; Peter Khan’s talk on prophecy in his Institute on Islam; Khazeh Fananapazir on the Sermons of Imám ‘Alí.
  • The Bayán: Adib Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 1; the summaries of the Persian Bayán by Peter Terry and Moojan Momen; notes of Nader Saiedi’s talks.
  • Hindu, Buddhist and Zoroastrian prophecy: Moojan Momen, Hinduism and the Bahá’í Faith (1990) and Buddhism and the Bahá’í Faith; Christopher Buck on Kalki and on Bahá’u’lláh as Zoroastrian saviour; Gloria Faizi, The Promise of Lord Krishna; Jamshed Fozdar on Maitreya; Farshid Kazemi, “Celestial Fire”.

Academic studies (Moshe Sharon on Daniel, Susan Maneck on the Yazd conversions, Mehrdad Amanat on the Jews of Iran) are cited as scholarship.

Traditions considered and not covered

No authoritative text found in this run’s searches of the corpus states that the promise of the Sikh, Jain, Confucian, Taoist, Shinto or Indigenous traditions is fulfilled. Two texts name figures outside the seven pages:

  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, from notes of a talk, says: “Blessed souls—whether Moses, Jesus, Zoroaster, Krishna, Buddha, Confucius or Muḥammad—were the cause of the illumination of the world of humanity” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 109.11). This names Confucius as a source of illumination. It does not speak of a promise.
  • A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (13 July 1938) lists nine Prophets, including “the Prophet of the Sabaeans Whose name is unrecorded” (Compilation, Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster, no. 14). It does not say what the Sabaeans were promised.

These traditions are not covered because no authoritative text in the corpus speaks of their promise. The page does not go beyond the texts.

For songwriting

  • The series. One tradition per song. Each tradition page lists singable exact phrases, each with its source and licence status, and notes on that tradition’s own music and poetry.
  • Scripture translations. The translations used (King James Version, 1611; Rodwell’s Qur’án, 1861; Arnold, 1885, and Telang, 1882, for the Gita, and Arnold, 1875, for the hymn of Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda; Wilson’s Vishnu Purana, 1840; Rhys Davids, 1910 and 1921, Warren, 1896, and Carus, 1894, for the Buddhist texts; Darmesteter, 1883, Mills, 1887, and West, 1880, for the Zoroastrian texts; Mulla Firuz’s Desatir, 1818) are in the public domain. The King James Version is printed in the United Kingdom under Crown letters patent.
  • Bahá’í texts. The Writings, Shoghi Effendi’s books and the messages of the Universal House of Justice are taken from the Bahá’í Reference Library. Its terms (bahai.org/legal) require the notice “Copyright © Bahá’í International Community” with attribution, and say: “Any use for a commercial purpose requires permission prior to use” (enquiries to termsofuse@bahai.org). A song published on a monetised channel should be treated as commercial.
  • The Dawn-Breakers. Nabíl’s narrative was translated and edited by Shoghi Effendi and first published in 1932, with copyright held by the National Spiritual Assembly of the Bahá’ís of the United States; the edition in the Bahá’í Reference Library is marked “© Bahá’í International Community”. For lyrics it is under the same terms as the other Bahá’í texts: credit, and prior permission for commercial use. Shoghi Effendi died on 4 November 1957, so in countries where copyright lasts for the author’s life plus seventy years his own books and translations come out of copyright on 1 January 2028. United States terms depend on publication date and renewal; the renewal record for The Dawn-Breakers was not checked.
  • Provisional translations and commentary. Provisional translations need the translator’s permission. The books by Bahá’í authors listed above are under copyright and are not for lyrics.
  • Passage bank. The passages used across the series, tagged by tradition, theme and image, with exact text, source and licence, are in `kb/library/passage-bank/the-day-foretold.json`.
  • Voice. A lyric should say “Bahá’u’lláh writes” or “the Bahá’í Writings say”, and should not claim that a tradition’s own scholars accept the Bahá’í reading.
