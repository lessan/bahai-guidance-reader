The Day Foretold: The Lord of Hosts and the Everlasting Father

Last updated 17 October 2026

What this page covers

This page belongs to the series the-day-foretold, which takes one religious tradition at a time. It sets out what the Hebrew Bible says about a coming Day of the Lord, what the Bahá’í Writings say about how that Day is fulfilled, how Bahá’í authors who are not part of the Writings have read the same verses, and some Jewish people who became Bahá’ís in the nineteenth century. It also describes Jewish sacred music, for a songwriter who wants to choose a musical idiom with care.

Shoghi Effendi gives the title for this tradition in God Passes By:

“To Israel He was neither more nor less than the incarnation of the “Everlasting Father”; the “Lord of Hosts” come down “with ten thousands of saints”” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

Three kinds of source are kept apart below.

  • The promise is the Hebrew Bible, quoted from the King James Version (1611).
  • The fulfilment is the Bahá’í Writings: Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (the Writings), Shoghi Effendi, and the Universal House of Justice. Each quotation says who wrote it. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi or of the House of Justice are labelled as such, and a Research Department memorandum is labelled as a memorandum.
  • Commentary and history are by named authors: Bahá’í authors who wrote books and articles on the prophecies, historians, and academic scholars. They are not authoritative. A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice states the rule: “As Bahá’ís, we know that we must turn to the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, and Shoghi Effendi for authoritative guidance in these matters. When a subject has not been mentioned or explained in the Sacred Writings, we are free to consult other books and to consider the opinions of scholars if we wish to do so.” (letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, quoted in a Research Department reply, `uhj_bible_passages.md`)

The page states the Bahá’í understanding as the Bahá’í understanding. It does not judge how Jewish readers read their own scripture, and it does not claim that Jewish scholars or institutions accept the readings given here.

The promise: the Hebrew Bible

All passages in this section are from the King James Version (1611), saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/jewish/`. The verses are ones that the Bahá’í texts below connect to this Day. Where Shoghi Effendi or Bahá’u’lláh renders a verse in slightly different words, that rendering is given in the next section.

The child, the titles and the Branch

“For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.” (Isaiah 9:6, King James Version)

“And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots:” (Isaiah 11:1). “The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them.” (Isaiah 11:6). “They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain: for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.” (Isaiah 11:9). “And he shall set up an ensign for the nations, and shall assemble the outcasts of Israel, and gather together the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth.” (Isaiah 11:12, King James Version)

The mountain of the house of the LORD

“And it shall come to pass in the last days, that the mountain of the LORD’s house shall be established in the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall flow unto it.” (Isaiah 2:2). “And many people shall go and say, Come ye, and let us go up to the mountain of the LORD, to the house of the God of Jacob; and he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths: for out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem.” (Isaiah 2:3). “And he shall judge among the nations, and shall rebuke many people: and they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” (Isaiah 2:4, King James Version)

The herald, the glory and the light

“And the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it.” (Isaiah 40:5). “O Zion, that bringest good tidings, get thee up into the high mountain; O Jerusalem, that bringest good tidings, lift up thy voice with strength; lift it up, be not afraid; say unto the cities of Judah, Behold your God!” (Isaiah 40:9). “Behold, the Lord GOD will come with strong hand, and his arm shall rule for him: behold, his reward is with him, and his work before him.” (Isaiah 40:10, King James Version)

“The wilderness and the solitary place shall be glad for them; and the desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose.” (Isaiah 35:1). “It shall blossom abundantly, and rejoice even with joy and singing: the glory of Lebanon shall be given unto it, the excellency of Carmel and Sharon, they shall see the glory of the LORD, and the excellency of our God.” (Isaiah 35:2, King James Version)

“Arise, shine; for thy light is come, and the glory of the LORD is risen upon thee.” (Isaiah 60:1, King James Version)

Zion, Carmel and the city of God

“And he said, The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem; and the habitations of the shepherds shall mourn, and the top of Carmel shall wither.” (Amos 1:2, King James Version)

“There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the city of God, the holy place of the tabernacles of the most High.” (Psalm 46:4). “The LORD of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge.” (Psalm 46:7). “Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in the mountain of his holiness.” (Psalm 48:1, King James Version)

The door of hope. “And I will give her her vineyards from thence, and the valley of Achor for a door of hope: and she shall sing there, as in the days of her youth, and as in the day when she came up out of the land of Egypt.” (Hosea 2:15, King James Version)

The King of glory, the glory from the east, and Sinai

“Lift up your heads, O ye gates; and be ye lift up, ye everlasting doors; and the King of glory shall come in.” (Psalm 24:7). “Who is this King of glory? The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory.” (Psalm 24:10, King James Version)

“Afterward he brought me to the gate, even the gate that looketh toward the east:” (Ezekiel 43:1). “And, behold, the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east: and his voice was like a noise of many waters: and the earth shined with his glory.” (Ezekiel 43:2, King James Version)

“In that day also he shall come even to thee from Assyria, and from the fortified cities, and from the fortress even to the river, and from sea to sea, and from mountain to mountain.” (Micah 7:12, King James Version)

“And he said, The LORD came from Sinai, and rose up from Seir unto them; he shined forth from mount Paran, and he came with ten thousands of saints: from his right hand went a fiery law for them.” (Deuteronomy 33:2, King James Version)

The desire of all nations, the Sun of righteousness and the one LORD

“And I will shake all nations, and the desire of all nations shall come: and I will fill this house with glory, saith the LORD of hosts.” (Haggai 2:7, King James Version)

“But it shall be one day which shall be known to the LORD, not day, nor night: but it shall come to pass, that at evening time it shall be light.” (Zechariah 14:7). “And it shall be in that day, that living waters shall go out from Jerusalem; half of them toward the former sea, and half of them toward the hinder sea: in summer and in winter shall it be.” (Zechariah 14:8). “And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one.” (Zechariah 14:9, King James Version)

“But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings; and ye shall go forth, and grow up as calves of the stall.” (Malachi 4:2). “Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD:” (Malachi 4:5, King James Version)

The time of the end

“Then I heard one saint speaking, and another saint said unto that certain saint which spake, How long shall be the vision concerning the daily sacrifice, and the transgression of desolation, to give both the sanctuary and the host to be trodden under foot?” (Daniel 8:13). “And he said unto me, Unto two thousand and three hundred days; then shall the sanctuary be cleansed.” (Daniel 8:14)

“Seventy weeks are determined upon thy people and upon thy holy city, to finish the transgression, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity, and to bring in everlasting righteousness, and to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most Holy.” (Daniel 9:24). “Know therefore and understand, that from the going forth of the commandment to restore and to build Jerusalem unto the Messiah the Prince shall be seven weeks, and threescore and two weeks: the street shall be built again, and the wall, even in troublous times.” (Daniel 9:25).

“But thou, O Daniel, shut up the words, and seal the book, even to the time of the end: many shall run to and fro, and knowledge shall be increased.” (Daniel 12:4). “And he said, Go thy way, Daniel: for the words are closed up and sealed till the time of the end.” (Daniel 12:9). “And from the time that the daily sacrifice shall be taken away, and the abomination that maketh desolate set up, there shall be a thousand two hundred and ninety days.” (Daniel 12:11). “Blessed is he that waiteth, and cometh to the thousand three hundred and five and thirty days.” (Daniel 12:12, King James Version)

The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The titles of the Day

Shoghi Effendi lists what Isaiah and David said of the One to come:

“To Him Isaiah, the greatest of the Jewish prophets, had alluded as the “Glory of the Lord,” the “Everlasting Father,” the “Prince of Peace,” the “Wonderful,” the “Counsellor,” the “Rod come forth out of the stem of Jesse” and the “Branch grown out of His roots,” Who “shall be established upon the throne of David,”” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.13)

and adds that David had sung of Him “in his Psalms, acclaiming Him as the “Lord of Hosts” and the “King of Glory.”” (GPB VI.13). The same paragraph goes on to the other prophets:

“To Him Haggai had referred as the “Desire of all nations,” and Zachariah as the “Branch” Who “shall grow up out of His place,” and “shall build the Temple of the Lord.” Zachariah had extolled Him as the “Lord” Who “shall be king over all the earth,” while to His day Joel and Zephaniah had both referred as the “day of Jehovah,” the latter describing it as “a day of wrath, a day of trouble and distress, a day of wasteness and desolation, a day of darkness and gloominess, a day of clouds and thick darkness, a day of the trumpet and alarm against the fenced cities, and against the high towers.” His Day Ezekiel and Daniel had, moreover, both acclaimed as the “day of the Lord,” and Malachi described as “the great and dreadful day of the Lord” when “the Sun of Righteousness” will “arise, with healing in His wings,” whilst Daniel had pronounced His advent as signalizing the end of the “abomination that maketh desolate.”” (GPB VI.13)

Writing of Baghdad, Shoghi Effendi says that “the Tabernacle of the promised “Lord of Hosts” was first erected” there (GPB VII.14).

Bahá’u’lláh states the identification in His own voice. “I am the One Whom the tongue of Isaiah hath extolled, the One with Whose name both the Torah and the Evangel were adorned.” (Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 77; also WOB 6.20). In a Tablet excerpt He writes: “The Promised Day is come and the Lord of Hosts hath appeared.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, TB 17.36).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá says of the same title: “…blessed Person of the Promised One is interpreted in the Holy Book as the Lord of Hosts—the heavenly armies.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablets of the Divine Plan, TDP 8.1). In Some Answered Questions He lists what each people was promised and puts the Jewish promise first: “In the Torah, the Jews are promised the Lord of Hosts and the Messiah.” (SAQ 10.8). He means that every religion awaited two Manifestations, one after the other, and He gives the pair for the Torah as the Lord of Hosts and the Messiah.

“The Father”. The title “Everlasting Father” is Isaiah’s (Isaiah 9:6) and Shoghi Effendi’s (GPB VI.11, VI.13). Bahá’u’lláh uses the title of Himself in an address to Christian bishops, quoted by Shoghi Effendi: “Say: O concourse of bishops! Trembling hath seized all the kindreds of the earth, and He Who is the Everlasting Father calleth aloud between earth and heaven.” (Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 253). In a Tablet that names the Jordan, Sinai and the Burning Bush He writes: “Say, Lo! The Father is come, and that which ye were promised in the Kingdom is fulfilled!” (Bahá’u’lláh, TB 2.7; also SLH 1.159).

Words to the Jewish people

Shoghi Effendi introduces a passage as addressed to the Jewish people:

“The Most Great Law is come, and the Ancient Beauty ruleth upon the throne of David.” (Bahá’u’lláh, quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 191)

The same passage continues: “The Breath hath been wafted, and the Breeze hath blown, and from Zion hath appeared that which was hidden, and from Jerusalem is heard the Voice of God, the One, the Incomparable, the Omniscient.” (PDC 191). Shoghi Effendi summarises it: “To the Jewish people, He, moreover, announced that the Most Great Law has come, that “the Ancient Beauty ruleth upon the throne of David,”” (GPB XII.35).

Bahá’u’lláh writes in the Gleanings: “The promises of God, as recorded in the holy Scriptures, have all been fulfilled. Out of Zion hath gone forth the Law of God, and Jerusalem, and the hills and land thereof, are filled with the glory of His Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, GWB X.1). In another passage He says: “We have, of old, uttered these words: Spread thy skirt, O Jerusalem!” (GWB LIX.4).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in a passage cited in The Advent of Divine Justice: “Praise be to God that whatsoever hath been announced in the Blessed Tablets unto the Israelites, and the things explicitly written in the letters of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, are all being fulfilled. Some have come to pass; others will be revealed in the future. The Ancient Beauty hath in His Sacred Tablets explicitly written that the day of their abasement is over.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, cited in The Advent of Divine Justice, pp. 55–56; see `uhj_unity-religions_jews_ishmael.md`)

Zion, Carmel and ‘Akká: Isaiah 40:9, Amos 1:2, Isaiah 35:2, Psalm 24 and Hosea 2:15

In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf Bahá’u’lláh quotes Isaiah 40:9 and then says: “This Day all the signs have appeared. A Great City hath descended from heaven, and Zion trembleth and exulteth with joy at the Revelation of God” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 209). He quotes Amos and comments: “Carmel, in the Book of God, hath been designated as the Hill of God, and His Vineyard. It is here that, by the grace of the Lord of Revelation, the Tabernacle of Glory hath been raised.” (ESW 210). He quotes David’s “Who will bring me into the Strong City?” and says: “The Strong City is ‘Akká, which hath been named the Most Great Prison” (ESW 208). In the same book He quotes Isaiah 35 in a slightly different wording from the King James Version: “the wilderness and the solitary place shall be glad for them; and the desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose. It shall blossom abundantly, and rejoice even with joy and singing: the glory of Lebanon shall be given unto it, the splendor of Carmel and Sharon, they shall see the glory of the Lord, and the splendor of our God.” (ESW 212). He also quotes Amos on “the Lord, the God of Hosts” (ESW 211).

In the Tablet of Carmel He addresses the mountain:

“Rejoice, for God hath in this Day established upon thee His throne, hath made thee the dawning-place of His signs and the dayspring of the evidences of His Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Tablet of Carmel, TB 1.3)

“Call out to Zion, O Carmel, and announce the joyful tidings: He that was hidden from mortal eyes is come! His all-conquering sovereignty is manifest; His all-encompassing splendor is revealed.” (TB 1.4)

The Tablet ends: “Ere long will God sail His Ark upon thee, and will manifest the people of Bahá who have been mentioned in the Book of Names.” (TB 1.4). Shoghi Effendi read the Ark of the Tablet of Carmel as the World Administrative Center on the mountain. The Universal House of Justice quotes his description of the Archives building, the first of the buildings on the Arc, as “the first stately Edifice destined to usher in the establishment of the World Administrative Center of the Faith on Mount Carmel—the Ark referred to by Bahá’u’lláh in the closing passages of His Tablet of Carmel.” (Shoghi Effendi, quoted in a message of the Universal House of Justice to all National Spiritual Assemblies, 4 January 1994, `merged_by_year/1994.md`).

In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas Bahá’u’lláh gives Zion a voice:

“Carmel hath, in this Day, hastened in longing adoration to attain His court, whilst from the heart of Zion there cometh the cry: “The promise is fulfilled. That which had been announced in the holy Writ of God, the Most Exalted, the Almighty, the Best-Beloved, is made manifest.”” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, KA 80)

On the Holy Land itself He writes: “Whatever hath come to pass in this Day hath been foretold in the Scriptures of old.” (GWB CLXIV.7). Shoghi Effendi gathers the prophecies about the Holy Land. First, on the Lord of Hosts appearing there:

“God, “faithful to His promise,” had, “to some of the Prophets” “revealed and given the good news that the ‘Lord of Hosts should be manifested in the Holy Land.’”” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB XI.2)

He then lists Isaiah on the high mountain, David on the King of Glory and Amos on Carmel (GPB XI.2). Next, on ‘Akká:

“‘Akká, itself, flanked by the “glory of Lebanon,” and lying in full view of the “splendor of Carmel,” at the foot of the hills which enclose the home of Jesus Christ Himself, had been described by David as “the Strong City,” designated by Hosea as “a door of hope,” and alluded to by Ezekiel as “the gate that looketh towards the East,” whereunto “the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the East,” His voice “like a noise of many waters.”” (GPB XI.3)

And on Carmel, in 1868:

“In that same year Bahá’u’lláh’s tent, the “Tabernacle of Glory,” was raised on Mt. Carmel, “the Hill of God and His Vineyard,” the home of Elijah, extolled by Isaiah as the “mountain of the Lord,” to which “all nations shall flow.”” (GPB XI.28)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in a Tablet to a woman believer, writes of Hosea’s verse: “This valley of Achor is the city of ‘Akká, and whoso hath interpreted this otherwise is of those who know not.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, SWAB 139.9)

In the same message of 4 January 1994 the Universal House of Justice applies Isaiah 2:2 to Carmel: “Mount Carmel was extolled by the prophet Isaiah almost three thousand years ago, when he announced that “it shall come to pass in the last days, that the mountain of the Lord’s house shall be established in the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall flow unto it.”” (Universal House of Justice, 4 January 1994).

Isaiah 2 and 11: the peace of the Day

In Some Answered Questions ‘Abdu’l-Bahá comments on Isaiah 11:1–9. He first considers whether the passage fits Christ. “This “rod out of the stem of Jesse” might seem to apply to Christ, for Joseph was a descendant of Jesse, the father of David.” (SAQ 12.2). He gives two reasons that it does not: Christ called Himself the Son of God, and the events described came to pass only in part, or not at all, in the Christian Dispensation. On the wolf and the lamb He says that the peace of the passage “did not materialize in the Dispensation of Christ” (SAQ 12.3). He then states: “But these verses apply word for word to Bahá’u’lláh.” (SAQ 12.4).

He reads the animals as peoples. “Strong and weak, rich and poor, contending kindreds and hostile nations—which are like the wolf and the lamb, the leopard and kid, the lion and the calf—will treat one another with the utmost love, unity, justice, and equity.” (SAQ 12.4).

On the ensign of Isaiah 11:10 and 11:12 He writes that the hoisting of “the Standard of God among all nations” is one of the great events of the Day of “that Incomparable Branch”, and that it means all nations gathering under one banner (SAQ 12.7). On the gathering of Israel He says: “That Incomparable Branch will gather together all Israel; that is, in His Dispensation Israel will be gathered in the Holy Land, and the Jewish people who are now scattered in the East and the West, the North and the South, will be assembled together.” (SAQ 12.7). The next paragraph continues: “Likewise Israel, which had been scattered throughout the world, was not gathered together in the Holy Land in the course of the Christian Dispensation, but in the beginning of the Dispensation of Bahá’u’lláh this divine promise, which has been clearly stated in all the Books of the Prophets, has begun to materialize.” (SAQ 12.8). This page gives these as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s statements of the fulfilment of the prophecy. It does not address any present political question.

Shoghi Effendi quotes the same group of verses from Isaiah 2 and 11 as a vision of the maturity of humankind: “No less enthralling is the vision of Isaiah, the greatest of the Hebrew Prophets, predicting, as far back as twenty five hundred years ago, the destiny which mankind must, at its stage of maturity, achieve” (Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, WOB 7.113). Shoghi Effendi writes of “the establishment of the Lesser Peace, as foretold by Bahá’u’lláh and foreshadowed by the Prophet Isaiah” (Shoghi Effendi, as quoted in a Research Department memorandum enclosed with a letter from the Department of the Secretariat of the Universal House of Justice, 19 April 2001, `merged_by_year/2001.md`).

Sinai and Moses

Shoghi Effendi uses Deuteronomy 33:2 in the sentence quoted at the top of this page: the “Lord of Hosts” come down “with ten thousands of saints”” (GPB VI.11). Bahá’u’lláh writes: “The episode of Sinai hath been reenacted in this Revelation and He Who conversed upon the Mount is calling aloud: Verily, the Desired One is come, seated upon the throne of certitude, could ye but perceive it.” (Bahá’u’lláh, TB 17.60). In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas: “This is the Day in which He Who held converse with God hath attained the light of the Ancient of Days, and quaffed the pure waters of reunion from this Cup that hath caused the seas to swell. Say: By the one true God! Sinai is circling round the Dayspring of Revelation” (KA 80). And in the Tablet where the Father is announced: “the Burning Bush calleth aloud: “He Who is the Desired One is come in His transcendent majesty.”” (TB 2.7). The Writings also say that Moses would have rejoiced in this Day: “Had Moses Himself attained it, He, likewise, would have raised His voice saying: “All praise be to Thee for having lifted upon me the light of Thy countenance and enrolled me among them that have been privileged to behold Thy face!”” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, SLH 2.18). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is quoted in the World Order of Bahá’u’lláh: “The Blessed Beauty is the One promised by the sacred books of the past, the revelation of the Source of light that shone upon Mount Sinai” (WOB 6.66).

The Writings honour Moses and the Prophets of Israel. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes what Moses did for the Israelites: “That people had sunk to the depths of degradation and were lifted up to the heights of glory.” (SAQ 5.2). In a talk at Temple Emmanu-El in San Francisco on 12 October 1912 (from notes of a talk, by Bijou Straun) He said: “From this review of the history of the Jewish people we learn that the foundation of the religion of God laid by Moses was the cause of their eternal honor and national prestige” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 112.9), and, speaking as a Bahá’í: “Verily, I now declare to you that Moses was the Interlocutor of God and a most noteworthy Prophet, that Moses revealed the fundamental law of God and founded the real ethical basis of the civilization and progress of humanity.” (PUP 112.19). Shoghi Effendi calls Isaiah “the greatest of the Jewish prophets” (GPB VI.13).

Daniel: the dates

‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives the dates in Some Answered Questions, chapter 10. This section follows that chapter in order and then sets out, in separate layers, what Shoghi Effendi, letters written on his behalf, the Universal House of Justice and the Research Department have said. The dates are ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reckoning of Daniel’s verses. They are not numbers that Daniel gives as years.

The rule: a day is a year. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states the rule once and uses it throughout: “Each day, according to the text of the Bible, is one year, for in the Torah it is said: “The day of the Lord is one year.”” (SAQ 10.12). (The scholar Moshe Sharon disputes the biblical basis of this sentence; see the commentary section below.)

The seventy weeks (Daniel 9:24–27): the advent of Christ. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá begins with the Messiah, as a proof that the method gives sound results before He applies it to the Báb: “The Book of Daniel fixes the period between the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the martyrdom of Christ at seventy weeks, for it is through the martyrdom of Christ that the sacrifice is ended and the altar destroyed. This prophecy thus refers to the advent of Christ.” (SAQ 10.10). He lists four edicts to rebuild Jerusalem, by Cyrus in 536 B.C., Darius in 519 B.C., Artaxerxes in 457 B.C. and Artaxerxes in 444 B.C. (SAQ 10.11), and says of the third: “What Daniel intended is the third edict, which was issued in 457 B.C.” (SAQ 10.12). He counts seventy weeks as 490 years: “The third edict of Artaxerxes was issued 457 years before the birth of Christ, and Christ was thirty-three years old at the time of His martyrdom and ascension. Thirty-three added to 457 is 490, which is the time announced by Daniel for the advent of Christ.” (SAQ 10.12). He then reconciles Daniel’s “seven weeks, and threescore and two weeks” with the seventy: “The rebuilding of Jerusalem took seven weeks, which is equivalent to forty-nine years. Seven weeks added to sixty-two weeks makes sixty-nine weeks, and in the last week the ascension of Christ took place.” (SAQ 10.14).

The 2,300 days (Daniel 8:13–14): the advent of the Báb in 1844. After quoting the verses He says: “Briefly, the point is that he fixes a period of 2,300 years, for according to the text of the Torah each day is one year.” (SAQ 10.16). His count in that paragraph is: “from the date of the edict of Artaxerxes to rebuild Jerusalem until the day of the birth of Christ there are 456 years, and from the birth of Christ until the day of the advent of the Báb there are 1,844 years, and if 456 years are added to this number it makes 2,300 years.” He concludes: “That is to say, the fulfilment of the vision of Daniel took place in A.D. 1844, and this is the year of the advent of the Báb.” (SAQ 10.16). Two figures appear in the chapter for the same edict: 457 B.C. as the date of the edict (SAQ 10.12) and 456 years from the edict to the birth of Christ (SAQ 10.16). Some commentators below give 456 B.C. as the date; that is their figure. The chapter then says that Christ pointed His hearers to this passage. He quotes Matthew 24:15, “When ye therefore shall see the abomination of desolation, spoken of by Daniel the prophet, stand in the holy place, (whoso readeth, let him understand)”, and says: “Thus He referred them to the eighth chapter of the Book of Daniel, implying that whoever reads it should grasp when that time shall be.” (SAQ 10.17)

The time, times and half a time (Daniel 12:6–7): 1260. “Thus three and a half years makes forty-two months, and forty-two months is 1,260 days, and each day in the Bible is equivalent to one year. And it is in the very year 1260 from the emigration of Muḥammad, according to the Muslim calendar, that the Báb, the Herald of Bahá’u’lláh, revealed His mission.” (SAQ 10.20). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá returns to the same number from the Book of Revelation: “The Book of Revelation fixes the advent of the Promised One after forty-two months. The Prophet Daniel specifies three times and a half, which is also forty-two months or 1,260 days.” (SAQ 13.13).

The 1,290 days (Daniel 12:11): the declaration of Bahá’u’lláh. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá counts these as lunar years from the public announcement of Muhammad’s mission, three years after its beginning: “And it was in the year 1290 from the proclamation of the mission of Muḥammad that Bahá’u’lláh announced His Revelation.” (SAQ 10.22). The Research Department works the arithmetic. The proclamation was ten years before the Hijra, so “the year 1290 from the proclamation of the mission of Muhammad was the year 1280 of the Hegira, or 1863-64 A.D.” (Research Department memorandum, “Daniel’s Prophecies”, 13 December 1990, revised February 1996, paraphrased from its section 1). Shoghi Effendi writes of Bahá’u’lláh’s declaration in the Garden of Ridván: “The “thousand two hundred and ninety days,” fixed by Daniel in the last chapter of His Book, as the duration of the “abomination that maketh desolate” had now elapsed.” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB IX.2).

The 1,335 days (Daniel 12:12): five layers. In the text of Some Answered Questions ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes the verse (SAQ 10.21) but does not explain the 1,335 days there. The explanation comes from other texts. They are listed below, each labelled.

  1. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, a Tablet published in The Passing of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (1922) and quoted by Esslemont. “These days must be reckoned as solar and not lunar years. For according to this calculation a century will have elapsed from the dawn of the Sun of Truth, then will the teachings of God be firmly established upon the earth, and the Divine Light shall flood the world from the East even unto the West. Then, on this day, will the faithful rejoice!” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, quoted in Esslemont, Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era, BNE 14.78). The Research Department memorandum quotes a second Tablet from the same book which counts the 1,335 years from the day of the flight of Muḥammad, “from the day of the flight of His Holiness” being its wording (the Hijra, A.D. 622) and says that at the close of that period “the signs of the rise, the glory, the exaltation, the spread of the Word of God throughout the East and the West shall appear.” The memorandum notes that the two Tablets “suggest that the prophecy is fulfilled by two different dates”, one from the centenary of the Declaration and one calculated from 622 A.D., “hence, 1963 and 1957.”
  1. Shoghi Effendi. In God Passes By he writes of the Declaration of 1863 that “The “hundred lunar years,” destined to immediately precede that blissful consummation (1335 days), announced by Daniel in that same chapter, had commenced.” (GPB IX.2). In October 1952, announcing the Ten Year Crusade, he cabled that the believers should vow to go on until each had contributed a share in laying “an unassailable administrative foundation for Bahá’u’lláh’s Christ-promised Kingdom on earth”, “as prophesied Daniel echoed ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on that day will faithful rejoice with exceeding gladness” (cable of October 1952, Messages to the Bahá’í World, p. 44, as quoted in the Research Department memorandum; the original is in capitals, without diacritics).
  1. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (by his secretaries). They give different dates, and the Research Department memorandum sets them side by side. A letter of 31 July 1946 connects “the prophecy of Daniel — 1957” with the spread of the Cause. A letter of 4 May 1946 says of Esslemont’s report of a table-talk: “we cannot be absolutely certain of what the Master said because it is not in a Tablet; He did state, however, in 2 Tablets, that this date will see the triumph of the Cause.” A letter of 1 April 1946 says that “‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s interpretation of the prophecy of Daniel does not definitely connect it with either the Lesser or the Most Great Peace, but rather with a great step forward on the part of humanity and the Bahá’í Faith.” On 30 June 1952, answering a National Spiritual Assembly that asked whether the 1,335 days end in 1957 or in 1963, a letter on his behalf says: “Regarding the prophecy of Daniel … this prophecy refers to the hundredth anniversary of the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Ridvan, Baghdad.” Letters of 7 March 1955 and 11 May 1956 tie it to the Ten Year Crusade. The first says: “Thus in the Ten Year Crusade, we are actually fulfilling the prophecy of Daniel”. The second says: “when we fulfil the Ten Year Crusade, we will have brought into fulfilment Daniel’s great prophecy of “Blessed is he who waits and comes to the 1335 days.”” On calculation, the memorandum gives two replies. The first, which ends with the date 18 December 1953, says that “The 1335 days referred to by Daniel will be fulfilled in 1963”, with the days “figured according to the solar calendar” from the Hijra, and that “this prophecy refers to happenings within the Faith, not occurrences outside the Faith.” The second, of 31 January 1955, says that the reckoning “does not bring it to the exact date of 1963, but a few more years”, and that this is no conflict because ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “says that it will be the beginning of the diffusion of the Light of the Faith in the east and the west.”
  1. The Universal House of Justice, 30 April 1963. At the Most Great Jubilee, the centenary of the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh, the House of Justice sent a message to the First Bahá’í World Congress. It says: “This historic moment marks at one and the same time the fulfillment of Daniel’s prophecy, the Hundredth Anniversary of the Declaration of the Promised One of all ages, the termination of the first epoch of the Divine Plan of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá” and the conclusion of Shoghi Effendi’s world-encircling Crusade (Universal House of Justice, 30 April 1963). The message names Daniel’s prophecy and the moment of the Jubilee. It does not name a verse, and it gives no calculation.
  1. The Research Department memorandum (13 December 1990, revised February 1996). This is a memorandum to the House of Justice, not a statement by it. After setting out the texts above, the Department found that “the prophecy concerning the 1,335 days is associated with the spread of the Bahá’í Faith throughout the world” and that the texts give “several different dates”, which the memorandum lists as 1957, 1960 (a hundred lunar years by “a lunar reckoning” after the Declaration of 1863) and 1963. It adds that a letter of 1 April 1946 gives 1853 as the start of a different hundred-year period, and says that another explanation is needed for the significance of that period. It records that it “has not been able to locate any additional authoritative texts which contain detailed instructions concerning how to make the calculation.” Its suggestion is that “The prophecy of Daniel about the 1,335 days is not fulfilled by just one single date. Its fulfilment is, rather, a process that extends over a period of time”, coinciding with the Ten Year Crusade, 1953–1963. A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 17 January 1978 recommends continued study of Some Answered Questions, “since this book contains the interpretations given by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on the meaning of some of the passages you mention from the Book of Daniel” (`uhj_old_new_testaments_fulltext.md`).

What the Writings say of Jewish expectation

The Writings describe the Jewish expectation of the Messiah in the tradition’s own terms, and they explain the descriptions as symbols. In a Tablet ‘Abdu’l-Bahá writes that the signs of the Messiah recorded in the Torah “did come to pass in the Manifestation of Christ, although not in the sense which the Jews understood, the description in the Torah being allegorical.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, SWAB 20.2). In a talk in Brooklyn on 16 June 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “All the words of the prophets were fulfilled, but because the Jews held tenaciously to hereditary interpretations, they did not understand the inner meanings of the Holy Bible; therefore, they denied Jesus Christ, the Messiah.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 71.11).

The same two points are in the Kitáb-i-Íqán and the Summons of the Lord of Hosts, where Bahá’u’lláh sets out what the Jewish teachers held. In the Íqán He writes that when Jesus came, “all the people of Israel arose in protest against Him. They clamored that He Whose advent the Bible had foretold must needs promulgate and fulfill the laws of Moses” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 17). The passage continues in severe terms about the cause of that protest. The page records them as Bahá’u’lláh’s words and does not repeat them. In the Summons He writes: “The doctors of the Torah in particular assert that no independent Prophet will come after Moses with a new Law. They maintain that a Scion of the House of David shall be made manifest Who will promulgate the Law of the Torah, and help establish and enforce its commandments throughout the East and the West.” (SLH 1.246). In the Íqán He answers the claim that no Messenger can follow Moses by the Qur’anic verse on the hand of God and asks: “How can He be conceived as powerless to raise up yet another Messenger after Moses?” (KI 148).

These are accounts of the reasons that were given in the past. They are not a statement about Jewish people today.

#### ‘Abdu’l-Bahá among Jewish audiences in 1912

On two days in November 1912 ‘Abdu’l-Bahá spoke at Washington’s Eighth Street Temple and then to a rabbi who called on Him. Both talks are in The Promulgation of Universal Peace (from notes by Joseph H. Hannen); the earlier talk at Temple Emmanu-El in San Francisco is quoted above.

The first, on 8 November 1912, begins: “God is one, the effulgence of God is one, and humanity constitutes the servants of that one God.” (PUP 121.1). It ends with a statement on prejudice: “For God is one and humanity is one, and the only creed of the Prophets is love and unity.” (PUP 121.17). The next day, at the home of Mr. and Mrs. Arthur J. Parsons, He spoke of the rabbi who had visited Him: “The address delivered last evening in the Jewish synagogue evidently disturbed some of the people, including the revered rabbi who called upon me this afternoon.” (PUP 122.1). He asked what harm could result if Jews declared “that Christ was the Word of God” and said that it “would cause the enmity of many centuries to pass away.” (PUP 122.10). The record ends the exchange: “The rabbi thoughtfully said, “I believe that what you have said is perfectly true, but I must ask one thing of you. Will you not tell the Christians to love us a little more?”” (PUP 122.12). The notes give ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s answer in the next sentence as “We have advised them and will continue to do so.”

How Bahá’í authors have read the prophecies

The texts in this section are not part of the Writings. They are books and articles by Bahá’í authors, a compilation prepared by a National Spiritual Assembly, and academic scholarship. They quote the Hebrew Bible and propose readings of it. Each reading below is labelled with its author, says whether the Writings make the same reading, and is otherwise given as that author’s reading only. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 31 January 1955 sets the limit: “Except for what has been explained by Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, we have no way of knowing what various symbolic allusions in the Bible mean.” (`uhj_old_new_testaments_fulltext.md`)

John Esslemont, Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era (first edition 1923), chapter 13

Esslemont’s book was first published in 1923. Its chapter 13 is a survey of Bible prophecy. Esslemont begins with a rule that matches what the Writings say about sealed prophecy: “If God sealed up the prophecies until the appointed time, and did not fully reveal the interpretation even to the prophets who uttered them, we may expect that none but the appointed Messenger of God will be able to break the seal and disclose the meanings concealed in the casket of the prophetic parables.” (Esslemont, BNE 13.3)

  • Isaiah 9:6. Esslemont argues that the verse fits Bahá’u’lláh more fully than Christ. Esslemont’s argument is that Bahá’u’lláh “by dealing at great length in His Writings with questions of government and administration” has taken “the government upon His shoulders”, and that He calls Himself the manifestation of the Father, where Christ called Himself the Son (BNE 13.13). The Writings do apply the titles of Isaiah 9:6 to Bahá’u’lláh (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.13, quoted above). Esslemont’s argument from government and administration is Esslemont’s own.
  • Isaiah 40:5. Esslemont notes that “Bahá’u’lláh” is Arabic for “Glory of God”, and that the Hebrew prophets “frequently” use this title for the Promised One (BNE 13.14). Shoghi Effendi lists “Glory of the Lord” among Isaiah’s titles for Him (GPB VI.13). The link to the meaning of the name is Esslemont’s.
  • The East. Esslemont writes that “Bahá’u’lláh appeared in Persia, which is eastward from Palestine, towards the rising of the sun”, and came to the Holy Land as an exile and prisoner (BNE 13.17). The Writings that read the “gate that looketh towards the East” (Ezekiel 43) take it to be ‘Akká (Shoghi Effendi, GPB XI.3). Esslemont’s reading, that the Glory comes from the east, is a different one.
  • Daniel 12:12. In the first edition (1923, p. 212) Esslemont recorded a table-talk of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in which the 1,335 days were reckoned as solar years from the Hijra and gave 1957 as the year, with universal peace to follow. This is the case the Research Department discusses in a memorandum of 6 November 1990. A letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi of 4 May 1946 says: “we cannot be absolutely certain of what the Master said because it is not in a Tablet”. A “Note on Revisions” in the 1946 edition says that “The Guardian has written that in the Bahá’í teachings themselves there is nothing to indicate that any definite degree of world peace will be established by 1957, nor by 1963”. Later editions quote instead the two Tablets in The Passing of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, as above (BNE 14.77–78). The history shows the difference between a Tablet and a pilgrim’s or an author’s record of a talk.

William Sears, Thief in the Night (1961)

Sears, a Hand of the Cause, wrote an account of how the prophecies were found, with Daniel for the time and other books for the place. These readings are Sears’s own. Several overlap with passages the Writings quote.

  • Isaiah 35:2, 40:5 and 60:1: “the Glory of the Lord”. Sears reads these verses together and says the title “the Glory of God” would be the Messiah’s name when He appeared, with Carmel and Sharon as the place of its light. The Writings quote Isaiah 35:2 and 40:5 (Bahá’u’lláh, ESW 212; Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.13, XI.3), but they do not read Isaiah 60:1 in the texts gathered for this page. Sears also reports that a group among the millennial Bible students of the 1844 period expected the Glory of God to appear on Mount Carmel and sold their possessions and sailed for the Holy Land. That is Sears’s historical account, and this page has not checked it.
  • Micah 7:12. Sears turns to Micah for the place of the Messiah’s second appearance, finding in “In that day also he shall come even to thee from Assyria” a pointer to Bahá’u’lláh’s exile from Baghdád to the fortified city of Constantinople. The Writings gathered here do not cite Micah 7:12. A Warwick Bahá’í Bookshop leaflet, “Judaism and the Bahá’í Faith”, makes the same mapping of “Assyria”, “the fortified cities” and “the fortress” onto Bahá’u’lláh’s banishments.
  • Psalm 24 and the year 1868. Sears quotes Psalm 24:7–10 as a prophecy of David and ties it to Bahá’u’lláh’s arrival at ‘Akká in 1868, which Sears draws from God Passes By, p. 183. Sears adds a sixteenth-century Bible commentator, Samuel ben Judah Valerio, who by Sears’s report calculated the end of the Jewish exile as the Jewish year 5628, which is 1868. This page has not seen Valerio’s text. Shoghi Effendi quotes Psalm 24:7–10 as David’s prediction of the Lord of Hosts in the Holy Land (GPB XI.2). The link with the date 1868 is Sears’s.

Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Bible (compilation, Central and East Africa, first published 1963)

This compilation was prepared for teaching in Africa. It lists, under the heading of prophecies “specifically regarding ‘Akká”, Hosea 2:15, Isaiah 65:10 and Ezekiel 43:1–2, and lists Isaiah 2:2–3 among prophecies that “came true” with the exile to ‘Akká. The Writings make the Hosea and Ezekiel identifications (SWAB 139.9, GPB XI.3). For Daniel it says that the 2,300 days are counted “from the date of the rebuilding of Jerusalem which was in 456 bc”, and that “The date we get is again 1844!” This uses 456 B.C. for the date, where Some Answered Questions gives 457 B.C. for the edict (SAQ 10.12) and 456 years from the edict to the birth of Christ (SAQ 10.16).

Daniel 8:14 and 1844: Munje, Maddocks and Riggs

Three Bahá’í authors take up the 2,300 days in different ways.

  • H. M. Munje, “1844 A.D.” Munje states the rule that “a day is equal to a year” from the Book of Numbers, takes the sanctuary to have been built by Ezra in 456 B.C., and subtracts 456 from 2,300 to reach 1844 (file `munje_1844_target_faiths_fulltext.md`). This follows ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s year but starts the count from Ezra, where ‘Abdu’l-Bahá starts it from the edict of Artaxerxes (SAQ 10.16).
  • Eileen Maddocks, “1844”. Maddocks writes about William Miller, whose calculation of the Second Advent for 1844 rested on Daniel 8:14. Maddocks notes that Miller took “the prophecies of Daniel to heart, especially Daniel 8:14”, and that Daniel’s words were “rolled up and sealed until the time of the end” (Daniel 12:9). Maddocks gives the history of the expectation, not a separate calculation. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá cites Matthew 24:15 as a pointer from Christ to Daniel 8 (SAQ 10.17).
  • Robert F. Riggs, I, Daniel. Riggs takes “The 2300 evenings and mornings are 2300 exactly-completed years” and counts them from Ezra’s journey under the first decree of Artaxerxes, so that they end at the Edict of Toleration of 1844, about two months before the Báb’s declaration of 23 May 1844. For the 1,335 days Riggs says that, because neither ‘Abdu’l-Bahá nor Shoghi Effendi “shed enough light” on the method, Riggs proposes two start dates, 622 (the Hijra) and 628 (the Truce of al-Hudaybiyyah), giving 1957, linked to the passing of Shoghi Effendi, and 1963, linked to the founding of the Universal House of Justice. The Research Department found no authoritative text that gives a method of calculation, so these start dates are Riggs’s proposal.

Other verse-by-verse readings

  • Haggai 2:6–7. Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání lists Haggai 2:6–7, with Joel 2, among the signs of the Day of God, and argues in The Bahá’í Proofs that the signs “are closed up and sealed” until the end. Shoghi Effendi applies “Desire of all nations” to Bahá’u’lláh (GPB VI.13).
  • Ezekiel 43:2. The Warwick leaflet and Yamartino, in The Second Coming, read the verse as the Glory arriving “from the way of the east”, meaning Bahá’u’lláh’s journey from Persia. Shoghi Effendi reads “the gate that looketh towards the East” of Ezekiel 43:1 as ‘Akká (GPB XI.3). The two readings differ in what “east” and “gate” refer to.
  • Malachi 4:5. Esslemont argues that the promised Elijah “must first come” was fulfilled in the person of John the Baptist, who came “in the spirit and power of Elias”, so that a promised return need not be the return of the same person (BNE 13.37). Yamartino draws the same lesson. Stockman, in notes on Judaism, traces the later Jewish belief that Elijah must precede the Messiah to Malachi 4:5. This page has not found a text of the Writings that applies Malachi 4:5 to a named person.

Academic scholarship

  • Moshe Sharon, “End of Days” (in Lights of ‘Irfán, vol. 19). Sharon writes as an academic scholar. Sharon summarises ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s calculations for Daniel 8:14 and 9:24, and makes three criticisms. First, Sharon says that the day-for-a-year rule that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes as “The day of the Lord is one year” rests on a wrong translation of Numbers 14:34, and that the Bible says a day of the Lord is a thousand years (Psalm 90:4), although Sharon adds that there are other sources for the idea that one day could mean one year. Second, Sharon gives the dates accepted there, citing a reference work: Artaxerxes’ reign began in 465 B.C. and Nehemiah received permission to rebuild the walls as late as 445 B.C. Third, Sharon cites Joseph ben ’Uliel (1858–1937), a rabbi born in Morocco, who in 1917 read Daniel’s 1,290 and 1,335 days as pointing to his own time, to show that Daniel’s numbers can be read to fit many dates. This page records these as an academic view. The Bahá’í reading of Daniel rests on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s statements (SAQ 10), and the Writings do not address Sharon’s points on translation and dating.
  • Moshe Sharon, “Jewish Conversion to the Bahá’í Faith” (unpublished). Sharon proposes why Persian Jews were attracted to Bahá’u’lláh. Iranian Jews, Sharon writes, cherished the hope of “a new Cyrus”, after Isaiah 45:1, who would end their humiliation, and Bahá’u’lláh’s message from the Holy Land spoke of a return to Zion. Sharon concludes that the Jews “regarded him as the new Cyrus and as the Jewish Messiah at the same time.” This is an explanation by a scholar of motives. Bahá’u’lláh’s own words about the Return are those quoted under “Words to the Jewish people”, above.
  • Stockman, “Notes on Judaism from a Bahá’í Perspective” (unpublished). Stockman, a Bahá’í author, dates the visions of Daniel 7–12 on historical-critical grounds to about 168 B.C. and describes the Elijah expectation as a later development. Stockman’s notes are an example of the academic reading of Daniel, which differs from reading its numbers as predictions of 1844 and later.

Those who recognised it

This section rests on history by Shoghi Effendi, on the historian Adib Taherzadeh, and on scholarship. Each account names its source. The sources differ in some details, and the differences are listed under “What the sources do not settle”.

Ḥakím Masíḥ: the first Jewish believer in Persia

Taherzadeh writes that “The first of the Jewish community to recognize the truth of the Mission of Bahá’u’lláh in Persia was a notable physician named Masih”, a court physician to Muḥammad Shah who accompanied the Shah on a journey to ’Iraq (Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 3). In Baghdád he learned that Ṭáhirih was staying in the home of an early believer and holding discussions with the divines. He went to listen. Taherzadeh writes that he was “captivated by her powerful arguments and sheer personality”. Taherzadeh quotes a memoir by Shaykh Kázim-i-Samandar, who asked him how he had come from the Faith of Moses to this Cause. Masíḥ answered that he had been “astonished and awestruck” by her way of speaking. The historian Mehrdad Amanat, in the Encyclopaedia Iranica entry “Judeo-Persian Communities of Iran in the Qajar Period”, also gives Ṭáhirih’s debates in Baghdad as the beginning and adds that in 1861 Masíḥ “openly confessed his Babi beliefs” after attending sick Bábí prisoners in Tehran.

Taherzadeh tells the second meeting. The Bábí Ismu’llahu’l-Aṣdaq and his young son Ibn-i-Aṣdaq were chained in the Síyáh-Chál in Tehran, and the child became seriously ill. No physician would treat a Bábí, and the jailer called on Ḥakím Masíḥ, who went at once and attended the boy for two months. Afterwards he stayed in the prison “sitting at the Ismu’lláh’s feet and learning about the Faith”. Taherzadeh then writes that “Soon after, he became a believer fully aware that the Promised One of the Old Testament, the ‘Everlasting Father’, the Lord of Hosts, had manifested Himself.” (Taherzadeh, vol. 3). The wording is Taherzadeh’s, in his account of the conversion. Bahá’u’lláh revealed a Tablet in his honour; most of the others were destroyed by damp after being hidden underground. Taherzadeh also records that Masíḥ taught his family, and that a descendant, his youngest grandson Dr Luṭfu’lláh Ḥakím, “was elected to the Universal House of Justice in 1963”, the year the first Universal House of Justice was elected (Taherzadeh, vol. 3).

Hamadán, from 1877

In God Passes By Shoghi Effendi records “the conversion of a considerable number of the adherents of the Jewish, Zoroastrian and Buddhist Faiths” among the first fruits of the teaching work (GPB XI.30), and refers to “the Jews and the Parsis who had been newly converted to the Faith, and were living, the former in Hamadán, and the latter in Yazd” when they were attacked (GPB XII.11).

The scholar Maneck, in “Conversion of Religious Minorities to the Bahá’í Faith in Iran”, tells how it began. In 1877 a Jewish physician, Ḥakím Áqá Ján, treated the wife of Muḥammad-Báqir, a Bahá’í of Hamadán, and by mistake gave her strychnine pills instead of quinine. She nearly died. Muḥammad-Báqir told him he would not hold him responsible, and Áqá Ján was so impressed by the kindness that he asked what religion Muḥammad-Báqir followed. Maneck writes that Áqá Ján “made a thorough investigation of the tenets of the Bahá’í religion and eventually embraced it along with some forty friends and family members, including his father, a leading rabbi of the town.” Maneck adds that the movement “began in Hamadan around 1877”, and that by 1884, according to the historian of Persian Jewry Ḥabíb Levy, it involved some one hundred and fifty of the eight hundred Jewish households there.

Amanat gives a variant of the same start. In 1877 Ḥakím Áqá Ján was invited to treat a patient in a Bábí household of Hamadán. He was impressed by the family’s “respect toward himself, their disregard for laws of impurity, their willingness to share food with him”. The message then went through his family connections to the physician Ḥakím Raḥamím, later a leader of the community, and “Within two years (1877-79)... some 50 Jews joined the nascent community of Hamadan”. Amanat adds that in 1847 Ṭáhirih had stayed briefly in Hamadán at the house of a Jewish physician, Elezar, until fears of an anti-Jewish riot ended her stay.

Ḥájí Yahúdá of Rasht and the Tablets to Áqá Yahúdá

Taherzadeh tells of a Jewish believer known as Ḥájí Yahúdá, the son of the chief rabbi of Rasht, who as a young man sold goods from town to town. On a trip to Hamadán he met some Bahá’ís and became one. About 1888–89 he went to ‘Akká and was brought to Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Riḍván. The sight of water flowing from a fountain by Bahá’u’lláh’s feet “vividly brought to his mind the vision of the Prophets of Israel as recorded in the Old Testament.” Taherzadeh quotes two passages. The first is: “There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the city of God, the holy place of the tabernacles of the most High. God is in the midst of her; she shall not be moved: God shall help her, and that right early.” (Psalm 46:4–5, King James Version, as quoted by Taherzadeh; saved in `kjv-psalms.md`). He saw himself “standing with awe and wonder in the presence of the Lord of the Old Testament” and prostrated himself. When he returned to Persia he taught among his own people, brought a number of Jews into the Faith, and suffered persecution from the Jewish community (Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 4).

A document prepared by Adib Masumian gives three Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh to an Áqá Yahúdá, “an early Jewish convert to the Bahá’í Faith from Hamadán”, in a provisional translation by Khazeh Fananapazir. The document does not identify him with Ḥájí Yahúdá of Rasht and this page does not either. The Tablets are provisional and are given here with that label. In Tablet 1, addressed to the “Concourse of the Torah”, Bahá’u’lláh says “the ten words (ten commandments, the Decalogue) have been revealed again in the one commandment”, and: “In truth, Mount Zion is calling aloud, giving glad-tidings unto all things” (provisional translation by Khazeh Fananapazir). In Tablet 3: “O Yahúdá! Verily Mount Sinai is today narrating the glad-tidings, but the people hearken not.” and “Say: O Concourse of Humanity! Verily, the day of Promise hath come, and the Promised One is here.” (provisional translation by Khazeh Fananapazir; `fananapazir_bahaullah_tablets_aqa-yahuda_fulltext.md`). Permission from the translator is needed to use these words in lyrics.

Mírzá ʻAzízu’lláh and the letter to Rothschild

Taherzadeh tells of Mírzá ʻAzízu’lláh-i-Jadhdháb, a believer of Jewish descent from Mashhad. His father, who knew the Old Testament well and taught it to Jewish youth, had secretly accepted Islam. Taherzadeh writes that he “advised his family to watch for the coming of the Lord of Hosts, as he had discovered according to the Holy Books that His advent was at hand.” ʻAzízu’lláh became a Bahá’í in 1876 and went to ‘Akká. In 1891, on his third visit, Bahá’u’lláh told him that He had proclaimed His mission to the rulers of the world and wished to convey the message of God to Baron Rothschild, “who could be considered as the leader of the Jewish people at the time”, and “directed him to write to Baron Rothschild and proclaim the Faith to him and if possible pay him a visit.” ʻAzízu’lláh’s own account, as Taherzadeh quotes it, is that he wrote to Rothschild of the coming of Bahá’u’lláh and “the fulfilment of the prophecies of the Old Testament concerning the advent of the Lord of Hosts who had revealed Himself on Mount Carmel at this time.” In September 1902 he met Leo Tolstoy and spoke to him of the station of Bahá’u’lláh (Taherzadeh, vol. 3). Amanat adds that he was born Esḥāq (1873–1950) in a family poor and low in standing, and that Amanat reads his acceptance as that of “the optimistic Bahá’í message that prophesized a future of advancement and progress for the Jews.”

Káshán

Momen, in his encyclopedia entry “Iran: Province of Káshán and Central Provinces”, writes that a large number of the Jews of Káshán became Bahá’ís, among them the Mítháqiyyih, Rayḥání, Yúsifiyán and Mutaḥḥidih families. Momen states that Áqá Muḥammad-Javád and Áqá Muḥammad-Báqir moved from Káshán to Hamadán, founded the Bahá’í community there, and began the conversion of Jews in Hamadán. Amanat says the message reached Káshán in the 1880s and “attracted a number of mostly disenfranchised young Jews”, among them Rayḥān Rayḥání, a self-educated orphan, and Ḥájjí Elyāhu Kāshāni, said to have been the city’s “most skillful and accomplished professional thief” before his conversion; Elyāhu became a teacher of the Faith and was attacked by a gang after Tehran’s Jewish leaders declared him ritually impure. Shoghi Effendi records that in Káshán “the adherents of the Faith of Jewish extraction were fined, beaten and chained at the instigation of both the Muḥammadan clergy and the Jewish doctors.” (GPB XX.6)

Teachers and writers

The treatise for Ḥakím Mírzá Ayyúb. Momen, in his entry on Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání, writes that “a great many Jews inquired about the Bahá’í Faith” while Abu’l-Faḍl was in Hamadán. A Bahá’í of Jewish background, Ḥakím Mírzá Ayyúb, wrote to him from Tehran with questions on the Torah and on the prophecies of the Promised One. The reply, the Risálih Ayyúbiyyih, was sent in 1305 A.H. (1887). This is scholarship on a work by a Bahá’í scholar. It is not a text of the Writings.

Ḥájí Mihdí Arjmand. Afroukhteh’s glossary lists Ḥájí Mihdí Arjmand (1861–1941), a teacher of the Faith in Hamadán of Jewish background, whose Gulshan-i-Ḥaqá’iq treats the relation of the Bahá’í Faith to Judaism and Christianity.

The Israelite assembly of Tehran. Amanat records that in Tehran Jewish Bahá’ís had separate Spiritual Assemblies for some years, until ‘Abdu’l-Bahá ended the practice, and that in 1903 the Spiritual Assembly of Israelites of Tehran wrote to the “Christian Bahá’ís of Chicago”, quoting biblical passages as proof of their new conviction. The letter was published in Star of the West (the Persian Najm-i-Bākhtar).

Mírzá Yaʻqúb-i-Muttaḥidih. Shoghi Effendi writes: “In Kirmánsháh, the martyr Mírzá Yá‘qub-i-Muttaḥidih, the ardent twenty-five year old Jewish convert to the Faith, was the last to lay down his life during ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s ministry; and his mother, according to his own instructions, celebrated his martyrdom in Hamadán with exemplary fortitude.” (GPB XX.10)

Why they came: three accounts

The reasons are given differently by different authors. These are their explanations, not statements in the Writings.

  • Taherzadeh writes that the Persian Jews and Zoroastrians who entered the Faith in the nineteenth century were making “a genuine act of faith”, not an expedient, and that their entry is an argument for the universality of Bahá’u’lláh’s claim to be “the Promised One of all ages”.
  • Amanat describes people who were poor and “ritually impure” in law, who found in the Bahá’í community equality and respect, “a way out of the ghetto mindset”, and for whom a promise of advancement mattered.
  • Sharon writes that Persian Jews cherished the hope of a new Cyrus and that Bahá’u’lláh spoke from the Holy Land of the return to Zion.

The three accounts do not exclude each other, and each rests on a different kind of evidence. Where a convert’s own words are preserved, they cite the Bible: the Lord of Hosts, Psalm 46, the glory on Carmel.

The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

Description from Wikipedia (“Cantillation”, “Hebrew cantillation”, “Piyyut”; saved under [source] and `music-piyyut.md`; CC BY-SA 4.0; description only). The saved extracts do not describe instruments, so none are named here.

Cantillation. Scripture is chanted from the Hebrew Bible by marks printed in the text. The article states that this chant “is technically regarded as a ritualized form of speech intonation rather than as a musical exercise like the singing of metrical hymns”, and that Jews therefore speak of “saying” or “reading” a passage, not “singing” it. Each cantillation mark has a musical phrase, and different books and occasions use different sets of phrases. The tunes vary between communities; the article names Ashkenazi, Sephardic and Eastern, and Yemenite traditions. For a songwriter, this means a line quoted from the prophets can follow the contour of speech, with the stress of the words setting the melody.

Piyyut. A piyyuṭ is a Jewish liturgical poem, “usually designated to be sung, chanted, or recited during religious services”. Many follow a poetic scheme such as an acrostic on the Hebrew alphabet or on the author’s name. The article says that, in the early period, “many piyyuṭim had repeating stanzas that the congregation would respond to, followed by the hazzan’s recitations.” Its best-known example is Adon Olam, in a repeated rhythmic pattern of short-long-long-long. This suggests two possible idioms: a spoken-contour chant for scripture lines, and a repeating stanza with a response for a chorus.

What the sources do not settle

  • Which verses are connected to this Day, and how closely. The Writings and Shoghi Effendi quote or comment on Isaiah 2, 9:6, 11, 35:2 and 40:9, Amos 1:2, Hosea 2:15, Ezekiel 43:2, Psalm 24:10, Deuteronomy 33:2 and Daniel. Shoghi Effendi’s paragraph in GPB VI.13 also applies the words of Haggai, Zechariah, Joel, Zephaniah and Malachi to Bahá’u’lláh and His Day. He quotes phrases and does not comment verse by verse; the sources gathered give no reading of Joel 2:28–31 or Zechariah 14:4 by the Writings. Readings of Micah 7:12, Isaiah 60:1, Psalm 48 and Zechariah 14:8 come from commentators only.
  • How the 1,335 days are counted. The research for this page settles more than the earlier version of it did. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives the 1,335 in Tablets published in The Passing of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi’s letters give 1957, 1960 and 1963. The Research Department states that it “has not been able to locate any additional authoritative texts which contain detailed instructions concerning how to make the calculation” and suggests the fulfilment is “a process that extends over a period of time”, 1953–1963. The exact method of calculation is not settled, and the page does not offer one.
  • The 1963 message. The House of Justice of 30 April 1963 calls the Jubilee the fulfilment of “Daniel’s prophecy” but does not say which verse or how it relates to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s 1844, 1260 and 1290. The Research Department memorandum links the message to Daniel 12:12 through Shoghi Effendi’s letters, but the message itself does not.
  • The start of the 2,300 days. Some Answered Questions gives 457 B.C. for the edict of Artaxerxes (SAQ 10.12) and 456 years from the edict to the birth of Christ (SAQ 10.16). Munje and the Africa compilation give 456 B.C.; Riggs counts from Ezra’s journey; Sharon gives 465 B.C. for the start of Artaxerxes’ reign and 445 B.C. for Nehemiah’s permission. The sources do not reconcile these figures.
  • ’Everlasting Father’ in Bahá’u’lláh’s text. An earlier version of this page did not find the phrase in Bahá’u’lláh’s own words. It is in the address to the bishops (PDC 253), where Shoghi Effendi quotes it. The link to Isaiah 9:6 is made by Shoghi Effendi (GPB VI.11, VI.13).
  • Jewish readings. The Bahá’í texts here are the Bahá’í understanding. Nothing in the sources shows that Jewish scholars or institutions accept it, and the page does not claim they do. Sharon’s criticism of the day-for-a-year rule is an academic view that the Writings do not answer.
  • The Hamadán beginning and the numbers. Maneck gives “some forty” friends and family with Áqá Ján and about one hundred and fifty households by 1884 (citing Levy); Amanat gives about fifty Jews in 1877–79. Maneck’s story names a mistake with strychnine for quinine and a Bahá’í, Muḥammad-Báqir; Amanat’s names a Bábí household and an unspecified erroneous medication. These are scholars’ figures and accounts, not a count from the Writings.

Concrete images.

  • A high mountain and a herald with a raised voice (Isaiah 40:9).
  • Carmel above the sea of Haifa, and the walled city of ‘Akká, a prison (ESW 208, 210).
  • The wolf and the lamb; a little child leading (Isaiah 11:6).
  • Sinai, with the fiery law at His right hand (Deuteronomy 33:2), and Sinai “circling round the Dayspring” (KA 80).
  • Water at the feet of Bahá’u’lláh in the Garden of Riḍván, and a man who sees in it the river of Psalm 46 (Taherzadeh, vol. 4).
  • A physician’s mistaken pills and a patient’s forgiveness in Hamadán in 1877 (Maneck).
  • A physician called to a child in the Síyáh-Chál who stays to learn at the prisoner’s feet (Taherzadeh, vol. 3).
  • A rabbi in Washington on 9 November 1912: “Will you not tell the Christians to love us a little more?” (PUP 122.12).
  • A mother in Hamadán celebrating her son’s martyrdom (GPB XX.10).

Possible emotional arcs.

  1. Waiting. The old titles said slowly: Wonderful, Counsellor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Then the herald: lift up your voice, be not afraid. Then the place: the prison city that is also a door of hope. Then people: a physician and a rabbi’s family in Hamadán, a man at a fountain in Riḍván. End on the mountain of the house of the LORD and the nations that flow to it, in the words of the prophets.
  2. A conversation. Begin with the rabbi’s request in Washington, 1912, and let each verse answer a line of it: the wolf and the lamb, swords into plowshares, the knowledge of the LORD. End with the request unanswered or with the reply, “We have advised them and will continue to do so.”

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not say that Jewish people rejected or failed to recognise a Messenger, or say that Jewish readings are mistaken. The sources do not support a judgement in the page’s voice.
  • It must not state that the Hebrew prophets named Bahá’u’lláh. The texts say that Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá apply these verses to Him; a lyric should attribute them (“Bahá’u’lláh writes…”) or leave it as the Bahá’í understanding.
  • It must not give 1844, 1260, 1290, 1335 or 1963 as Daniel’s own numbers. They are ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reckoning and the Guardian’s and the House of Justice’s account of it. The 1,335 days are not fulfilled by one date according to the Research Department’s suggestion.
  • It must not say that universal peace was promised for 1957 or 1963. A note in the 1946 edition of Esslemont’s book says the Guardian wrote that the Bahá’í teachings contain nothing to indicate a definite degree of world peace by 1957 or 1963.
  • It must not attach a reading to a verse beyond what the texts state: for Haggai, Zechariah, Joel, Zephaniah and Malachi, Shoghi Effendi’s phrases in GPB VI.13 are the authoritative link. Micah 7:12, Isaiah 60:1 and Psalm 48 are commentators’ readings only.
  • It must not say that Jewish institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.
  • It must not use the provisional translations of the Tablets to Áqá Yahúdá without the translator’s permission.
