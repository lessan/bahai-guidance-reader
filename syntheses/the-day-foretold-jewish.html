The Day Foretold: The Lord of Hosts and the Everlasting Father

Last updated 16 October 2026

What this page covers

This page belongs to the series the-day-foretold, which takes one religious tradition at a time. It sets out what the Hebrew Bible says about a coming Day of the Lord, what the Bahá’í Writings say about how that Day is fulfilled, and some Jewish people who became Bahá’ís in the nineteenth century. It also describes Jewish sacred music, for a songwriter who wants to choose a musical idiom with care.

Shoghi Effendi gives the title for this tradition in God Passes By:

“To Israel He was neither more nor less than the incarnation of the “Everlasting Father”; the “Lord of Hosts” come down “with ten thousands of saints”” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

Three kinds of source are kept apart below. The promise is the Hebrew Bible, quoted from the King James Version (1611). The fulfilment is the Bahá’í Writings and the writings of Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice. The history of Jewish believers is told by historians and scholars, named where they appear. The page states the Bahá’í understanding as the Bahá’í understanding. It does not judge how Jewish readers read their own scripture.

The promise: the Hebrew Bible

All passages in this section are from the King James Version (1611), saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/jewish/`. The verses are ones that the Bahá’í texts below connect to this Day. Where Shoghi Effendi or Bahá’u’lláh renders a verse in slightly different words, that rendering is given in the next section.

The child and the titles. “For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.” (Isaiah 9:6, King James Version)

The rod from the stem of Jesse. “And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots:” (Isaiah 11:1). “The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid; and the calf and the young lion and the fatling together; and a little child shall lead them.” (Isaiah 11:6). “They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain: for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.” (Isaiah 11:9). “And he shall set up an ensign for the nations, and shall assemble the outcasts of Israel, and gather together the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth.” (Isaiah 11:12, King James Version)

The mountain of the house of the LORD. “And it shall come to pass in the last days, that the mountain of the LORD’s house shall be established in the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall flow unto it.” (Isaiah 2:2). “nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more.” (Isaiah 2:4). (King James Version)

The herald on the mountain. “O Zion, that bringest good tidings, get thee up into the high mountain; O Jerusalem, that bringest good tidings, lift up thy voice with strength; lift it up, be not afraid; say unto the cities of Judah, Behold your God!” (Isaiah 40:9). “And the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it.” (Isaiah 40:5, King James Version)

Carmel and Zion. “And he said, The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem; and the habitations of the shepherds shall mourn, and the top of Carmel shall wither.” (Amos 1:2, King James Version)

The King of glory, the glory from the east, and Sinai. “Who is this King of glory? The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory.” (Psalm 24:10). “And, behold, the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east: and his voice was like a noise of many waters: and the earth shined with his glory.” (Ezekiel 43:2). “And he said, The LORD came from Sinai, and rose up from Seir unto them; he shined forth from mount Paran, and he came with ten thousands of saints: from his right hand went a fiery law for them.” (Deuteronomy 33:2, King James Version)

The door of hope. “And I will give her her vineyards from thence, and the valley of Achor for a door of hope: and she shall sing there, as in the days of her youth, and as in the day when she came up out of the land of Egypt.” (Hosea 2:15, King James Version)

The time of the end. “And he said unto me, Unto two thousand and three hundred days; then shall the sanctuary be cleansed.” (Daniel 8:14). “And from the time that the daily sacrifice shall be taken away, and the abomination that maketh desolate set up, there shall be a thousand two hundred and ninety days.” (Daniel 12:11). “Blessed is he that waiteth, and cometh to the thousand three hundred and five and thirty days.” (Daniel 12:12, King James Version)

The desire of all nations and the Sun of righteousness. “And I will shake all nations, and the desire of all nations shall come: and I will fill this house with glory, saith the LORD of hosts.” (Haggai 2:7). “But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings; and ye shall go forth, and grow up as calves of the stall.” (Malachi 4:2, King James Version)

The Day of the LORD. “And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one.” (Zechariah 14:9, King James Version)

The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The titles of the Day

Shoghi Effendi lists what Isaiah and David said of the One to come:

“To Him Isaiah, the greatest of the Jewish prophets, had alluded as the “Glory of the Lord,” the “Everlasting Father,” the “Prince of Peace,” the “Wonderful,” the “Counsellor,” the “Rod come forth out of the stem of Jesse” and the “Branch grown out of His roots,” Who “shall be established upon the throne of David,”” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.13)

and adds that David had sung of Him “in his Psalms, acclaiming Him as the “Lord of Hosts” and the “King of Glory.”” (GPB VI.13). The same paragraph goes on to the other prophets:

“To Him Haggai had referred as the “Desire of all nations,” and Zachariah as the “Branch” Who “shall grow up out of His place,” and “shall build the Temple of the Lord.” Zachariah had extolled Him as the “Lord” Who “shall be king over all the earth,” while to His day Joel and Zephaniah had both referred as the “day of Jehovah,” the latter describing it as “a day of wrath, a day of trouble and distress, a day of wasteness and desolation, a day of darkness and gloominess, a day of clouds and thick darkness, a day of the trumpet and alarm against the fenced cities, and against the high towers.” His Day Ezekiel and Daniel had, moreover, both acclaimed as the “day of the Lord,” and Malachi described as “the great and dreadful day of the Lord” when “the Sun of Righteousness” will “arise, with healing in His wings,” whilst Daniel had pronounced His advent as signalizing the end of the “abomination that maketh desolate.”” (GPB VI.13)

Writing of Baghdad, Shoghi Effendi says that “the Tabernacle of the promised “Lord of Hosts” was first erected” there (GPB VII.14).

Bahá’u’lláh states the identification in His own voice. “I am the One Whom the tongue of Isaiah hath extolled, the One with Whose name both the Torah and the Evangel were adorned.” (Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 77; also WOB 6.20). In a Tablet excerpt He writes: “The Promised Day is come and the Lord of Hosts hath appeared.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, TB 17.36). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says of the same title: “…blessed Person of the Promised One is interpreted in the Holy Book as the Lord of Hosts—the heavenly armies.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablets of the Divine Plan, TDP 8.1)

Words to the Jewish people

Shoghi Effendi introduces a passage as addressed to the Jewish people:

“The Most Great Law is come, and the Ancient Beauty ruleth upon the throne of David.” (Bahá’u’lláh, quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 191)

The same passage continues: “The Breath hath been wafted, and the Breeze hath blown, and from Zion hath appeared that which was hidden, and from Jerusalem is heard the Voice of God, the One, the Incomparable, the Omniscient.” (PDC 191). Bahá’u’lláh writes in the Gleanings: “Out of Zion hath gone forth the Law of God, and Jerusalem, and the hills and land thereof, are filled with the glory of His Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, GWB X.1).

Isaiah 40:9, Amos 1:2 and Isaiah 35:2: Zion, Carmel and the Holy Land

In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf Bahá’u’lláh quotes Isaiah 40:9 and then says: “This Day all the signs have appeared. A Great City hath descended from heaven, and Zion trembleth and exulteth with joy at the Revelation of God” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 209). He quotes Amos and comments: “Carmel, in the Book of God, hath been designated as the Hill of God, and His Vineyard. It is here that, by the grace of the Lord of Revelation, the Tabernacle of Glory hath been raised.” (ESW 210). He quotes David’s “Who will bring me into the Strong City?” and says: “The Strong City is ‘Akká, which hath been named the Most Great Prison” (ESW 208).

In the Tablet of Carmel He addresses the mountain: “Rejoice, for God hath in this Day established upon thee His throne, hath made thee the dawning-place of His signs and the dayspring of the evidences of His Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Tablet of Carmel, TB 1.3). And: “Call out to Zion, O Carmel, and announce the joyful tidings: He that was hidden from mortal eyes is come!” (TB 1.4)

On the Holy Land itself He writes: “Whatever hath come to pass in this Day hath been foretold in the Scriptures of old.” (GWB CLXIV.7). Shoghi Effendi gathers the prophecies about the Holy Land: Isaiah, David and Amos on the Lord of Hosts appearing there (GPB XI.2), and ‘Akká as “the Strong City” of David, “a door of hope” in Hosea, and “the gate that looketh towards the East” in Ezekiel, to which “the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the East” (GPB XI.3). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in a Tablet to a woman believer, writes of Hosea’s verse: “This valley of Achor is the city of ‘Akká, and whoso hath interpreted this otherwise is of those who know not.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, SWAB 139.9)

Isaiah 11: the rod of Jesse

In Some Answered Questions ‘Abdu’l-Bahá comments on Isaiah 11:1–9. He states: “But these verses apply word for word to Bahá’u’lláh.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 12.4). On Israel he writes: “Likewise Israel, which had been scattered throughout the world, was not gathered together in the Holy Land in the course of the Christian Dispensation, but in the beginning of the Dispensation of Bahá’u’lláh this divine promise, which has been clearly stated in all the Books of the Prophets, has begun to materialize.” (SAQ 12.8)

Daniel: the dates

‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives the dates in Some Answered Questions, chapter 10, on the principle that in the Torah’s prophetic reckoning a day stands for a year. For Daniel 8:13–14 (the 2,300 days) he counts 456 years from the edict of Artaxerxes to rebuild Jerusalem to the birth of Christ, and 1,844 years from the birth of Christ to the advent of the Báb, and concludes: “That is to say, the fulfilment of the vision of Daniel took place in A.D. 1844, and this is the year of the advent of the Báb.” (SAQ 10.16)

For the “time, times, and an half” of Daniel 12:7, he states that three and a half years make forty-two months, and forty-two months make 1,260 days, each day being a year: “And it is in the very year 1260 from the emigration of Muḥammad, according to the Muslim calendar, that the Báb, the Herald of Bahá’u’lláh, revealed His mission.” (SAQ 10.20). After quoting Daniel 12:11–12, he dates Bahá’u’lláh’s announcement: “And it was in the year 1290 from the proclamation of the mission of Muḥammad that Bahá’u’lláh announced His Revelation.” (SAQ 10.22). In the text of chapter 10 he gives no explanation of the 1,335 days of Daniel 12:12.

On 30 April 1963 the Universal House of Justice sent a message to the First Bahá’í World Congress, held at the Most Great Jubilee, the centenary of the Declaration of Bahá’u’lláh. It says: “This historic moment marks at one and the same time the fulfillment of Daniel’s prophecy, the Hundredth Anniversary of the Declaration of the Promised One of all ages, the termination of the first epoch of the Divine Plan of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá” and the conclusion of Shoghi Effendi’s world-encircling Crusade (Universal House of Justice, 30 April 1963). The message names Daniel’s prophecy and the moment of the Jubilee. It does not name a chapter or verse, and it gives no calculation.

Deuteronomy 33:2 and “ten thousands of saints”

Shoghi Effendi uses the phrase from Deuteronomy 33:2 in the sentence quoted at the top of this page: the “Lord of Hosts” come down “with ten thousands of saints”” (GPB VI.11). Bahá’u’lláh writes: “The episode of Sinai hath been reenacted in this Revelation” (TB 17.60). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is quoted in the World Order of Bahá’u’lláh: “The Blessed Beauty is the One promised by the sacred books of the past, the revelation of the Source of light that shone upon Mount Sinai” (WOB 6.66).

Moses and the Prophets of Israel

The Writings honour Moses and the Prophets of Israel. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes what Moses did for the Israelites: “That people had sunk to the depths of degradation and were lifted up to the heights of glory.” (SAQ 5.2). Shoghi Effendi calls Isaiah “the greatest of the Jewish prophets” (GPB VI.13). Bahá’u’lláh writes that “the promises of God, as recorded in the holy Scriptures, have all been fulfilled.” (GWB X.1)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá on why many did not recognise Christ

‘Abdu’l-Bahá spoke at length about the messianic signs and the time of Christ. These are His statements, quoted, and the page does not apply them to any present reading. In a Tablet He writes that the signs of the Messiah recorded in the Torah “did come to pass in the Manifestation of Christ, although not in the sense which the Jews understood, the description in the Torah being allegorical.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, SWAB 20.2). In a talk in Brooklyn on 16 June 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “All the words of the prophets were fulfilled, but because the Jews held tenaciously to hereditary interpretations, they did not understand the inner meanings of the Holy Bible; therefore, they denied Jesus Christ, the Messiah.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 71.11).

Those who recognised it

This section rests on history by Shoghi Effendi and on scholarship.

Hamadán, from 1877. In God Passes By Shoghi Effendi records “the conversion of a considerable number of the adherents of the Jewish, Zoroastrian and Buddhist Faiths” among the first fruits of the teaching work (GPB XI.30), and refers to “the Jews and the Parsis who had been newly converted to the Faith, and were living, the former in Hamadán, and the latter in Yazd” when they were attacked (GPB XII.11).

The scholar Maneck, in “Conversion of Religious Minorities to the Bahá’í Faith in Iran”, tells how it began. In 1877 a Jewish physician, Ḥakím Áqá Ján, treated the wife of Muḥammad-Báqir, a Bahá’í of Hamadán, and by mistake gave her strychnine pills instead of quinine. She nearly died. Muḥammad-Báqir told him he would not hold him responsible, and Áqá Ján was so impressed by the kindness that he asked what religion Muḥammad-Báqir followed. Maneck writes that Áqá Ján “made a thorough investigation of the tenets of the Bahá’í religion and eventually embraced it along with some forty friends and family members, including his father, a leading rabbi of the town.” Maneck adds that the movement “began in Hamadan around 1877”, and that by 1884, according to the historian of Persian Jewry Ḥabíb Levy, it involved some one hundred and fifty of the eight hundred Jewish households there.

Káshán. Momen, in his encyclopedia entry “Iran: Province of Káshán and Central Provinces”, writes that a large number of the Jews of Káshán became Bahá’ís, among them the Mítháqiyyih, Rayḥání, Yúsifiyán and Mutaḥḥidih families. He states that Áqá Muḥammad-Javád and Áqá Muḥammad-Báqir moved from Káshán to Hamadán, founded the Bahá’í community there, and began the conversion of Jews in Hamadán. Shoghi Effendi records that in Káshán “the adherents of the Faith of Jewish extraction were fined, beaten and chained at the instigation of both the Muḥammadan clergy and the Jewish doctors.” (GPB XX.6)

The treatise for Ḥakím Mírzá Ayyúb. Momen, in his entry on Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání, writes that “a great many Jews inquired about the Bahá’í Faith” while Abu’l-Faḍl was in Hamadán. A Bahá’í of Jewish background, Ḥakím Mírzá Ayyúb, wrote to him from Tehran with questions on the Torah and on the prophecies of the Promised One. The reply, the Risálih Ayyúbiyyih, was sent in 1305 A.H. (1887). This is scholarship on a work by a Bahá’í scholar. It is not a text of the Writings.

Mírzá Yaʻqúb-i-Muttaḥidih. Shoghi Effendi writes: “In Kirmánsháh, the martyr Mírzá Yá‘qub-i-Muttaḥidih, the ardent twenty-five year old Jewish convert to the Faith, was the last to lay down his life during ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s ministry; and his mother, according to his own instructions, celebrated his martyrdom in Hamadán with exemplary fortitude.” (GPB XX.10)

The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

Description from Wikipedia (“Cantillation”, “Hebrew cantillation”, “Piyyut”; saved under [source] and `music-piyyut.md`; CC BY-SA 4.0; description only). The saved extracts do not describe instruments, so none are named here.

Cantillation. Scripture is chanted from the Hebrew Bible by marks printed in the text. The article states that this chant “is technically regarded as a ritualized form of speech intonation rather than as a musical exercise like the singing of metrical hymns”, and that Jews therefore speak of “saying” or “reading” a passage, not “singing” it. Each cantillation mark has a musical phrase, and different books and occasions use different sets of phrases. The tunes vary between communities; the article names Ashkenazi, Sephardic and Eastern, and Yemenite traditions. For a songwriter, this means a line quoted from the prophets can follow the contour of speech, with the stress of the words setting the melody.

Piyyut. A piyyuṭ is a Jewish liturgical poem, “usually designated to be sung, chanted, or recited during religious services”. Many follow a poetic scheme such as an acrostic on the Hebrew alphabet or on the author’s name. The article says that in the piyyuṭ the cantor “is assisted by the congregation in chanting melodies”, and that many piyyuṭim have repeating stanzas to which the congregation responds. Its best-known example is Adon Olam, in a repeated rhythmic pattern of short-long-long-long. This suggests two possible idioms: a spoken-contour chant for scripture lines, and a repeating stanza with a response for a chorus.

What the sources do not settle

  • Which verses are connected to this Day, and how closely. The Writings and Shoghi Effendi quote or comment on Isaiah 9:6, Isaiah 11, Isaiah 35:2, Isaiah 40:9, Amos 1:2, Hosea 2:15, Ezekiel 43:2, Psalm 24:10, Deuteronomy 33:2 and Daniel. Shoghi Effendi’s paragraph in GPB VI.13 also applies the words of Haggai (“Desire of all nations”), Zechariah (the “Branch”; the “Lord” Who “shall be king over all the earth”), Joel and Zephaniah (the “day of Jehovah”) and Malachi (“the Sun of Righteousness”) to Bahá’u’lláh and His Day. He quotes phrases; he does not comment verse by verse, and the sources gathered do not give a verse-by-verse reading of Joel 2:28–31 or Zechariah 14:4.
  • The 1,335 days of Daniel 12:12. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes the verse in Some Answered Questions but the text of chapter 10 gives no date for it.
  • The 1963 message. It calls the Jubilee the fulfilment of “Daniel’s prophecy” but does not say which verse, or how it relates to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s 1844, 1260 and 1290.
  • Jewish readings. The Bahá’í texts here are the Bahá’í understanding. Nothing in the sources shows that Jewish scholars or institutions accept it, and the page does not claim they do.
  • Numbers of early Jewish believers. Maneck gives “some forty” friends and family with Áqá Ján and about one hundred and fifty households by 1884 (citing Levy). These are a scholar’s figures, not a count from the Writings.

Concrete images. A high mountain and a herald with a raised voice. Carmel above the sea of Haifa, and the walled city of ‘Akká, a prison. The wolf and the lamb; a little child leading. Sinai, with the fiery law at His right hand. A physician’s mistaken pills and a patient’s forgiveness in Hamadán in 1877. A mother in Hamadán celebrating her son’s martyrdom.

A possible emotional arc. Waiting, with the old titles said slowly (Wonderful, Counsellor, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace). Then the herald: lift up your voice, be not afraid. Then the place: the prison city that is also a door of hope. Then people: a physician and a rabbi’s family in Hamadán. End on the mountain of the house of the LORD and the nations that flow to it, in the words of the prophets.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not say that Jewish people rejected or failed to recognise a Messenger, or say that Jewish readings are mistaken. The sources do not support a judgement in the page’s voice.
  • It must not state that the Hebrew prophets named Bahá’u’lláh. The texts say that Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá apply these verses to Him; a lyric should attribute them (“Bahá’u’lláh writes…”) or leave it as the Bahá’í understanding.
  • It must not give 1844, 1260 or 1290 as Daniel’s own numbers. They are ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s reckoning.
  • It must not attach a reading to a verse beyond what the texts state: for Haggai, Zechariah, Joel, Zephaniah and Malachi, Shoghi Effendi’s phrases in GPB VI.13 are the authoritative link.
  • It must not say that Jewish institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.
