The Poems of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá

Last updated 17 October 2026

Few of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s writings are in verse. Two Persian poems can be read in English today. The first is a prayer of eight rhymed couplets, “O Lord so rich in bounty, so replete with grace”, which has an authorised English translation in rhyming couplets. The second is a longer love poem, “My rosy-cheeked most glorious One”, which has only a provisional translation. This page describes both, gathers what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi say about poetry and poets, and notes where ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes the Persian poets. It is one page of a series; see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poet

A scholarly paper by Julio Savi and Faezeh Mardani, presented at the Irfan Colloquia in 2016 and published in Lights of Irfán 18, says that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “Who Himself wrote a few poems in Persian and in Turkish” left guidance on poetry. This is scholarship, not authority. The copy in the corpus has only the abstract and introduction, so the page cannot say which poems the authors discuss. The abstract lists what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says poetry should have: inspiration, beauty, eloquence, versified language, novelty, expressivity, depth and loftiness of meaning.

The two poems below are the ones this page found with the original text and an English version available.

The two poems

1. “O Lord so rich in bounty” (Persian: Ay Khudá-yi pur-‘aṭá-yi Dhu’l-Minan)

  • Form. Eight rhyming couplets, sixteen lines. The Persian is saved in [source]. It begins:

> ای خدای پرعطای ذو المنن > واقف جان و دل و اسرار من

  • Where it is published. The Bahá’í Reference Library prints it, in English, in Additional Prayers Revealed by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, among other prayers, followed by the signature “‘Abdu’l-Bahá” (paragraphs 52.1 to 52.8, one per couplet). The saved Persian page comes from the Library’s Persian section for the “other Tablets and talks” of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. The library file does not name a translator.
  • What is not given. The sources do not say when or where ‘Abdu’l-Bahá wrote it, or to whom.

2. “My rosy-cheeked most glorious One” (Persian: Ay gulrukh-i abhá-yi man)

  • Form. Nine stanzas of four lines. In each stanza the first three lines rhyme with one another (in the first stanza, abhá-yi man, a‘lá-yi man, Sínáyi man), and the fourth line ends in the same sound, -úy-i tu (“of Thee”), in every stanza (for example rúy-i tu, kúy-i tu, búy-i tu). The Persian is saved in [source]. The first line is:

> ای گلرخ ابهای من

  • Where it is published. In Persian, in Muntakhabátí az Makátíb-i-Ḥaḍrat-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá, vol. 2, pp. 17–18 (selection no. 27), as cited by the translator Adib Masumian. The saved Persian text is a typescript from Masumian’s page; the page does not say who typed it.
  • Occasion. Not established (see the last section). Masumian writes: “I am not aware of the exact circumstances under which this poem was written”. He then adds that “it seems reasonable to assume that this loss is what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is mourning in the poem, and that He may have composed it shortly after His Father passed away”. That is his inference, given as an assumption, and it is not a statement from the Writings. He also notes that part of the poem is chanted in the film Exemplar.

The text in English

The prayer: authorised translation (rhymed)

Authorised translation, Bahá’í Reference Library; translator not named in the file. Licence: `ok-with-credit` (© Bahá’í International Community; check the terms of use at bahai.org/legal before a commercial release).

O Lord so rich in bounty, so replete with grace, Whose knowledge doth mine inmost heart and soul embrace!

At morn, the solace of my soul is none but Thee; The knower of my loss and woe is none but Thee.

The heart that for a moment hath Thy mention known Will seek no friend save longing pain for Thee alone.

Withered be the heart that sigheth not for Thee, And better blind the eye that crieth not for Thee!

In all mine hours of deepest gloom, O Lord of might, My heart hath Thy remembrance for a shining light.

Do, through Thy favour, breathe Thy spirit into me, That what hath never been may thus forever be.

Consider not our merit and our worth, O Lord of bounty, but the grace Thou pourest forth.

Upon these broken-winged birds whose flight is slow Out of Thy tender mercy newfound wings bestow.

(Additional Prayers Revealed by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá 52.1–52.8)

The authorised English has the same eight couplets as the Persian, one for one, and keeps the rhyme. The Library does not describe the translator’s method.

The love poem: provisional translation by Adib Masumian (2024)

Provisional translation by Adib Masumian (2024), adibmasumian.com. There is no authorised English translation. Licence: `permission-needed`. The page shows no copyright or permission notice; the translator is living, so ask before printing it. Masumian calls it “a provisional translation (in other words, not official or authorized)”. He sets it “to iambic tetrameter and an AABB rhyme scheme”. His version is therefore in rhymed couplets; the Persian is in four-line stanzas, so the form differs from the original.

Extracts, to show how the poem opens and closes:

My rosy-cheeked most glorious One My lofty Lord rivaled by none

Bahá! This dust upon Thy door

The Persian of the last stanza begins ín khák-i dargáhat Bahá, and “Bahá” also ends the next two lines of that stanza. Masumian takes it as a name in the address. The word bahá is also an ordinary Persian word meaning “worth” or “price”. The sources do not say which sense, or whether both, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá intended. Another difference is in the fourth stanza, where the Persian line ends rúḥ-i Masíḥá búy-i tu (on a plain reading, “the spirit of the Messiah is Thy fragrance”); Masumian renders the line “Without Thy breath our lives are naught!”. Both observations are plain readings of the Persian made for this page, and a reader of Persian should check them.

A machine-drafted provisional translation (Gemini 4 Argon, 2026-10-17), not reviewed by a translator, is kept in the library at `kb/library/provisional-translations/abdul-baha-ey-gulrukh-i-abha-yi-man.md`.

Themes and images

The prayer. The poem addresses God as bountiful and as the one who knows the speaker’s heart. It says four things in turn.

  1. Nothing else consoles the speaker: “At morn, the solace of my soul is none but Thee”.
  2. A heart that has once mentioned God wants no other company, and a heart that does not “sigh” for God is called withered: “Withered be the heart that sigheth not for Thee”.
  3. God’s remembrance lights the dark hours: “My heart hath Thy remembrance for a shining light”.
  4. The speaker asks not to be judged by merit but by grace, and asks for new strength: “Upon these broken-winged birds whose flight is slow / Out of Thy tender mercy newfound wings bestow.”

The images are morning, light in darkness, and the broken-winged bird.

The love poem. In Masumian’s translation the speaker addresses God, whom he calls Beloved, as “rosy-cheeked” and “lofty Lord”, as “brightly Burning Bush”, and as the one whose “threshold” is his place of circling. The poem then moves to the grief of hearts withered by separation, the scent of musk and roses that fills the world, the speaker’s tears, sighs and captivity in a lock of the Beloved’s hair, and the last request for a drop from the Beloved’s stream. Nearly all the images belong to the Persian love-lyric tradition (the rosy face, the curling lock, musk, the sigh); the Burning Bush and God’s Mount refer to Sinai. The poem does not say who the Beloved is, apart from the possible address “Bahá” in the last stanza; Masumian’s introduction suggests a loss of Bahá’u’lláh.

What ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi say of poetry and poets

To a poet. In a Tablet, published in Tablets of Abdul-Baha Abbas, vol. I, p. 59, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá writes:

O thou bird of pleasing tones! Thy little book of poems, which were very sweet, was read. It was a source of joy, for it was a spiritual anthem and a melody of the love of God.

(The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith 18.1)

Endeavour your utmost to compose beautiful poems to be chanted with heavenly music; thus may their beauty affect the minds and impress the hearts of those who listen.

(The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith 18.3)

(Quoted from the compilation The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith, which gives Tablets of Abdul-Baha Abbas as its source; licence `ok-with-credit`.)

On how poems affect hearts. In the same Tablet: “When eloquence of expression, beauty of sense and sweetness of composition unite with new melodies the effect is ever great, especially if it be the anthem of the verses of oneness and the songs of praise to the Lord of Glory.” (The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith 18.2)

Shoghi Effendi. A letter written on his behalf on 3 February 1952 says:

Music is one of the arts, and the Prophets of God do not teach the arts; but the tremendous cultural impetus which religion gives to society gradually produces new and wonderful forms of art.

(The Importance of the Arts in Promoting the Faith 41.1)

This letter concerns music. It does not mention poetry.

In the Memorials of the Faithful. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes early believers as poets, and quotes their lines. Of Nabíl-i-Zarandí He writes: “His native genius was pure inspiration, his poetic gift like a crystal stream. In particular his ode ‘Bahá, Bahá!’ was written in sheer ecstasy.” (Memorials of the Faithful 10.11). Of Darvísh Ṣidq-‘Alí: “He had a fine poetic gift and wrote odes to sing the praises of Him Whom the world has wronged and rejected.” (Memorials of the Faithful 11.2). Of Shaykh ‘Alí-Akbar-i-Mázgání, who wished to write verse but lacked meter and rhyme, He quotes the line: “I planned a poem, but my Beloved told me, / ‘Plan only this, that thine eyes should behold Me.’” (Memorials of the Faithful 40.3).

On women poets. In a talk in America He says: “Qurratu’l-‘Ayn, a Bahá’í, was a poetess.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace 32.3)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes the Persian poets

In The Secret of Divine Civilization (translated by Marzieh Gail in consultation with Ali-Kuli Khan) ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes verse in several places. The translator’s endnotes name the poets: Rúmí (the Mathnaví), Sa‘dí (the Gulistán) and Saná’í. Two examples, both in Gail’s English:

Thou, Brother, art thy thought alone, The rest is only thew and bone.

(The Secret of Divine Civilization 195; the endnote cites Rúmí, Mathnaví II)

Desire and self come in the door And blot out virtue, bright before,

(The Secret of Divine Civilization 119; the endnote cites Rúmí, Mathnaví I)

In a Tablet about Caucasia, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says of the Báb’s exile to Chihríq that “a savour thereof reached the nostrils of Ḥáfiẓ of Shíráz, who recited this couplet”:

O zephyr, shouldst thou pass by the banks of the Araxes, Implant a kiss on the earth of that valley and make fragrant thy breath.

(Tablets of Abdul-Baha Abbas 178.3. A note in the volume says the quotation alludes to a famous ode of Ḥáfiẓ.) A search of Some Answered Questions, Paris Talks and The Promulgation of Universal Peace found no named quotation from Rúmí, Ḥáfiẓ or Sa‘dí in them; there may be unnamed ones.

What is not settled

  • Date and occasion of both poems. No source in the corpus gives them. For the rosy-cheeked poem, the idea that it mourns the passing of Bahá’u’lláh in 1892 is the translator’s assumption.
  • Authorship of the rosy-cheeked poem. The Persian is attributed to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in a printed selection (Muntakhabátí az Makátíb, vol. 2, no. 27); this page has not seen the original manuscript or the printed page, only a typescript on a translator’s site.
  • The translator of the authorised prayer. The Library file does not name one.
  • The reading of “Bahá” in the last stanza (name or ordinary word), and of other lines noted above.
  • How many poems He wrote. The Savi and Mardani abstract says “a few”, in Persian and Turkish. This page found only the two.
  • Turkish poems. None was found in the corpus.

The couplets already rhyme in English, so they can be set as they stand, line by line.

Exact phrases from the provisional translation (licence `permission-needed`; ask Adib Masumian first, and label any use “provisional translation”): “My lofty Lord rivaled by none”; “O brightly Burning Bush of mine”.

Phrases from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s quotations of other poets (Marzieh Gail’s English; check the terms before a commercial release): “Thou, Brother, art thy thought alone”.

Concrete images. Morning; a light in the dark hours; a heart withered because it does not sigh for God; a broken-winged bird given new wings; (love poem) a threshold circled, a Burning Bush, musk and rose-scent spreading over the earth, tears, a lock of hair, a single drop from a stream.

A possible arc. For the prayer: the speaker addresses a God who already knows him; says no one else consoles him; says that remembrance is his light in the night; and asks not for what he deserves but for wings. For the love poem: longing and separation, the world filling with fragrance, then the speaker’s small offering to the Beloved.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • That the date, occasion or recipient of either poem is known.
  • That the rosy-cheeked poem is the text of the Writings. It is a provisional translation, and the English is the translator’s.
  • That the poem mourns the Ascension of Bahá’u’lláh, or that the Beloved is named in it. These are the translator’s inference.
  • That Masumian’s lines may be printed freely.
  • That the prayer was written to be sung or chanted. The sources do not say so, though ‘Abdu’l-Bahá encourages poems “to be chanted with heavenly music”.

On translations in published work. The Universal House of Justice, in its policy of 4 July 1999 on authorised and provisional translations, says:

Individual believers are free to translate into English any of the Holy Writings for their own use. Such translations may also be issued electronically, and may be published in circulars or journals without review by anyone other than the editors of the publications, but they must, in all cases, be clearly labeled as “provisional translations.”

(Policy on Authorized and Provisional Translations, 4 July 1999, item 2, in [source].) Item 3 of the same policy requires that translations in books and pamphlets be cleared by the Bahá’í World Centre first.
