The Extremes of Wealth and Poverty: What the Writings Require, and What They Leave Open

Last updated 4 August 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í teachings treat gross economic disparity as a moral disorder before it is a technical one, and prescribe not equality but moderation — a band with a floor beneath it and a ceiling above it, achieved by four converging means that must operate together: transformed individual conscience, voluntary sharing, structural legislation, and a law of giving that no institution may enforce.

Three things follow that are worth stating plainly before the evidence:

  • The target is the extremes, not difference. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá rejects absolute equality as firmly as He rejects concentration — “unjustified equality is also fraught with peril.” Anyone reading the corpus as a warrant for levelling is reading past the sentence that immediately follows the indictment.
  • The corpus contains no economic system, and says so. Shoghi Effendi returned to this point across a decade of letters. Bahá’ís “cannot claim to possess at present an economic order or system”; the House of Justice will develop one later, “in consultation with economic experts.” What exists now are principles and a small number of concrete legal instruments.
  • The phrase itself is Shoghi Effendi’s. “The abolition of the extremes of wealth and poverty” is his English summary formula from God Passes By, not a verbatim utterance of Bahá’u’lláh’s. The underlying teaching lives in the texts under other words — moderation, adjustment, equalization, voluntary sharing — and the substitution matters, because those words carry different obligations.

My position on what this asks of a reader today: the highest-leverage Bahá’í contribution to economic justice in 2026 is not advocacy for a redistribution policy, and it is not waiting for the future economic order to be revealed. It is the two things the House of Justice actually named in its 1 March 2017 letter — coherence between one’s own economic conduct and one’s beliefs, and community-building processes in populations that are themselves poor, working on the root causes of poverty where they live. That is a slower and less satisfying answer than either ideological programme offers. It is the one the texts support.

A house with a floor and a ceiling

Start with the shape of the thing, because the shape is unusual.

Almost every serious modern account of inequality argues in one direction. Either concentration at the top is the problem, and the remedy is to bring the top down; or destitution at the bottom is the problem, and the remedy is growth that lifts the bottom up. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, addressing a Paris audience in 1911, states both at once and refuses to choose:

“It is important to limit riches, as it is also of importance to limit poverty. Either extreme is not good.”

The image that follows is domestic and unsparing: “A financier with colossal wealth should not exist whilst near him is a poor man in dire necessity.” And then the diagnostic move that gives the principle its edge — “When we see poverty allowed to reach a condition of starvation it is a sure sign that somewhere we shall find tyranny.” Destitution is read as evidence, not as misfortune. It points somewhere. Someone is doing something.

What is being described is a house with both a floor and a ceiling — a band within which economic difference is legitimate and expected, bounded on both sides by law. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the boundedness explicit in the same passage: “There must be special laws made, dealing with these extremes of riches and of want.” Not exhortation. Laws.

Hold that shape. Everything below either builds the floor, lowers the ceiling, or works on the people who must be willing to live inside the walls.

What Bahá’u’lláh establishes, and what He withholds

Bahaullah supplies the moral frame and three legal instruments — and conspicuously withholds a programme. The Universal House of Justice states the omission as a fact about the Revelation rather than as an embarrassment: “Although Bahá’u’lláh does not set out in His Revelation a detailed economic system, a constant theme throughout the entire corpus of His teachings is the reorganization of human society” (1 March 2017).

The moral frame is stated as a relation of trust, not of charity. In the Hidden Words the poor are not a category of need but a category of responsibility:

“O YE RICH ONES ON EARTH! The poor in your midst are My trust; guard ye My trust, and be not intent only on your own ease.”

A trust is something held on behalf of its owner. The formulation makes indifference a breach rather than a coldness. Elsewhere in the same book the mechanism of that indifference is named precisely — “Tell the rich of the midnight sighing of the poor, lest heedlessness lead them into the path of destruction.” What separates the rich from the poor in Bahá’u’lláh’s account is first of all a failure of attention, and attention is trainable.

Yet nothing in this sanctifies poverty. The same passage of Gleanings that instructs the wealthy to “have the utmost regard for the poor” turns immediately to the poor themselves: “Please God, the poor may exert themselves and strive to earn the means of livelihood. This is a duty which, in this most great Revelation, hath been prescribed unto every one.” The Kitáb-i-Aqdas makes it law, and gives it a startling valuation:

“It is incumbent upon each one of you to engage in some occupation—such as a craft, a trade or the like. We have exalted your engagement in such work to the rank of worship of the one true God… The most despised of men in the sight of God are they who sit and beg.”

Two constituencies are dissolved by that sentence: the idle rich and the professional mendicant. Begging is abolished as an institution, alongside asceticism and monasticism. The Bahá’í position refuses both the sanctification of accumulation and the sanctification of destitution — a symmetry that will recur at every level of the argument.

The three instruments the Aqdas actually establishes are worth setting out side by side, because each attacks concentration from a different angle:

InstrumentMechanismEffect on concentration
Ḥuqúqu’lláh19% of the value of possessions once they exceed nineteen mithqáls of gold, then 19% of each further increase after expenses; residence and needed furnishings exemptA recurring claim on surplus rather than income — it bites hardest where accumulation is largest
Law of inheritanceEstate divided among seven categories of heirs; Ḥuqúqu’lláh and debts settled firstDisperses holdings each generation rather than compounding them
Obligation to workEvery person takes up a trade; mendicancy abolished; work in service ranked as worshipRemoves both the inherited idleness at the top and the enforced dependency at the bottom

The first of these is the one most often mistaken for a tax, and the mistake costs the principle its point. The Codification of the Law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh is emphatic that computation and payment “are exclusively a matter of conscience between the individual and God,” that soliciting it is prohibited, and that only general reminders are permissible. No believer may be approached individually. And it is precisely this — a levy on surplus wealth that no institution can enforce — which the Codification identifies as the thing ‘Abdu’l-Bahá meant by a spiritual solution to economic problems:

“the implications of the law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh for the realization of a number of the principles of the Faith, such as the elimination of extremes of wealth and poverty, and a more equitable distribution of resources, will increasingly become manifest as the friends assume in ever greater measure the responsibility for observing it.”

Note what has been done here. The single most direct instrument the Revelation provides against concentrated wealth has been made unenforceable on purpose. A system that could produce the outcome by compulsion alone would, on this reading, have solved the wrong problem. (Practical computation is treated separately in HuququllahComputation.)

Governing all of it is a rule Bahá’u’lláh states as general law in the Words of Paradise: “In all matters moderation is desirable. If a thing is carried to excess, it will prove a source of evil.” And the measure of a person is relocated away from holdings altogether — “Man’s merit lieth in service and virtue and not in the pageantry of wealth and riches.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the diagnosis, the limit, and the machinery

Nearly all the specific content comes from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, and His fullest treatment sits in an unlikely place — a chapter of Some Answered Questions titled “Strikes,” answering a question about labour disputes.

The diagnosis

He begins by refusing to locate the fault in either party alone: the difficulty has a twofold origin, “the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners” and “the gratuitous demands, the greed, and the intransigence of the workers.” Then He goes beneath both to the structure:

“the root cause of these difficulties lies in the law of nature that governs present-day civilization, for it results in a handful of people accumulating vast fortunes that far exceed their needs, while the greater number remain naked, destitute, and helpless. This is at once contrary to justice, to humanity, and to fairness; it is the very height of inequity.”

The comparison He then draws is worth pausing on, because it is doing argumentative work rather than decorating. Among animals — “a shepherd’s flock,” “a herd of deer in the wilderness,” “the songbirds that dwell in the mountains” — “a certain kind of justice and equality prevails,” and they “live in perfect peace and joy.” The disparity under discussion is uniquely human. It is therefore not natural, and not inevitable, and not to be excused as the price of complexity.

He also counts the cost on both sides of the ledger. Concentration is not merely unfair to the poor; it wastes the poor: “the lives of a vast multitude have been rendered fruitless.” And it burdens the rich, who come “under a most heavy burden” that “exhaust[s] one’s natural powers.” The moral appeal, when it comes, is put as a question no argument can absorb: “How can one see one’s fellow men hungry, destitute, and deprived, and yet live in peace and comfort in one’s splendid mansion?”

The limit

Immediately after the indictment comes the sentence that keeps this from being a levelling doctrine:

“However, absolute equality is just as untenable, for complete equality in wealth, power, commerce, agriculture, and industry would result in chaos and disorder, disrupt livelihoods, provoke universal discontent, and undermine the orderly conduct of the affairs of the community. For unjustified equality is also fraught with peril.”

The stated goal is “moderation,” and moderation is defined operationally: “the enactment of such laws and regulations as would prevent the unwarranted concentration of wealth in the hands of the few and satisfy the essential needs of the many.” A ceiling and a floor, again. In The Promulgation of Universal Peace He puts the same thought in the register of prophecy: “It is not meant that all will be equal, for inequality in degree and capacity is a property of nature… In the future there will be no very rich nor extremely poor.”

The machinery

Four mechanisms recur across the talks and Tablets, and they are unusually concrete for a body of religious writing.

Legislation, not sentiment alone. “One must therefore enact such laws and regulations as will moderate the excessive fortunes of the few and meet the basic needs of the myriad millions of the poor” (Some Answered Questions). And in Promulgation: “The greatest means for prevention is that whereby the laws of the community will be so framed and enacted that it will not be possible for a few to be millionaires and many destitute.” The Bahá’í teaching does not treat voluntary generosity as sufficient.

Profit-sharing, and the worker as stakeholder. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá specifies a share of “a fourth or fifth of the profits of the factory in accordance with its means,” on the reasoning that “the capital and the management come from the latter and the toil and labour from the former.” He adds provision for injury, incapacity, old age, and surviving children — “a modest pension from the revenues of the factory itself.” The obligation runs both ways: workers “should not make excessive demands… or go on strike,” with the rights of both parties “officially fixed” and enforceable. And He defends the state’s standing to intervene against the claim that this is a private matter: “the commercial, industrial, and agricultural affairs, and even the general business of the nation, are all intimately linked together: An impairment to one is a loss to all.”

A graduated levy with a subsistence floor. The village storehouse scheme — set out in a Tablet of 4 October 1912 and in a Promulgation talk — is the only fully worked institutional design in the corpus, and it is more radical than its age. A person whose income equals his necessary expenses pays nothing at all. Above that, rates climb through His worked examples from one-tenth to one-fourth to one-third, reaching one-half at the top. And beneath the floor the flow reverses: a person whose essential needs exceed his income, “provided he hath not been remiss in his work,” “must receive help from the general storehouse so that he may not remain in need and may live in comfort.” The storehouse funds orphans, the incapacitated, the needy, a village school, and public health; anything left over goes to the national treasury; elected village trustees administer it.

That is a negative income tax with a universal floor, financed locally, governed by consultation — sketched in 1912. Its stated result: “a man’s capacity for production and his needs will be equalized and reconciled through taxation… there will be no poor in the community.” The Aqdas notes make the floor an institutional obligation rather than a kindness — where a person is helpless or stricken by dire poverty, “it is incumbent on the wealthy or the Deputies to provide him with a monthly allowance for his subsistence,” the Deputies being the House of Justice.

It is no accident that this begins with the farmer. “The question of economics must commence with the farmer… for the farmer is the first active agent in human society.” Economic justice in these texts is built from the village upward, not legislated from the capital downward — a continuity with the Bahá’í approach to development traced in agriculture and SocialAction.

Voluntary sharing, ranked above the law. And then, having built all that legal machinery, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá ranks something above it. Among the teachings enumerated in the Tablets to The Hague is:

“voluntary sharing of one’s property with others among mankind. This voluntary sharing is greater than equality, and consists in this, that man should not prefer himself to others… But this should not be introduced by coercion so that it becomes a law and man is compelled to follow it.”

The reason is given elsewhere, in a letter to an American philanthropist: “the harvest of force is turmoil and the ruin of the social order. On the other hand voluntary sharing, the freely-chosen expending of one’s substance, leadeth to society’s comfort and peace.”

Wealth is not the enemy

Running underneath all of this is a valuation of wealth that neither ascetic nor acquisitive readers will find comfortable. In The Secret of Divine Civilization:

“Wealth is praiseworthy in the highest degree, if it is acquired by an individual’s own efforts and the grace of God, in commerce, agriculture, art and industry, and if it be expended for philanthropic purposes… Wealth is most commendable, provided the entire population is wealthy.”

The conditional in that last clause is the whole doctrine compressed. Wealth is not condemned; wealth in the presence of destitution is condemned — and the highest station is reserved not for the philanthropist but for the person who “should initiate measures which would universally enrich the masses of the people,” an achievement He ranks as “the supreme achievement” in the sight of God. The distinction is between relieving poverty and building the conditions under which it does not recur.

And the final cause is located inward. “The fundamentals of the whole economic condition are divine in nature and are associated with the world of the heart and spirit,” He tells an American audience — the change will come “not through warfare, but welfare,” when “hearts must be so cemented together, love must become so dominant that the rich shall most willingly extend assistance to the poor.”

Shoghi Effendi: the formula, and the fence

Shoghi Effendi does two things: he fixes the vocabulary, and he builds a fence around it.

The vocabulary comes in God Passes By, where “the abolition of the extremes of wealth and poverty” appears in the canonical list of the principles ‘Abdu’l-Bahá expounded in the West — set among the oneness of humanity, the harmony of science and religion, the equality of women and men, compulsory education, a world tribunal, and the exaltation of work to the rank of worship. It is worth knowing that this is a summary formula and not a translated utterance, because English-language Bahá’í discourse has largely inherited the phrase without its provenance, and the phrase is more absolute than most of what it summarises.

His own statement of the target concedes natural difference and then draws the line hard:

“social inequality is the inevitable outcome of the natural inequality of men. Human beings are different in ability and should, therefore, be different in their social and economic standing. Extremes of wealth and poverty should, however, be totally abolished. Those whose brains have contributed to the creation and improvement of the means of production must be fairly rewarded, though these means may be owned and controlled by others.” — 26 December 1935, written on his behalf

At the planetary scale, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh sketches the architecture that would be required: “The economic resources of the world will be organized, its sources of raw materials will be tapped and fully utilized, its markets will be coördinated and developed, and the distribution of its products will be equitably regulated.” Elimination of extremes, in the Guardian’s hands, stops being an ethical aspiration and becomes a structural requirement of world order — of a piece with world federation and a supreme tribunal.

The fence is the more useful contribution for a reader today. Across letters written on his behalf between 1932 and 1945 he repeats, with unusual consistency, that the Faith has no economics:

“the Teachings of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá do not provide specific and detailed solutions to all such economic questions, which mostly pertain to the domain of technical economics… The International House of Justice will have, in consultation with economic experts, to assist in the formulation and evolution of the Bahá’í economic system of the future. One thing, however, is certain: that the Cause neither accepts the theories of the Capitalistic economics in full, nor can it agree with the Marxists and Communists in their repudiation of the principle of private ownership and of this vital sacred right of the individual.” — 22 April 1939, written on his behalf

Private ownership is upheld as “a vital sacred right”; what requires “fundamental changes” is its “methods and features.” Between those two clauses sits most of the space in which Bahá’í economic thought will eventually have to be built. The same body of letters does name specific measures — progressive income tax “at quite a steep rate,” high death duties, profit-sharing (the correct term, he insists, not “income-sharing”), consultation between capital and labour, and a moral duty when writing a will to avoid “over-accumulation and concentration in a few individuals or groups of individuals.”

The House of Justice: from principle to conduct

Four documents carry the modern development, and they move steadily inward — from a statement about the world, to a critique of development, to an address to the individual believer.

1985 — The Promise of World Peace frames disparity as a security condition rather than a humanitarian one: “The inordinate disparity between rich and poor, a source of acute suffering, keeps the world in a state of instability, virtually on the brink of war.” The prescribed method is as notable as the diagnosis — consultation with experts across disciplines, “devoid of economic and ideological polemics, and involving the people directly affected in the decisions that must urgently be made.”

1995 — The Prosperity of Humankind turns the critique on the machinery of development itself. Poverty persists, it argues, not for want of resources but because “the necessary scientific and technological advances respond to a set of priorities only tangentially related to the real interests of the generality of humankind.” Then it indicts religion in the same breath, naming the doctrines “which cannot distinguish between contentment and mere passivity and which teach that poverty is an inherent feature of earthly life.” Quietism is treated as complicity.

2010 — to the Bahá’ís of Iran supplies a usable test. Wealth is legitimate on three conditions: how it was acquired (“by an individual’s own efforts”), whether the means used “enrich the generality of the people,” and how it is spent. Applied, this puts specific practices in the category of the “unworthy and unacceptable” — “the exploitation of others, the monopolization and manipulation of markets, and the production of goods that promote violence and immorality” — and it names two matters for scrutiny that most economic ethics leaves alone: the relationship between minimum wage and cost of living, and “the wide margin, often unjustifiable, between the production costs of certain goods and the price at which they are sold.”

1 March 2017 — to the Bahá’ís of the World is the definitive contemporary statement, and its addressee is the point. It is directed “more especially to the individual believer.” Its diagnosis is unsparing:

“The welfare of any segment of humanity is inextricably bound up with the welfare of the whole… Unconscionable quantities of wealth are being amassed, and the instability this creates is made worse by how income and opportunity are spread so unevenly both between nations and within nations. But it need not be so… There is no justification for continuing to perpetuate structures, rules, and systems that manifestly fail to serve the interests of all peoples.”

It then declines both available orthodoxies in a single paragraph. Against the market consensus: the Revelation challenges the assumption “that self-interest, far from needing to be restrained, drives prosperity, and that progress depends upon its expression through relentless competition.” Against ascetic and anti-wealth positions: “neither are the teachings in sympathy with sweeping dismissals of wealth as inherently distasteful or immoral, and asceticism is prohibited.” What remains is a three-word standard — “Wealth must serve humanity” — and a relocation of the work from policy to conduct:

“Every choice a Bahá’í makes—as employee or employer, producer or consumer, borrower or lender, benefactor or beneficiary—leaves a trace, and the moral duty to lead a coherent life demands that one’s economic decisions be in accordance with lofty ideals.”

Ḥuqúqu’lláh is named as the discipline that makes this concrete — “examining one’s life to determine what is a necessity and then discharging with joy one’s obligation.” The letter closes by tying the principle to the community-building enterprise and to place: “The larger the presence of a Bahá’í community in a population, the greater its responsibility to find ways of addressing the root causes of the poverty in its surroundings.”

Five years later the Riḍván 2026 message still names “economic inequity” among the conditions against which the friends’ efforts occur — and commends the youth in particular “for resisting the impulse to criticize and condemn, finding instead constructive ways to steer around these imposing obstacles.”

The boundaries the guidance itself draws

Because this topic sits close to live political argument, it helps to have the negative space mapped as clearly as the positive. The corpus rules out, on its own terms:

  • Equality of outcome — rejected as producing “chaos and disorder,” and as “fraught with peril.”
  • Abolition of private property — upheld as “this vital sacred right of the individual.”
  • Coercive redistribution — “the harvest of force is turmoil and the ruin of the social order.”
  • A blanket endorsement of existing markets — the Cause does not accept capitalist theory “in full”; its “present form… would require adjustments.”
  • A complete Bahá’í economics available now — “the Bahá’ís, therefore, cannot claim to possess at present an economic order or system which they can officially associate with the Faith, nor should they now attempt to establish any such economic scheme.”
  • Charity as a sufficient response — the texts call for enacted law, graduated taxation, profit-sharing, pensions, and a subsistence floor, all of which are obligations of the body politic.
  • Spiritual quietism — “warfare, but welfare” rejects sedition, not structural reform; and The Prosperity of Humankind names passivity-as-contentment a failure of religion.

Each of these has a partisan echo, and the pattern of the exclusions is itself informative: the teaching declines every available political home.

What the world looks like from here, in 2026

The corpus supplies the principles; it cannot describe the present state of the field. The following figures are field description, drawn from external sources, and they will age.

The scale of concentration has moved a long way past what ‘Abdu’l-Bahá described to Parisian audiences in 1911. The World Inequality Report 2026 finds that within almost every world region the top 1% now hold more wealth than the bottom 90% combined, and that the top 0.001% — fewer than 60,000 people — own three times more wealth than the entire bottom half of humanity. Roughly 1% of global GDP flows each year from poorer countries to richer ones in net income transfers. The report also runs the environmental ledger alongside the economic one: the top 10% account for 77% of the emissions associated with private capital ownership, the bottom 50% for 3% — an empirical restatement of the link the 2017 letter drew between pursuing “economic gain without regard for how the natural environment, which provides sustenance for all, is affected.”

At the top, Oxfam’s January 2026 report records billionaire wealth at $18.3 trillion, up more than 16% in 2025 — an increase of $2.5 trillion in a single year, which Oxfam notes is close to the total wealth held by the bottom half of humanity, some 4.1 billion people. The number of billionaires passed 3,000 for the first time.

At the bottom, the UN’s 2025 Sustainable Development Goals report counts 808 million people in extreme poverty — 9.9% of humanity, one person in ten — measured against the World Bank’s revised $3.00-a-day line (2021 purchasing power parity). That revision, made in June 2025, raised the count by roughly 125 million people from the previous estimate of 677 million: the line moved, and a hundred million people crossed to the wrong side of it without anything in their lives changing. The report judges eradication by 2030 “highly unlikely.”

Set the 1912 storehouse sketch against those numbers and the gap is not embarrassing so much as clarifying. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s design was for a village. What is now being described is a planetary distribution that no village-scale instrument can reach — which is precisely why the Guardian insisted that the economic teachings mattered less, on their own, than “what it has on the international question,” and why The Promise of World Peace filed the disparity under peace rather than under charity.

Meanwhile the Bahá’í community’s own capacity in this field has grown by an order of magnitude within one generation. The Riḍván 2021 message reports that against some 250 sustained social and economic development projects in 1996, there are now 1,500; that the number of Bahá’í-inspired organisations has quadrupled to surpass 160; and that more than 70,000 grassroots social action initiatives of short duration are undertaken each year — a fifty-fold increase. The Bahá’í International Community has carried the argument into intergovernmental settings, most directly in Towards a Just Economic Order: Conceptual Foundations and Moral Prerequisites, its statement to the 56th session of the Commission for Social Development (29 January 2018), which puts the case at the level of assumption rather than policy:

“Can an understanding of economics grounded in a presumption of individuals or groups gaining advantage over others lead to anything but the grossly unequal conditions multiplying on every side? Alternatively, what would global economic structures look like if collaboration were understood to be a more powerful driver of development than competition?”

That is the argument the Bahá’í community is actually making in the world’s forums: not a policy proposal, but a challenge to the conceptual model beneath the policies.

What follows for a reader deciding how to act

The temptation with a topic this large is to conclude that the answer is structural and therefore someone else’s. The 2017 letter forecloses that, deliberately, by addressing the individual first. Three lines of action follow, in ascending order of difficulty.

Make your own economic life coherent. This is the least glamorous and the most explicitly required. It means the ordinary particulars: what you pay people, what margins you take, what you buy and how much, what you owe, what you leave. Ḥuqúqu’lláh is the discipline the House of Justice names for this, and it works less as a transfer than as an annual forcing function — you cannot compute it without first deciding, in writing, what in your life is a necessity. Most people never make that determination once. The law obliges it repeatedly. Related practical treatments: HuququllahComputation, charitable-giving-beyond-huquq, bahai-aligned-investing, inheritance-and-family-wealth.

Work on root causes where you live, at a scale your community can actually carry. The 2017 letter ties responsibility to presence: the larger the Bahá’í community in a population, the greater its obligation to address the root causes of poverty in its surroundings. The OSED framework is insistent that “the scope and complexity of social action must be commensurate with the human resources available in a village or neighbourhood to carry it forward,” and that “social change is not a project that one group of people carries out for the benefit of another.” The 70,000 short-duration initiatives per year are not a lesser category of this work; they are its normal form. SocialAction and social-action-guidance treat the spectrum in full.

If you bring technical skill, build the instruments of legibility and local control. The gap between ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s village storehouse and a planetary distribution is, in significant part, a gap in knowledge infrastructure — the storehouse worked because a village board could see every household’s income and needs. At planetary scale that visibility does not exist, and where it does, it is owned by the parties least interested in redistribution. The Prosperity of Humankind diagnoses precisely this: the problem is that technical capacity “respond[s] to a set of priorities only tangentially related to the real interests of the generality of humankind,” and the remedy it names is “a vast increase in access to knowledge.” So: measurement tools a community can run on itself and own the output of; local economic data held by the people it describes; open tooling for cooperatives and village-scale finance; Ḥuqúqu’lláh and household-budget computation aids that make the “what is a necessity” question tractable rather than paralysing. The discipline the Bahá’í framework imposes on all of it is that the technology must serve a need the population has identified, and must build the population’s capacity rather than substituting for it.

None of this amounts to an economic system, and it should not be presented as one. What it amounts to is the claim the 2017 letter actually makes: that a community which orders its own economic life coherently, and which works patiently on poverty where it lives, is generating the experience from which a future order will eventually be built. “Such efforts will add to a growing storehouse of knowledge in this regard.” The metaphor is not accidental.

The argument in one line

The extremes are treated in these texts as a symptom of a failure of relationship, which is why the remedies are layered rather than singular: law sets the outer bounds because sentiment cannot be relied upon; voluntary sharing is ranked above law because it exceeds what law can reach; and Ḥuqúqu’lláh — the instrument aimed most directly at concentrated wealth — is made unenforceable, because an outcome achieved by compulsion alone would have left the actual disorder untouched.

Open questions and honest limits

  • There is no Bahá’í economic system, and none is coming soon. Shoghi Effendi assigned its formulation to the House of Justice “in consultation with economic experts,” at an unspecified future point. Anyone presenting a worked Bahá’í macroeconomics is going beyond the sources.
  • The storehouse scheme’s status is unsettled. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s tax schedule is given in dollars, in worked examples, for an agrarian village in 1912. Whether the specific rates are prescriptive, illustrative, or contingent on that setting is not resolved by the texts, and this page does not resolve it.
  • The English formula is a translation artefact in part. “The abolition of the extremes of wealth and poverty” is Shoghi Effendi’s summary of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Western talks in God Passes By. It is not a rendering of a single passage of Bahá’u’lláh’s, and it is more categorical than most of the underlying material — a point that should be held whenever the phrase is quoted as scripture.
  • Where the ceiling actually sits is not specified. The texts are clear that a ceiling exists and that law must set it. No text says at what level. “Moderation” is defined by function (“prevent the unwarranted concentration of wealth… satisfy the essential needs of the many”) rather than by threshold.
  • Some widely circulated inequality statistics could not be verified to a primary source for this page — notably the frequently quoted claim that the top 10% hold 75% of global wealth and the bottom half 2%. The figures cited above are the ones traceable to the World Inequality Lab, Oxfam, and UN Statistics directly. All external figures date to late 2025 and January 2026 and will need re-checking.
  • The Bahá’í activity figures are from Riḍván 2021 and are now five years old; the Nine Year Plan has run its course since, and current counts are likely higher.
