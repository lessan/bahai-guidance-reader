Stewardship of Limited Means: The Bahá’í Fund and the Ordering of Priorities

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The Bahá’í world runs a global enterprise — a World Centre, national communities on every continent, Houses of Worship from Wilmette to Battambang, a training-institute process reaching millions, pioneers, relief, development — on money that arrives entirely unbidden. Every device an ordinary organisation would use to close the gap between ambition and income is closed to Bahá’í institutions by explicit ruling: no soliciting the public, no accepting money from non-Bahá’ís for Bahá’í purposes, no assessment or tithe, no pressure “however slight and indirect”, no published lists of who gave what, no enforceable pledges, and — for Ḥuqúqu’lláh — no demanding it at all, ever, from anyone. What remains is a system in which the revenue side is fixed by conscience and cannot be managed.

My position on the central question: because the income side is deliberately un-engineerable, priority-setting is not an accompaniment to the Bahá’í Fund; it is the Fund’s principal instrument of governance. The characteristic Bahá’í financial virtue is therefore not generosity — generosity is assumed, unmeasured, and nobody’s business — but the institutional nerve to stop doing things. Shoghi Effendi named the whole discipline in a single clause in 1957: “a wise economy, the elimination of non-essentials, concentration on essentials and a careful supervision.” And the ordering is not improvised. Across a century the guidance lays down a stable lexical order — Ḥuqúqu’lláh before the Fund; the general interests before the local; the teaching work before edifices; and, on the individual’s side, one’s family, one’s debts and one’s old age before sacrificial giving. Most believers have never seen that order set out in one place. It is set out below.

The practical judgement I would press hardest, for anyone deciding how to act: give unrestricted. Earmarking is a genuine right and must never be abridged — the friends are “absolutely free to specify the object and purpose of their donations” (Shoghi Effendi, 26 November 1923) — but its aggregate effect is to move priority-setting from the body that can see the whole to the individual who can see one project. The Guardian named the failure mode in 1950: “too much labelling of contributions will tie the hands of the Assembly.” Field evidence from 2026 suggests it has happened.

The Fund was designed so that its income cannot be engineered

The institution has a birthday. On 12 March 1923, writing to the Bahá’ís of the West as the first National Assemblies were forming, Shoghi Effendi called the Bahá’í Fund into existence and, in the same paragraph, tied it to the reason it had to exist at all:

“As the progress and extension of spiritual activities is dependent and conditioned upon material means, it is of absolute necessity that immediately after the establishment of local as well as national Spiritual Assemblies, a Bahá’í Fund be established, to be placed under the exclusive control of the Spiritual Assembly… The members of the Spiritual Assembly will at their own discretion expend it to promote the Teaching Campaign, to help the needy, to establish educational Bahá’í institutions, to extend in every way possible their sphere of service.” — Shoghi Effendi, letter of 12 March 1923 (Bahá’í Administration)

Two clauses in that sentence do all the later work. Exclusive control means the money, once given, is the institution’s to direct — the donor’s authority ends at the moment of the gift. At their own discretion means the Assembly is not executing the donor’s plan; it is executing its own. Everything that follows about priorities depends on those two phrases holding.

Within three years the Guardian had to defend the other side of the arrangement. Assemblies, having discovered budgets, had begun to discover pressure:

“I feel urged to remind you of the necessity of ever bearing in mind the cardinal principle that all contributions to the Fund are to be purely and strictly voluntary in character. It should be made clear and evident to every one that any form of compulsion, however slight and indirect, strikes at the very root of the principle underlying the formation of the Fund ever since its inception. While appeals of a general character, carefully-worded and moving and dignified in tone are welcome under all circumstances, it should be left entirely to the discretion of every conscientious believer to decide upon the nature, the amount, and purpose of his or her contribution.” — Shoghi Effendi, letter of 10 January 1926 (Bahá’í Administration)

Six months later, in his own handwriting appended to a letter written on his behalf, he compressed it further: “We should appeal to but not coerce the friends” (9 July 1926). And in 1932, asked about donations collected at a Convention, his secretary drew the line at the exact place where religious fundraising usually crosses it — permitting gatherings held in “a true quiet, prayerful atmosphere when a person is of his own accord aroused to make some sacrifice” and forbidding those in which “mob psychology is used to induce them to pay.” The letter then makes the ruling operational rather than pious: “The distinction is very delicate, but it is for the chairman to use his power to see that one desirable form is not corrupted into the other” (28 May 1932).

The second closure is stranger to outside eyes and more consequential. Bahá’í institutions may not take money from non-Bahá’ís for Bahá’í purposes. The reasoning, given while the Wilmette House of Worship was being financed by a community that plainly could not afford it, is not about purity of funds but about the nature of the gift:

“So vast and significant an enterprise as the construction of the first Mashriqu’l-Adhkár of the West should be supported, not by the munificence of a few but by the joint contributions of the entire mass of the convinced followers of the Faith… the emanations of spiritual power and inspiration destined to radiate from the central Edifice of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár will to a very large extent depend upon the range and variety of the contributing believers, as well as upon the nature and degree of self-abnegation which their unsolicited offerings will entail.” — Shoghi Effendi, letter of 25 October 1929 (Bahá’í Administration)

The same letter calls these institutions “Bahá’u’lláh’s gifts bestowed upon the world” — and a gift cannot be paid for by its recipient. When a widow offered her late husband’s bequest to the Temple in 1950, the ruling was courteous and absolute: “because he was not a Bahá’í, we cannot use his money for our purposes, as we consider our Faith and its institutions our free gift to humanity; you can, however, and indeed should, accept it for charity and expend it in his name” (5 July 1950). Gifts from sympathisers go into a separately maintained charitable account, never into the Fund proper. The boundary is drawn around specifically Bahá’í activity, not around everything Bahá’ís do: a Bahá’í-run school open to all children may take gate money from a school concert, “not the same as a bazaar where the things sold are solely for the Bahá’í Fund” (30 June 1952).

Three further closures complete the design. Confidentiality: Assemblies may keep records, “but no pressure must ever be brought on the Bahá’ís to contribute, it must be voluntary, and should be considered confidential” (26 October 1945). No conditions: “He does not feel that it is desirable to lay down any conditions for giving to the Bahá’í Fund” (19 October 1947). No enforceable pledges: the House of Justice permits pledges as a planning device, allows a private and courteous reminder, and then removes their teeth — “such pledges are not an obligation in any legal sense; their redemption is entirely a matter of conscience. Lists of those making pledges must not be publicized” (7 August 1985, annexed memorandum).

Add them up and the picture is unusual. A treasurer of a Bahá’í institution has no lever on income at all. He may explain, report, thank, and wait. Everything else that can be done about the gap between what the community wants to do and what it can pay for has to be done downstream.

Every era has run short — and the shortfall is not a scandal

It would be easy to read the recurring appeals as evidence that something is failing. The record suggests the opposite: the gap is a permanent feature, and the institutions have said so plainly in every generation, usually in the same breath as announcing an expansion.

Shoghi Effendi, in the very letter that set the Temple’s financing rule, was candid about the arithmetic: “we cannot fail to realize at the present stage of our work the extremely limited number of contributors qualified to lend financial support to such a vast, such an elaborate and costly enterprise” — and then refused to let the arithmetic govern: “I beseech you, dear friends, not to allow considerations of number, or the consciousness of the limitation of our resources… to blur your vision, to dim your hopes, or to paralyse your efforts” (25 October 1929).

The Universal House of Justice inherited the condition intact. Its first substantial letter on the subject, of 18 December 1963 — written to lay the needs of the Fund before the world community on the eve of the Nine Year Plan — records a decision most institutions would not disclose:

“It is these very areas which are the most fruitful in teaching, and a sum of money spent here will produce ten times—even a hundred times—the results obtainable in other parts of the world. Yet in the past months the Universal House of Justice has had to refuse a number of appeals for assistance from such areas because there just was not enough money in the International Fund.” — The Universal House of Justice, 18 December 1963 (muhj-1963-1986)

That is the whole problem in three lines: the highest-yield opportunities identified, and declined for want of money. Two and a half years later the Riḍván 1966 message named the arithmetic behind it and drew the only conclusion available: “Since only those who have openly proclaimed their recognition of Bahá’u’lláh are permitted to contribute financially to the establishment of His World Order, it is apparent that more, much more is required from the few now so privileged.” The word privileged is doing deliberate work — it is the same word used when the House of Justice asked communities to help new believers see that “the voluntary act of contributing to the Fund is both a privilege and a spiritual obligation” (2 February 1966). The shortfall is reported as an honour rather than a complaint, in the same messages that record requests being refused.

Thirty years after 1963 the same sentence recurs in different words. Launching the Three Year Plan on 5 March 1993, the House of Justice wrote that the work “demand[s] material resources which have not been adequate for some time, even though substantial increases have been made in the contributions to Bahá’í Funds.”

Two episodes show what the shortfall looks like when it becomes acute. The first is political. After the Iranian Revolution of 1979, “at one moment, 61% of the income of the international funds was cut off” — a single-year loss of nearly two-thirds of international income, disclosed in a letter of 18 November 1991 that was itself reporting a fresh crisis. That 1991 letter is the most revealing document in the whole corpus on how the institutions behave under strain:

“In response to the growing needs of the teaching work and the Arc Projects, we have for several years been drastically cutting the expenditure of the World Center, canceling or postponing many activities which, although valuable, cannot be pursued under the present condition of shortage of funds. This process cannot be taken much further without seriously hampering the ability of the World Center to function at the level which the interests of the Faith require.” — The Universal House of Justice, 18 November 1991 (muhj-1986-2001)

Note the sequence. Cutting came first, over several years, and the appeal came only when cutting had reached its floor. And the appeal, when it came, asked for two things at once — increased contributions and “the diversion of funds from those local and national projects which are not essential.” The House of Justice did not simply ask for more; it asked national and local institutions to stop doing things.

The second episode is architectural. The Mount Carmel building programme announced on 31 August 1987 came with a number attached, and the number was frank about its own uncertainty: “It is impossible at this stage to give an accurate estimate of the cost of these projects. All that we can now say is that in the immediate future two objectives have to be met: to accumulate rapidly a reserve of fifty million dollars… and to provide an income of between twenty and twenty-five million dollars for the Bahá’í International Fund for each of the next ten years.” A fifty-million-dollar reserve and a decade of twenty-to-twenty-five million a year, requested from a community that may not be solicited and whose contributions may not be tracked publicly, with no fundraising campaign permitted in the ordinary sense of the word.

Even a solution can create a shortfall. In its Riḍván 2003 message the House of Justice reported that contributions to the newly established World Centre Endowment Fund “have not yet reached a level equal to the annual need” — and named the trade-off it made in response: setting aside five million dollars to build an income-producing corpus “by drawing upon the Bahá’í International Fund… suspending activities in other fields that it would have been normal to pursue.” Building an endowment meant suspending programmes, and the House of Justice said so in a Riḍván message to the whole world.

The discipline lives on the spending side, not the giving side

If income cannot be managed, expenditure must be. The guidance on how to spend Bahá’í money is older than the House of Justice and considerably more pointed than most friends expect.

The earliest formulation is also the most modern in temper. Writing on the Guardian’s behalf in 1929 about the financial questions facing the American community, his secretary set out something very close to a portfolio method: “We should study the needs of the Cause, find which field will give the greatest yield, and then appropriate the necessary funds. And such a task is surely most difficult and responsible” (19 December 1929). Yield, not merit; and the difficulty is acknowledged rather than waved away.

Four years earlier the Guardian had already named the failure mode that this method exists to prevent — not waste, but dispersion. He urged the friends “not to dissipate their efforts, but to seek, after frank, mature, and continuous deliberation, to arrive at a common conclusion as to the most urgent requirements and needs of the hour, and having unified their views to strive to uphold and enforce them with promptitude, whole-heartedness, and understanding” (16 January 1925, Bahá’í Administration). That instruction is addressed to the friends collectively, through consultation, not to a finance committee. Priority-setting in this system is a consultative act before it is an accounting one.

What the Guardian actually ranked, when he ranked, is instructive. In 1936 he named the chief claim on a national fund, and it was neither buildings nor administration: “the continuous expenditure of a considerable sum to provide for travelling expenses of teachers who are in need constitutes in these days the chief obligation of the national fund” (14 November 1936). In 1952 he vetoed a proposed property with a sentence that reads like a modern capital-allocation memo: “Bahá’í funds should not be invested in building up a place that has dear associations for a number of the friends, but is not going to really serve a large group of the believers” (8 June 1952). Sentiment is not a criterion. Reach is.

And in 1957, in what may be the single most useful paragraph in the corpus for anyone administering a Bahá’í budget, the Guardian’s secretary described how the Shrine of the Báb, the International Archives and the gardens of Mount Carmel had actually been paid for:

“It is only through a wise economy, the elimination of non-essentials, concentration on essentials and a careful supervision that the Guardian himself has been able to build the Shrine and the International Archives at the World Centre, and surround the Holy Places here by what appear in the eyes of the public to be lavish gardens, but are in reality the result of rigorous and economical planning.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 8 August 1957

The same letter continues into a piece of institutional psychology that is easy to miss. It warns an African National Assembly against letting a Temple built with foreign money teach the local community that “our resources are infinite and that the affairs of the Cause can be supported from abroad,” and rules that “under no circumstances should a heavy budget be imposed upon such weak communities, and thus discourage them from the outset.” Visible economy is not only cheaper; it is educative. A community that watches its institutions spend carefully learns that the money is real, and that it is theirs.

The Universal House of Justice made that link explicit in the Five Year Plan review of 25 May 1975, under a heading it wrote for the purpose — Wisdom in the Use of Bahá’í Funds:

“The more rigorous the exercise of economy on the part of National Spiritual Assemblies, the sooner will the body of the friends be encouraged to feel financial responsibility toward the progress of the Faith in their areas, to place greater reliance upon the wise administration of the National Spiritual Assembly, and to offer their resources, however modest they may be, for the furthering of its plans and activities.” — The Universal House of Justice, 25 May 1975 (muhj-1963-1986)

The same statement gives the reason that supervision matters even when the sums are small, and it is a reason about people rather than money: lax control “is both an unfair temptation to the untrustworthy and a test to the body of the believers, causing them to become disenchanted with Bahá’í administration and weakening their resolve to fulfill their sacred obligation of contributing to the Fund.” Extravagance does not merely waste; it converts contributors into sceptics, and the loss compounds on the income side where nothing can be done about it.

Two decades on, writing to the Conference of the Continental Boards of Counsellors on 26 December 1995 as the Four Year Plan began, the House of Justice added the modern corrective — that money is not itself an input to growth:

“As experience has shown, however, the expenditure of money does not, by itself, bring results. The challenge before you is to help develop in the various institutions and agencies involved in the execution of the Plan the capacity to expend funds in a judicious and effective manner.” — The Universal House of Justice, 26 December 1995 (muhj-1986-2001)

Capacity to spend well is named there as a thing to be built, alongside capacity to teach and to educate. That is an unusual claim for a religious body to make about its own treasuries, and it is the claim on which this whole article rests.

The order of precedence, laid out in one place

Scattered across a hundred years of letters is a consistent ranking. Assembled, it reads less like a set of pious preferences and more like a lexical order: each rule resolves a specific collision, and the rules do not contradict each other.

Several of these deserve unpacking, because the flat statement misleads.

The local-versus-national rule is not a prohibition on local funds; it is a rule about ranking under scarcity, and it is immediately qualified. The same 1985 memorandum that states it turns around and recommends the opposite sequence for teaching purposes: “the stability of the National Assembly rests on the firmness of the Local Spiritual Assemblies, and in the matter of educating the friends in the importance of the Fund, it is often most practical and efficacious to concentrate at first on the development of the local funds and the efficient operation of the Local Spiritual Assemblies.” Priority of claim and priority of attention run in opposite directions, and both statements are in the same document. A National Assembly that used the precedence rule to starve local funds would have misread it.

Nor does precedence mean sequence. In 1953, with the international work newly urgent, the Guardian’s secretary corrected exactly that inference: “it certainly was not his intent that the friends must contribute first to the Local and then the National Funds, before contributing to the international activities of the Faith, which at this time are of paramount importance” — restating that “everyone is free to contribute to whatever funds they wish, and to the degree their conscience and feeling of sacrifice moves them” (25 March 1953). The precedence order tells an institution how to rank claims on money it holds. It does not tell an individual which envelope to fill first.

The rule that Bahá’í work precedes general charity is argued, not asserted, and the argument is worth reading before anyone repeats the conclusion:

“We must always bear in mind that there are so few Bahá’ís in the world, relative to the world’s population, and so many people in need, that even if all of us gave all we had, it would not alleviate more than an infinitesimal amount of suffering. This does not mean we must not help the needy, we should; but our contributions to the Faith are the surest way of lifting once and for all time the burden of hunger and misery from mankind… Non-Bahá’ís cannot contribute to our work or do it for us; so really our first obligation is to support our own teaching work, as this will lead to the healing of the nations.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 8 December 1947

The reasoning is comparative advantage, not indifference. A Bahá’í community’s marginal dollar buys almost nothing against world poverty and buys something no other dollar can buy against the causes of it. The pastoral caveat matters as much as the ruling: this does not mean we must not help the needy, we should. The question of charitable giving beyond the Fund is treated in charitable-giving-beyond-huquq, and the wider economic teaching in wealth-and-poverty.

Ḥuqúqu’lláh is not a larger Fund; it is a different institution

Friends routinely file the Right of God under “giving,” alongside the Fund, and the guidance goes to some trouble to prevent it. The two differ in origin, in law, in who decides, and in what the believer may do with the money.

Origin. The Fund is an administrative institution created by the Guardian in 1923. Ḥuqúqu’lláh is a law of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas: “Should anyone acquire one hundred mithqáls of gold, nineteen mithqáls thereof are God’s and to be rendered unto Him, the Fashioner of earth and heaven” (¶97). Bahá’u’lláh states the purpose in the same verse — “By this means He hath desired to purify what ye possess” — and elsewhere gives the reason the law was accepted at all: “He Who is the Eternal Truth—exalted be His glory—hath made the fulfilment of every undertaking on earth dependent on material means. Hence it is enjoined upon every individual to offer that which is the Right of God.”

Whose money it is. This is the distinction that does the most work. Writing to the National Spiritual Assembly of Iran on 25 October 1970, the House of Justice put it without softening: “whatever portion of one’s wealth is due to the Ḥuqúqu’lláh belongs to the World Centre of the Cause of God, not to the individuals concerned. Thus the friends should not follow their own volition and judgement in using any of the funds set aside for Ḥuqúqu’lláh for any other purpose, even for charitable contributions of the Faith.” Money owed as Ḥuqúq is not the believer’s to be generous with. Fund contributions are.

Who decides how it is spent. Ḥuqúq is disbursed by the Head of the Faith alone. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Will and Testament directs it to the Guardian; in his absence, to the House of Justice — “the authority in the Cause to whom all must turn.” Consequently, and unlike the Fund, Ḥuqúq cannot be earmarked and cannot be given in anyone’s honour, and no believer can discharge another’s obligation. The accounts are kept separate for exactly that reason: the disposition of Ḥuqúq “is subject to decision by the Central Authority in the Cause to which all must turn, whereas the purposes of the contributions to other Funds may be determined by the donors themselves” (Codification).

What it is spent on. Shoghi Effendi, citing the Will and Testament, listed the purposes in 1933: “teaching the Cause of God in countries throughout the East and the West, establishing institutions, building Bahá’í Temples and promoting benevolent undertakings and the general weal.” ‘Abdu’l-Bahá names the same span — “the relief of the poor, the disabled, the needy, and the orphans, and for other vital needs of the Cause of God.” A letter of 16 February 1998 written on the House of Justice’s behalf is unusually direct about what is and is not published: “The House of Justice does not issue an accounting of the current expenditure of these funds. However, there is no secret as to the uses to which the funds are put.” It then enumerates them, and adds the datum that explains the whole flow of money in the Bahá’í world — that these funds sustain developments throughout a world community “many of whose national budgets must be subsidized by the House of Justice, owing to the fact that the vast majority of the world’s Bahá’ís are poor and cannot adequately support their national funds.”

How it may be asked for: never. The prohibition on solicitation is stronger for Ḥuqúq than for anything else in the Faith, and Bahá’u’lláh gives the reason in terms of the Cause’s dignity rather than the donor’s comfort:

“Time and again have We written and commanded that no one should solicit such payment. The offering of every person that voluntarily tendereth the Ḥuqúqu’lláh with the utmost joy and pleasure may be accepted, otherwise acceptance was not and is not permissible… were a person to possess the whole world and would tender his possessions at the cost of degrading the honour of the Cause, even to the extent of a grain of mustard, it would be essential and imperative to refuse to accept such wealth.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Ḥuqúqu’lláh—The Right of God

There is a further asymmetry worth naming for anyone tempted to treat one as a substitute for the other. Contributions to the Fund cannot count towards Ḥuqúq; but a believer may, at his own discretion, treat Fund contributions as an expense when computing the surplus on which Ḥuqúq falls due — or may instead make them only out of money already purified. A letter of 3 February 1987 leaves it open, and even allows treating “some contributions in one way and some in the other.” The mechanics of computation are worked through in HuququllahComputation, and the spiritual character in huqququllah-not-a-tax.

The institution around the law has been built out steadily and is still being built. Ḥuqúq applied only to believers of Iran and the Middle East until Riḍván 1991, when the House of Justice announced that from Riḍván 1992 “the Law of Ḥuqúqu’lláh, the Right of God, will become universally applicable.” The International Board of Trustees was established in 2005, taking over from the Hand of the Cause ‘Alí-Muḥammad Varqá as Chief Trustee; by 2021 it coordinated 33 National and Regional Boards and over 1,000 Representatives. On 27 November 2025 the House of Justice reported the Board’s growth from three members to seven, the extension of terms from three years to five, and a change of name: “since there is no longer a Chief Trustee, members of the National and Regional Boards, previously referred to as Deputy Trustees, will henceforth be known simply as Trustees.” This is what an institution looks like when it grows into a law over a century — see evolving-guidance.

How a global enterprise actually budgets

The money moves through four permanent layers, plus a set of purpose-built funds established as needs arose.

At the bottom of that stack the process is thoroughly ordinary, and its ordinariness is the point. The Australian National Assembly’s Bahá’í Treasurers’ Manual defines budgeting as “the making of a systematic plan of spending of anticipated income” and insists on a sequence that any strategist would recognise: “The planning process should be undertaken first and should inform the development of the budget… Plans are partly dependent on the financial resources available, and an iterative process may be necessary to arrive at an approved plan and budget.” Plan, cost, re-plan. Not: raise money, then decide.

Two features of that manual bear directly on priority-setting. The first is the community pledge — prepared a month or two before the Bahá’í financial year begins and presented for community consultation at the Annual General Meeting at Riḍván, aggregating what the Assembly will budget to send to the National Fund with an estimate of what individuals will send directly. It is a forecast, consulted upon in public, with no figure attached to any name. The second is a standing recommendation that reads as the operational form of everything above: Local Assemblies are asked to budget “to the National Fund, and not to other national level funds, leaving the National Spiritual Assembly free to use funds according to national needs and priorities,” and likewise to the Continental and International Funds, “leaving the Universal House of Justice free to decide how the funds are used.”

The design is visible in that sentence: at each layer, the institution asks to be given discretion rather than instructions. It is the 1923 principle — at their own discretion — expressed as budget policy in 2025.

The upward flows are real and large. In its Riḍván 1973 review of the Nine Year Plan the House of Justice disclosed that “sixty percent of the international funds of the Faith is used to assist the work of National Spiritual Assemblies, to promote the teaching work and to defend the Cause against attacks in many parts of the world. Without such help from the Bahá’í world community many National Assemblies would be paralyzed in their efforts of expansion and deepening.” The International Fund is not primarily a World Centre budget; it is largely a redistribution mechanism.

The House of Justice also seeds priorities with its own money when it wants a behaviour to start. Having called in the Riḍván 1996 message for the deputization of institute teachers, it announced on 6 August 1996 a contribution of US$300,000 divided among the five Continental Funds, adding with disarming candour that “this contribution is but a fraction of the amount called for.” The International Teaching Centre then did something rarely seen in religious finance: it identified the three figures around which worldwide deputization needs clustered — US$1,200, US$3,600 and US$7,200 per staff member per year — and had Counsellors pass them to Auxiliary Board members, so that friends wishing to deputize could choose a region and know the price. Sixteen years later the same instrument served a different purpose. Told that the persecuted believers of Iran were eager to contribute to the Temples Fund, and judging that any material offering from them “risks being misrepresented by the enemies of the Faith,” the House of Justice declined the contributions — and contributed five million dollars to the Temples Fund on their behalf (Department of the Secretariat, 2012).

Below all of this, one layer has deliberately been left without a treasury. Asked in 2015 whether Area Teaching Committees might hold cluster funds, the House of Justice answered that “a ‘Cluster Fund’ cannot be established” and that cluster agencies “would not engage in fund-raising or solicit contributions from the believers” — pointing instead to Local Assemblies hosting events, gifts toward specific projects, earmarked contributions through the National Fund or its regional branch, and regional treasurers appointing assistants in advanced clusters (18 November 2015). The cluster is a unit of planning and learning, not of finance; the practical consequences are worked out in cluster-fund-raising.

Sacrifice has an upper limit, and the texts set it

The guidance on giving is famous for one half of a sentence and neglectful of the other. The famous half is from 1935, and it is uncompromising:

“There can be no limit to one’s contributions to the national fund. The more one can give the better it is, especially when such offerings necessitate the sacrifice of other wants and desires on the part of the donor. The harder the sacrifice the more meritorious will it be, of course, in the sight of God. For after all it is not so much the quantity of one’s offerings that matters, but rather the measure of deprivation that such offerings entail.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 31 December 1935

The measure of deprivation is the metric, and it explains why an institution can never know who has given sacrificially: the sum is not the sacrifice. It also explains why the Guardian was so moved by a believer who gave the Fund the difference between the price of a cheap coffin and an expensive one (4 May 1941) — a gift whose whole meaning is invisible on a ledger.

The neglected half sets the boundary. You may not borrow to give. “Even though Shoghi Effendi would urge every believer to sacrifice as much as possible for the sake of contributing towards the fund of the National Assembly, yet he would discourage the friends to incur debts for that purpose. We are asked to give what we have, not what we do not possess, especially if such an act causes suffering to others” (4 May 1932). The clause especially if such an act causes suffering to others is the pivot: sacrificial giving that transfers hardship onto dependants is not sacrifice at all, only relocation.

The fullest statement of the limit came in 1993, in reply to a believer who had evidently offered too much. It is the passage most likely to be needed by the person reading this page:

“The House of Justice has never gone to the extreme of telling the friends that now is the time to give their long-range savings to the Fund. It sets forth the needs of the Cause, and calls upon the believers to sacrifice, but the degree and manner of his sacrifice depends on each believer’s own faith and good judgment in light of his responsibilities. Again and again in His Writings Bahá’u’lláh emphasizes the need for moderation in all things.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 1 July 1993 (muhj-1986-2001)

The same letter names the responsibilities that rank ahead of giving — “educating their children, preparing for their old age so that they will not be a burden on others” — retells the story of the man who asked Muḥammad whether to tie up his camel or trust in God (“tie up the camel and trust in God”), and concludes that “one should not consciously place oneself in a position where one would have to beg others for material support.”

And then it makes the move that unlocks the whole question. Ḥuqúqu’lláh is the measure. “In this Dispensation, by the institution of Ḥuqúqu’lláh, Bahá’u’lláh has provided us with a measure. We are all obliged to pay that portion of our savings that is God’s Right. What remains thereafter is ours to use at our own discretion, for the promotion of the work of the Cause and for helping others less fortunate than ourselves.” The believer anxious about how much is enough has an answer that is definite where the law is definite and free where the law is free: pay the nineteen per cent that is not yours; above that line, decide. Bahá’u’lláh’s own rule of proportion stands behind it — “In all matters moderation is desirable. If a thing is carried to excess, it will prove a source of evil” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Words of Paradise, ninth leaf) — and its application to zeal, including financial zeal, is developed in excellence-and-moderation.

Two pastoral corollaries follow, both explicit in the sources. The believer who has nothing to give has not failed: writing to a supporter of the Temple whose means had collapsed, the Guardian’s secretary reordered her priorities for her — “the best way in which you can effectively support the Temple cause is not through material means but by the moral help which is your primary obligation… Material considerations, though essential, are not the most vital by any means” (30 December 1933). And the Ḥuqúq threshold is real: a letter of 23 June 1987 tells a believer who will never accumulate assessable property worth nineteen mithqáls of gold that “you have no obligation to pay Ḥuqúqu’lláh” — then, characteristically, keeps the door open: “this does not mean that you may not contribute to this Fund if you wish to do so out of your love for Bahá’u’lláh and the generosity of your heart.”

What the discipline of limited means has taught the community to build

A system that cannot buy its way forward has to grow other capacities, and it is possible to trace the ones the Bahá’í community has grown.

Universal participation instead of major donors. The Guardian’s standard, in his own handwriting in 1957, was “All, no matter how modest their resources, must participate. Upon the degree of self-sacrifice involved in these individual contributions will directly depend the efficacy and the spiritual influence which these nascent administrative institutions… will exert.” The House of Justice took it as a design principle, listing contribution in 1964 among the things every believer can do — “all can pray, fight their own spiritual battles, and contribute to the Fund” — precisely because “not all believers can give public talks, not all are called upon to serve on administrative institutions.” Even children: “Any Bahá’í can give to the Cause’s Funds, adult or child… Bahá’í children have always given to the Cause, everywhere” (18 August 1949). A community whose budget rests on three donors has not understood the institution, and would also be fragile.

In-kind giving and voluntary service as real financial instruments. The 1985 memorandum treats produce and handicrafts as “a large potential source of regular donations” and instructs Assemblies to arrange their collection and sale; it also names voluntary service as a financial fact, since it “has an effect on the Fund by reducing the cost of carrying out the work of the Faith, and should be undertaken with joy by the friends.” The 29 December 2015 letter still lists “in-kind contributions particularly in certain places” as part of the habit of giving. An hour of a tutor’s time is, in this accounting, a contribution.

Trustworthiness as an income strategy, and transparency about use paired with secrecy about donors. The 1985 memorandum opens with the first: “A primary requisite for all who have responsibility for the care of the funds of the Faith is trustworthiness. This, as Bahá’u’lláh has stressed, is one of the most basic and vital of all human virtues, and its exercise has a direct and profound influence on the willingness of the believers to contribute to the Fund.” Where solicitation is forbidden, integrity is the only marketing there is. The second pair of policies is opposite in direction and constantly confused: Assemblies are told to “take the members of their communities into their confidence and regularly inform them of the uses to which the Fund is put and the projects for which money is needed” (1985), while individual amounts remain among the matters “in relation to which the Assembly must observe strict confidentiality” (2000). Open books, closed ledger. The wider treatment of the underlying virtue is in truthfulness-foundation.

Pioneers who pay their own way. The economics of the teaching work were settled early and on principle, not on cost: “a pioneer should be, or become as soon as possible, financially independent of the Fund in his chosen post, not only to husband the financial resources of the Faith but because it is a Bahá’í principle that everyone should work and support himself and his family whenever possible, and there is no such profession as pioneer or teacher in the Bahá’í Faith as there are professional missionaries and clergymen in other religions” (25 May 1975). The same statement then costs the exceptions honestly — including the believer whose age makes self-support impossible, where an Assembly “would be fully justified in supporting them, but it should realize from the outset the extent of the responsibility it is incurring for an indefinite period into the future.”

Giving folded into family consultation. The most recent development takes the discipline into the household. Writing on Bahá’í family life on 19 March 2025, the House of Justice placed the Fund inside the ordinary business of a family’s finances: wise stewardship “must take into account many considerations, including how money is earned, spent, and saved; how the education and well-being of the children are maintained; how much is to be allocated for the Funds of the Faith or to support community affairs; and how to discharge the obligation of Ḥuqúqu’lláh” — through which the family learns “generosity, responsibility, the difference between needs and wants, and the management of material means.” The difference between needs and wants is the household form of “the elimination of non-essentials.”

Behind all of it lies the reason the House of Justice gave the Counsellors on 29 December 2015 for why any of this matters spiritually rather than merely practically:

“The future civilization envisaged by Bahá’u’lláh is a prosperous one, in which the vast resources of the world will be directed towards humanity’s elevation and regeneration, not its debasement and destruction. The act of contributing to the Fund, then, is imbued with profound meaning: it is a practical way of hastening the advent of that civilization, and a necessary one, for as Bahá’u’lláh Himself has explained, ‘He Who is the Eternal Truth—exalted be His glory—hath made the fulfilment of every undertaking on earth dependent on material means.’” — The Universal House of Justice, 29 December 2015 (framework-action)

The same passage names the three-way relationship that this article has been describing from three angles: “the sacrificial and generous contributions of the individual believer, the collective consciousness promoted by the community of the needs of the Fund, and the careful stewardship of financial resources exercised by the institutions of the Faith can be regarded as expressions of the love that binds these three actors more closely together.” Individual, community, institution — the same three protagonists as everywhere else in the Plan, with money as the medium.

The field in 2026: what is visible from outside

The corpus supplies the principles. It cannot describe what the arrangement currently looks like from outside, and the following is offered strictly as description of the field, current as of August 2026, not as authority for any Bahá’í principle. Public figures will age.

Aggregate accounts are public where the law requires it. Confidentiality attaches to the individual donor, not to the institution’s books. The National Spiritual Assembly of the Bahá’ís of the United Kingdom is registered charity 250851; for the financial year ending 31 March 2025 its filed accounts record total income of £10,728,187 against total expenditure of £10,811,226. A national community spending slightly more than it received in a single year is not remarkable in charity accounting — reserves and timing explain most such gaps — but it is a tidy illustration of the condition this article began with: the ambition runs marginally ahead of the means, in public, in the ordinary way.

Earmarking has become a live operational problem. The Office of the Treasurer of the United States Bahá’í community publishes a description of the funds available to American believers, listing alongside the unrestricted National Bahá’í Fund a set of earmarked options — the Shrine of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Fund, the International Temples Fund, the Bahá’í Development Fund, the Persian Relief Fund, the Humanitarian Relief Fund, and others. Its guidance restates the principle exactly as the Guardian left it — “the priority of unrestricted contributions does not alter the principle of the freedom to earmark” — while reporting that earmarking has risen to levels leaving unrestricted funds insufficient for operations, and reminding believers of “the importance of meeting the National Fund goal with unrestricted contributions.”

That is worth pausing on, because it is the Guardian’s 1950 warning arriving on schedule seventy-six years later:

“It is up to the individual to decide; if he wishes to devote a sum to a specific purpose, he is free to do so; but the friends should recognize the fact that too much labelling of contributions will tie the hands of the Assembly and prevent it from meeting its many obligations in various fields of Bahá’í activity.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 23 June 1950

Nothing about modern giving platforms is neutral here. A page that presents a donor with eight named funds and a general one will, predictably, drain the general one — not because anyone intends it, but because named causes are legible and administration is not. The right to earmark is untouchable. The presentation of that right is a design decision, and it is currently being made by whoever builds the form.

The building programme is live and expanding. The Riḍván 2023 message called for local Houses of Worship in Kanchanpur, Nepal, and Mwinilunga, Zambia, and a national House of Worship in Canada near the long-established National Ḥaẓíratu’l-Quds in Toronto; on 8 April 2025 a national Mashriqu’l-Adhkár for the Philippines was announced in the vicinity of Manila. Construction of the Shrine of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá continues in ‘Akká, with its own dedicated Fund inaugurated on 7 May 2019 and the ‘Akká Visitors’ Centre reported complete in late 2025. Each is a decade-scale commitment financed by voluntary contribution, undertaken by a community that has not been told what it will cost because, in 1987 as now, “it is impossible at this stage to give an accurate estimate.”

Taking a position: what to give, and what to stop doing

For the individual, the sequence follows the sources rather than the intuitions.

Work out Ḥuqúqu’lláh first, because it is not yours. It is a law, not a donation; it takes precedence over Fund contributions and over pilgrimage; debts come off before it is computed; and if you genuinely cannot pay, “God will verily excuse him.” HuququllahComputation does the arithmetic. Then the 1993 letter’s sentence becomes usable: what remains thereafter is ours to use at our own discretion.

Give regularly and small rather than occasionally and large. The Guardian asked for “a continuous flow of contributions” and told Assemblies to press for regular giving “irrespective of whether there is an emergency to be met or not” (29 July 1935). A treasurer can plan against a standing order; nobody can plan against a good year.

Give unrestricted unless you have a specific reason not to. This is the single highest-leverage change available to an ordinary believer, and it costs nothing. Earmark when you are deputizing a named teacher, or responding to a Temple or relief appeal you have thought about. Do not earmark merely because the form offered you a list.

Do not borrow, do not compromise your family’s security, and do not measure yourself against anyone. The sum is not the sacrifice; “the measure of deprivation” is, and it is unobservable by design. A believer who gives ten pounds a month out of very little has, on the text’s own criterion, possibly outgiven the room.

Count service and in-kind gifts honestly, but do not treat them as a discharge. Voluntary service genuinely reduces the cost of the work. It does not remove the believer’s own relationship to the Fund, which the House of Justice describes as “a fundamental aspect of Bahá’í identity.”

For an institution — an Assembly, a Council, a treasurer — the discipline is the harder one.

Plan first, cost second, and re-plan. The iterative sequence in the treasurers’ manual is not bureaucratic; it is the only way to arrive at a budget that expresses priorities rather than habits.

Name what you will not do this year, and say so. The House of Justice cancelled and postponed activities “which, although valuable, cannot be pursued” for several years before it appealed for money, and said as much in public. An Assembly that only ever adds line items is not setting priorities; it is deferring them.

Ask for discretion, not instructions — and then earn it. The Australian policy of budgeting to the National Fund “and not to other national level funds” works only where the receiving institution reports honestly on use. Confidence upward is purchased by transparency downward.

Watch contributions as a symptom, never as a target. Rising Local Fund income is a downstream indicator of a community’s vitality. A community that chases the indicator has inverted the instrument, and will end up applying exactly the pressure the 1926 letter forbids.

And for a reader with technical skill — software, data, finance — the leverage is unusually specific, because almost every tool the sector has built rests on assumptions this system rejects. Donor-management software is organised around segmentation, ask-ladders, lapsed-donor campaigns and lifetime-value modelling; every one of those is a form of the pressure the 10 January 1926 letter puts out of bounds. What Bahá’í institutions need instead is mostly unbuilt: treasury tooling that forecasts income from anonymised history and pledge aggregates without surfacing an individual’s giving to anyone but the treasurer; contribution interfaces whose default is unrestricted and which make earmarking available without making it attractive; budget tools that force the “what will we stop doing” column to be filled before the totals balance; reporting that shows a community what its money did without exposing a single donor. The design constraint is the interesting part: a giving tool whose success metric is that it never nudges.

The final word belongs to the letter that opened the modern era of this question. Writing to the whole Bahá’í world on 18 December 1963, before a single goal of the Nine Year Plan had been won, the House of Justice put the matter in the only terms that make the arithmetic bearable: “Contributing to the Fund is a service that every believer can render, be he poor or wealthy; for this is a spiritual responsibility in which the amount given is not important. It is the degree of the sacrifice of the giver, the love with which he makes his gift, and the unity of all the friends in this service which bring spiritual confirmations.” The means will remain limited. The ordering of priorities is how a community keeps faith with its own ambitions anyway.

Open questions / honest limits

  • No published global figures. The House of Justice “does not issue an accounting of the current expenditure” of Ḥuqúqu’lláh funds (16 February 1998), and no consolidated worldwide account of Bahá’í Fund income or expenditure is published. The figures cited in this page are national or project-level and cannot be aggregated into a picture of the whole. Anyone wanting a total is not going to get one, and should not construct one from parts.
  • The 1973 “sixty percent” figure is fifty-three years old. It described how the international funds were used at the close of the Nine Year Plan. It is quoted here because it is the clearest disclosure of the redistribution mechanism in the corpus, not because it describes 2026. The current proportion is not published.
  • “Priority” language shifts between contexts. The rule that national interests precede local ones (Guardian, restated 1985) governs how institutions rank claims. The freedom to contribute to any fund in any order (25 March 1953) governs individuals. These are not in conflict, but flat quotation of either one out of context produces a false ruling, and this page has tried to keep them apart.
  • Where a Bahá’í-inspired organisation ends and the Fund begins is deliberately unsettled. Development agencies with their own governance may raise funds from governments and foundations — the House of Justice noted in 1992 that five National Assemblies ran women’s-advancement projects with UNIFEM assistance — while the Fund proper remains closed to non-Bahá’í money. A 1993 letter cautions that such organisations should not “unduly divert the contributions of the believers from the essential and primary tasks of supporting the Funds of the Faith,” and declines to answer whether they are “Bahá’í”: “such a question cannot be answered by a simple ‘yes’ or ‘no.’” This page does not settle it either.
  • How institutions actually decide, case by case, is not documented. The corpus supplies criteria — greatest yield, urgency, reach, essentials over non-essentials — but no worked example of a real budget deliberation, and none exists in the published record. The consultative reasoning inside an Assembly is properly confidential, which means the most interesting part of the process cannot be studied.
  • The external figures will age and are partial. The UK charity filing is one national community in one year; the US Office of the Treasurer’s guidance describes one national situation. Neither grounds a Bahá’í principle, and neither should be generalised to the Bahá’í world.
  • This page does not treat the mechanics of computing Ḥuqúqu’lláh (HuququllahComputation), the pastoral handling of unequal contributions (unequal-fund-contribution), Bahá’í wills and bequests (bahai-wills-and-estate-planning), or consultation as a decision-making method (consultation) — each has its own page.
