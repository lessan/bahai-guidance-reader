Justice, Crime and Strikes: Some Answered Questions 76, 77 and 78

Last updated 15 October 2026

What this page covers

Three consecutive chapters near the end of Some Answered Questions ask what we owe a person who has wronged us, what a society owes everyone else, and what an employer and a worker owe each other. Chapter 76, “The Justice and Mercy of God”, defines justice as “rendering to each his due” and sets beside it bounty, the giving of what is not due. Chapter 77, “The Punishment of Criminals”, answers the question “should a criminal be punished, or should he be forgiven and his crime overlooked?” by dividing it: the individual forgives, the community punishes, and both should put their effort into education so that crimes are not committed in the first place. Chapter 78, “Strikes”, diagnoses the labour disputes of the day as the product of greed on both sides and of an economic order that lets “a handful of people” accumulate fortunes while most remain destitute, and proposes laws that fix wages, a share of profits and pensions, with disputes settled by a court whose verdict is enforced. Together the chapters come to about 3,300 words.

The chapters make eight claims:

  1. Justice and bounty are different things. Justice gives what is owed, such as a wage for a day’s work; bounty gives what is not owed, such as alms or forgiveness (76 ¶1).
  2. Reason alone shows that murder, theft and other injuries are wrong, so everyone is accountable for them, whether or not a religion has reached them (76 ¶2–4).
  3. Revenge is wrong and useless. It does not heal the first injury; it adds a second of the same kind. The wronged individual should forgive, and better still return good (76 ¶5; 77 ¶2, ¶5).
  4. The community must punish, not to take revenge but to protect others and deter, without grudge or prejudice. If both the victim and the community forgave, “a murder would be committed at every moment” (76 ¶6; 77 ¶2, ¶6–10). Christ’s command to turn the other cheek concerns private wrongs between individuals, not the state’s duty to protect.
  5. Penal systems that rely on prisons, chains and exile degrade the people they claim to reform. The community’s first duty is education in the broad sense: learning, morals and manners. Crime comes from ignorance and will subside as education spreads (77 ¶3, ¶11–14).
  6. Present-day economic life is unjust. The concentration of wealth beside destitution is “contrary to justice, to humanity, and to fairness” (78 ¶2–4). Absolute equality is equally unworkable (78 ¶5, ¶7).
  7. Law should secure moderation: a daily wage, a share of profits (“a fourth or fifth ... or ... in some other way”), and pensions for the injured, the old and orphans, with mutual rights fixed by law and disputes settled by a fair hearing and an enforced verdict instead of strikes (78 ¶5–11).
  8. Beyond the law, the wealthy should give freely, and giving that is not compelled is “most commendable” (78 ¶12–13).

The setting

Some Answered Questions records answers ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave to Laura Clifford Barney in ‘Akká between 1904 and 1906. The Foreword to the 2014 translation describes these as “the most perilous and dramatic years of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s ministry, when He was confined within the walls of the prison city by the Ottoman authorities, subjected to continual surveillance, and confronted with the constant threat of further exile or of execution.” He corrected the Persian notes twice in His own hand, which gives the book the standing of His own writing, unlike notes of His talks that He did not see.

How the conversations were recorded. Youness Afroukhteh, His secretary, describes the arrangement at the lunch table: “This servant usually sat on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s right, opposite Miss Barney, and translated her questions from English into Persian; then I translated the Master’s replies from Persian into English, while Miss Rosenberg swiftly recorded both.” (Memories of Nine Years in ‘Akká, pp. 315–316). Later Mírzá Munír recorded the replies in Persian. Barney wrote in 1937 that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “not only corrected Mirza Munir’s first draft, but after the corrections were incorporated ‘Abdu’l-Bahá again reviewed the corrected version and signed each corrected subject” (Afroukhteh, pp. 342–344).

Chapters 76–78 belong to Part 5, “Miscellaneous Subjects”. Chapter 77 is the only one of the three that opens with a recorded question; chapter 78 opens “You have asked about strikes”; chapter 76 has no question. Barney’s preface says the talks “were not given in any special order, but have now been roughly classified”, so the order is hers, and the three chapters need not record one conversation. The corpus does not date them individually.

The conversation was at a table with others present. Chapter 77 ¶10 refers to a man in the room, “Siyyid Manshádí here”, whom the book’s note identifies only as “A Bahá’í sitting at table” (note 154), and imagines an armed man entering “the room at this moment”. Ethel Rosenberg, the stenographer at the table, kept her own English notes; one is headed “August 9th — Prisoners and Punishment” and reads in part: “Personally one must always forgive injuries, the government and laws and society must prevent crime.” Her notes are not the corrected text and are not quoted here as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s words, but they show the subject was discussed at the table in August 1904. (Her note also contains statements on prisoners’ treatment that are not in chapter 77; see “What could not be verified”.)

The speaker was a prisoner. On 20 August 1901 Sultan ‘Abdu’l-Ḥamíd had ordered that the restrictions on ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, gradually relaxed over the 1890s, be reimposed; Shoghi Effendi records that “One of His first acts was to intercede on behalf of His brothers”, whose intrigues had brought the order about (God Passes By). A commission of inquiry investigated Him in these years; another arrived in the winter of 1907, and rumours circulated “that He might be cast into the sea, or exiled to Fízán in Tripolitania, or hanged on the gallows”. He was released only after the Young Turk Revolution of 1908, when a telegram to Constantinople confirmed that He was included among “all religious and political prisoners held under the old régime”. ‘Akká itself was the Ottoman penal city to which Bahá’u’lláh had been sent in 1868; chapter 9 of the book recalls that He “promulgated His teachings from within this prison of murderers, thieves, and highwaymen”.

So when chapter 77 ¶11 speaks of states that build “prisons”, acquire “chains and fetters” and ordain “places of exile and banishment”, it describes a system the speaker and his family had lived in for half a century. And when ¶10 says “if someone were to attack, injure, oppress, and wound me, I would in no wise oppose him”, the speaker had recently interceded for the relatives who had worked for his confinement. The household also distributed alms every Friday; God Passes By records that He did this “with His own hands, with a regularity and generosity that won Him the title of ‘Father of the Poor’”, and Afroukhteh adds that He gave most generously to the disabled and sent away the able-bodied so that they would “earn an honest living”.

Barney’s own position. Laura Clifford Barney (1879–1974) was, in the Foreword’s words, “the daughter of a socially prominent family of scholars and artists from Washington, D.C.”; her father, Albert Clifford Barney, was a wealthy industrialist, and in 1904 her mother, Alice Pike Barney, bought the house next to Washington’s first social settlement, Neighborhood House, so that it could expand. Afroukhteh writes that “although she was both wealthy and young, she had an intense affinity for life in her present surroundings”. A question about strikes from a young American heiress is not surprising in years of the largest strikes then known (see “The debate these chapters entered”). Nothing found records why she asked, and the corpus does not record her question in her own words. She later served the League of Nations and the International Council of Women on education and peace.

Chapter 78 reached English readers ten years late. The 1908 English editions (London and Philadelphia) have no chapter on strikes: there chapter 78 is “The Reality of the Exterior World”. “Strikes” first appears in English in the second edition, Chicago, 1918. Barney wrote in 1937 (in Afroukhteh’s back-translation) that “In the second edition of the book two additional subjects were added”, and Afroukhteh’s note identifies one as “Strikes”. The Persian edition in the corpus has it in its present place. The chapter belongs to the same 1904–1906 conversations as the rest; nothing found dates it more closely.

Switzerland. Afroukhteh records that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá would tease Barney: “In the heat of this summer season you should be living in the beautiful mountains of Switzerland in a palace or a mansion. What are you doing in this dilapidated city of ’Akka spending time with us prisoners?” Switzerland is also the country chapter 77 ¶13 names as one where violent crime is rare.

The Persian

The Persian titles are more direct than the English ones:

  • Chapter 76: dar dhikr-i ‘adl va raḥmat-i Ḥaqq, “on the justice and mercy of God” (Ḥaqq, “the True One”). Justice is ’adl; bounty is faḍl. The definition of justice in ¶1 is an Arabic formula, i’ṭá-yi kull dhí ḥaqq ḥaqqahu, “giving every holder of a right his right”.
  • Chapter 77: mujrim mustaḥaqq-i ‘uqúbat ast yá ’afv?, “does the criminal deserve punishment or pardon?” The title is the question.
  • Chapter 78: mas’alih-yi i’tiṣáb, “the question of the strike”.

Five features of the Persian change how the English reads:

  1. “Punishment” in chapter 77 is mostly qiṣáṣ. The contrast in 76 ¶6 and 77 ¶2–6 is between intiqám, private revenge, and qiṣáṣ, requital by the community. Qiṣáṣ is the term of Islamic law for lawful retaliation, above all a life for a life. A listener in 1904 would have heard ‘Abdu’l-Bahá take the standard legal word for retaliation and give it a new purpose: “the protection of the rights of man” (ḥuqúq-i bashar, literally “human rights”) and deterrence (rad’ va man’). Revenge, by contrast, is tashaffí-yi ṣadr, literally “the soothing of the breast”. The English renders qiṣáṣ in several ways (“punishment and requital”, “punish”, “punishment”), so the English reader cannot see that one word runs through the chapter. (The meaning of qiṣáṣ in Islamic law is background knowledge, not checked in a corpus source.)
  2. 77 ¶6 answers a rule of Islamic law. “Were both the victim’s heirs and the community to forgive” renders agar várith-i maqtúl ‘afv kunad va hay’at-i ijtimá’íyyih ‘afv namáyad. In Islamic law the right to retaliation for murder belongs to the victim’s heirs, who may pardon the killer or accept blood-money. The paragraph places the right with the community as well: the heirs’ forgiveness does not end the matter, because the community must protect others. This is a reading of the Persian; the text does not mention blood-money. [Interpretation]
  3. “Imprison or punish” in 77 ¶6 is ḥabs kunad yá qiṣáṣ namáyad. For a murderer, qiṣáṣ meant death. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62 gives the same two options, death or life imprisonment (see “The same themes elsewhere”).
  4. “Peter” and “Paul” in 77 ¶5 are Zayd and ’Amr, the stock names of Arabic grammar and law books, like “A and B”. The 2014 translators substituted names familiar to English readers. The example is a textbook case, not a reference to the apostles.
  5. In 76 ¶4, God’s “mercy” is faḍl, the same word ¶1 defines as bounty. “God will nevertheless deal with him in His mercy” renders khudá bi-faḍl mu’ámilih mikonad, “God deals with him by bounty”. The chapter’s argument is tighter in Persian: ¶1 defines justice and bounty, and ¶4 says God treats the person who never received the law by bounty. Raḥmat (mercy) appears only in the title. Chapter 62 ¶7 uses the same terms: “grace is to bestow without desert, and justice is to give that which is deserved.”

Other details: the body politic is hay’at-i ijtimá’íyyih, “the social body”. Education throughout is tarbiyat, and 77 ¶11 uses the same word for what the penal system claims to do (“seeking thereby to reform the criminal” is literally “so that by these means they may educate the criminals”); the paragraph’s contrast is between two kinds of tarbiyat. “Forsake violent behaviour” (77 ¶11) is az darandigí ijtináb namáyand, from darandih, a beast of prey; the “wolves” of ¶6 are nufús-i darandih mánand-i gurg. “A fourth or fifth of the profits” (78 ¶5) is shirkatí dar rub’ va yá khums-i manáfi’, “a partnership in a quarter or a fifth of the profits”, and “factory” is the French loanword fabrík. “The myriad millions of the poor” (78 ¶4) is hazár milyún, “a thousand million”, close to most of the world’s population in 1905. “Unjustified equality” (78 ¶5) is musávát-i ghayr-i mashrú’, “equality not sanctioned by law”, and the government’s intervention in ¶10 is mudákhilih-yi mashrú’ih, “lawful intervention”: the chapter’s vocabulary is legal throughout. “The lawless tribes of Africa” (77 ¶13) is barábirih-yi Afríqá, “the barbarians of Africa”.

Two readings need checking against the authoritative Persian before they are used: 78 ¶9’s “the laws of justice and compassion” renders qánún-i ’adl va ḥaqqáníyyat, where ḥaqqáníyyat more usually means rightfulness or equity; and 78 ¶11 appears to contain gáhí, “sometimes”, which the English does not show.

Chapter 76: “The Justice and Mercy of God”

The title promises a chapter about God. The text that follows is almost entirely about people: a workman’s wage, alms to a poor man, a blow returned for a blow, and the state’s response to an aggressor. The title’s two terms, justice and mercy, are worked out through those human cases, and the bridge to God is made explicitly only in chapter 77 ¶8.

¶1. Justice and bounty

“KNOW THAT JUSTICE consists in rendering to each his due. For example, when a workman labours from morning till evening, justice requires that he be paid his wage, but bounty consists in rewarding him even when he has done no work and expended no effort.” (¶1)

The paragraph sets up the distinction on which all three chapters turn. Justice gives what is owed. Bounty gives what is not owed. The first example is economic, and it anticipates chapter 78: a wage for a day’s work is a matter of justice, not generosity, so an employer who pays it has done nothing beyond what is required. Alms “to a poor man who has made no effort and done nothing for your benefit to deserve it” is bounty. The last example moves from money to injury: “Christ besought forgiveness for those responsible for His death: This is called bounty.”

That last example matters for everything that follows. Forgiveness of a wrong is classed with alms, as something given beyond what is due, not as part of justice. It is admirable, and it is not something anyone can demand. The paragraph does not say that justice is inferior to bounty; it says they are different things.

The poor man in the alms example is someone who has “done nothing for your benefit”; the phrase describes the giver’s position (the poor man has no claim on him), not the poor man’s character. Chapter 78 makes clear that workers who labour and remain destitute are victims of injustice, not recipients of undeserved bounty.

¶2–3. Reason and religious law

“Now, the question of the excellence or baseness of things is determined either by reason or by religious law.” (¶2)

The chapter then asks how we know what is good and bad at all. One view holds that it is known only from religious law. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives the example of a Jewish dietary rule, “that meat and butter cannot be eaten together, for this is ‘trefah’”, and glosses the Hebrew words for his listener (“’trefah’ in Hebrew means unclean, while ‘kosher’ means clean”). The point of the example is that such a rule is binding because it is commanded, not because reason discovers it. (In Jewish usage treif, from a word for an animal torn by beasts, is the general term for food that is not kosher; the gloss here is a simplification for a Western listener, and the episode need not rely on it.)

“But the divine philosophers hold that the excellence or baseness of things depends upon both reason and religious law. Thus, the prohibitions on murder, theft, treachery, falsehood, hypocrisy, and iniquity are based on reason: Every rational mind can grasp that these are all vile and reprehensible.” (¶3)

The argument for this is brief and physical: “if you merely prick a man with a thorn he will cry out in pain: How well must he realize then that murder, according to reason, is vile and reprehensible.” A person who knows what his own pain is knows what he would be doing to someone else. And so a murderer “would be held accountable for it whether the prophetic message had reached him or not, for reason itself grasps the reprehensible character of this deed.”

This is the ground on which the next chapter’s public punishment rests. The wrongs a state punishes in chapter 77 (the oppressor, the murderer, the assailant) are wrongs that reason knows. Criminal law does not need a shared religion to justify itself.

¶4. What has not been taught

“But if the prophetic injunctions have not reached a place and the people fail, as a result, to act in conformity with the divine teachings, then they are not held accountable according to the laws of religion.” (¶4)

The example is Christ’s teaching “that cruelty should be met with kindness”. Someone who has never heard it and “returns injury for injury” in the ordinary way “is not held accountable according to the laws of religion”, and although he does not deserve divine favour, “God will nevertheless deal with him in His mercy and grant him forgiveness”.

There is a tension with the next paragraph, which says vengeance “is reprehensible even according to reason”. The two can be read together: ¶4 concerns a positive command, to answer cruelty with kindness, which goes beyond anything reason requires; ¶5 concerns the narrower point that retaliation achieves nothing, which reason can see. Someone who has never heard the first may still be expected to see the second. [Interpretation] The text does not reconcile the two paragraphs explicitly.

¶5. Why revenge is pointless

“Now, vengeance is reprehensible even according to reason, for it is of no benefit to the avenger. If a man strikes another, and the victim chooses to exact revenge by returning the blow, what advantage will he gain? Will this be a balm to his wound or a remedy for his pain? No, God forbid! In truth the two actions are the same: Both are injuries; the only difference is that one preceded the other.” (¶5)

The argument is not that revenge is forbidden, though it is; it is that revenge does not do what the avenger hopes. It does not heal the wound. It adds a second injury of the same kind. The paragraph ends with two degrees of response: “if the victim forgives, or better still, if he acts in the opposite manner, this is praiseworthy.” Not retaliating is good; doing good to the aggressor is better.

¶6. The body politic

“As for the body politic, it punishes the aggressor but not to exact revenge. The purpose of this punishment, rather, is to deter and dissuade, and to oppose iniquity and aggression, so as to prevent others from extending their hand likewise in oppression. But if the victim chooses to forgive and to show instead the greatest mercy, this is most approved in the sight of God.” (¶6)

In one paragraph the chapter puts two responses to the same wrong side by side. The community punishes; the victim forgives. Neither cancels the other. The victim’s forgiveness does not stop the state from acting, and the state’s punishment does not make the victim’s forgiveness pointless. Chapter 77 develops exactly this, and adds the reason the two cannot be merged: if both the victim and the community forgave, nobody would be protected.

“Body politic” translates a phrase the book uses for organised society acting through its government and courts. The English phrase has its own history in political philosophy; here it means the public authority, as opposed to the individual who was harmed.

Chapter 77: “The Punishment of Criminals”

Chapter 77 is the only one of the three that opens with a recorded question, and the question is posed as a choice between two answers. The chapter refuses the choice.

¶1–2. Two kinds of retribution

“QUESTION: SHOULD A criminal be punished, or should he be forgiven and his crime overlooked?” (¶1)

“Answer: There are two kinds of retributive actions: One is revenge and retaliation, and the other—punishment and requital. An individual has no right to seek revenge, but the body politic has the right to punish the criminal.” (¶2)

The answer divides the question by who is acting. An individual who returns harm is taking revenge; the community that punishes is doing something of a different kind. The difference lies in purpose and in feeling: punishment “is intended to dissuade and deter others from committing similar crimes. It is for the protection of the rights of man and does not constitute revenge, for revenge is that inner gratification that results from returning like for like.”

The definition of revenge is psychological. It is the satisfaction of having returned the harm. A punishment carried out to feel that satisfaction would be revenge even if a court imposed it. ¶6 says this directly.

The paragraph also gives the reason punishment cannot be dropped: “if criminals were entirely left to their own devices, the order of the world would be disrupted.” And it restates the individual’s duty: “he should show forgiveness and magnanimity, for this is that which befits the human world.”

¶3. Education first

“The body politic, however, must punish the oppressor, the murderer, and the assailant, to dissuade and deter others from committing similar crimes. But that which is essential is to so educate the masses that no crimes will be committed in the first place; for a people can be so educated as to shrink entirely from any crime, and indeed regard the crime itself as the greatest chastisement and the most grievous torment and punishment.” (¶3)

Here the chapter introduces its main proposal, which it returns to at the end (¶11–14). Punishment is necessary; education is “essential”. The aim is a people for whom committing a crime is itself the punishment. This is the “subtle torment” of chapter 75, in which a vice is “a torment in itself” for a person of conscience, applied to a whole society.

¶4. What is practicable

“We must speak only of that which is practically feasible in the world. There is indeed an abundance of lofty ideals and sentiments that cannot be put into effect. Therefore we must confine ourselves to that which is practicable.” (¶4)

This short paragraph follows directly on the most idealistic sentence in the chapter. It can be read as a check on that ideal: until education has done its work, the community must still punish. It also rules out the opposite ideal, the abolition of punishment. Both readings are consistent with the rest of the chapter; the text does not say which it has in mind. [Interpretation]

¶5. Peter and Paul

“If Peter kills the son of Paul, Paul has no right to kill the son of Peter. Were he to do so, it would be an act of vengeance and blameworthy in the extreme. Rather, he must act in the opposite manner and show forgiveness, and, if possible, even be of some assistance to his aggressor.” (¶5)

The example is chosen to be as hard as possible: a murdered son. The paragraph repeats chapter 76 ¶5 almost word for word (“The two actions are indeed one and the same ... The only difference is that one preceded the other”) and adds the strongest demand in either chapter, to be “of some assistance” to the killer, qualified by “if possible”.

In the Persian the two men are Zayd and ’Amr, the stock names of Arabic law books (“A and B”); the English names are the translators’. The paragraph addresses Paul’s own conduct. It says nothing about whether Peter should be tried or punished; ¶6 answers that, and the answer is yes.

¶6. Protection, not grudge

“But the body politic has the right to preserve and to protect. It holds no grudge and harbours no enmity towards the murderer, but chooses to imprison or punish him solely to ensure the protection of others.” (¶6)

The state’s punishment has one stated purpose, protection, and a required state of mind: no grudge, no enmity, no prejudice, no wish “to gratify a sense of vengeance”. The paragraph gives the reason with an image: “were both the victim’s heirs and the community to forgive and return good for evil, the wrongdoers would never cease their onslaught and a murder would be committed at every moment—nay, bloodthirsty individuals would, like wolves, entirely destroy the flock of God.”

“Imprison or punish” (ḥabs ... yá qiṣáṣ) is the only place in the chapter where imprisonment is named as an acceptable measure; for a murderer, qiṣáṣ meant death, so the two options are those of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62. The phrase “both the victim’s heirs and the community” answers the rule of Islamic law under which the heirs alone hold the right to retaliate or pardon (see “The Persian”). ¶11 criticises the prison system as it was; ¶6 does not reject imprisonment as such.

¶7. Turning the other cheek

“Thus when Christ said, ‘Whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the left one also’, the purpose was to educate the people, not to imply that one should assist a wolf that has fallen upon a flock of sheep and is intent upon devouring them all.” (¶7)

The verse (Matthew 5:39) was at the centre of a live argument when these answers were given: Tolstoy had read it as forbidding any use of force, including by courts and armies (see “The debate these chapters entered”). This paragraph reads the verse as moral education of the individual, and adds that Christ “most certainly would have prevented” a wolf from destroying the sheep.

¶8. Justice and forgiveness in God

“Just as forgiveness is one of the attributes of God’s mercy, so is justice one of the attributes of His lordship. The canopy of existence rests upon the pole of justice and not of forgiveness, and the life of mankind depends on justice and not on forgiveness.” (¶8)

This is the paragraph that connects the two chapters to the title of chapter 76. In God there are both mercy and justice; forgiveness belongs to the first, the order of the world to the second. The Persian image is of a tent: khaymih-yi vujúd bar sutún-i ‘adl qá’im, “the tent of existence stands on the pillar of justice”. Bahá’u’lláh uses a related image for the same idea: “the canopy of world order is upraised upon the two pillars of reward and punishment” (Ishráqát). A world run on forgiveness alone would collapse: “if a decree of amnesty were to be enacted henceforth in all countries, the whole world would soon be thrown into disarray”. The historical example is Attila: “if the powers of Europe had not resisted the notorious Attila, he would not have left a single soul alive.”

¶9. Wolves

“Some men are like bloodthirsty wolves: If they were to see no punishment ahead, they would kill others solely for the sake of their own pleasure and diversion.” (¶9)

Two examples follow: “One of the tyrants of Persia killed his tutor for mere amusement”, and the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), who “would summon his ministers, deputies, and trustees to his presence, have a box full of scorpions let loose among them, and, forbidding anyone to move, would burst into boisterous laughter whenever one of them was stung.”

Both examples are rulers. The “wolves” of this paragraph are men with power and no one to restrain them, not an underclass. The paragraph says “some men”, not criminals in general, and its claim is conditional: these men would act so “if they were to see no punishment ahead”. It is an argument for deterrence aimed first at the powerful. [Observation] The scorpions also link this chapter to chapter 74 ¶5, where a scorpion’s venom is the example of something good in itself and evil only in relation to its victim; here the evil is the man who releases them.

¶10. Private and public

“In sum, the proper functioning of the body politic depends upon justice and not forgiveness. So what Christ meant by forgiveness and magnanimity is not that if another nation were to assail you; burn your homes; plunder your possessions; assault your wives, children, and kin; and violate your honour, you must submit to that tyrannical host and permit them to carry out every manner of iniquity and oppression. Rather, the words of Christ refer to private transactions between two individuals, stating that if one person assaults another, the injured party should forgive. But the body politic must safeguard the rights of man.” (¶10)

The paragraph then turns to the people at the table: “if someone were to attack, injure, oppress, and wound me, I would in no wise oppose him but would show forgiveness. But if someone were to attack Siyyid Manshádí here, I would of course prevent him.” The book’s note identifies Siyyid Manshádí only as “A Bahá’í sitting at table” (note 154). The point is that forgiveness is something a person can give for wrongs done to himself. To stand aside while someone else is attacked is not forgiveness: “Although to the assailant non-interference would appear as kindness, it would be sheer oppression towards Manshádí.”

The second illustration is a hypothetical intruder, “a savage Arab ... brandishing a sword”. The ethnic descriptor is in the authorized English text; it describes an imagined attacker in the room in ‘Akká, in the idiom of the time and place, and the speaker himself lived among Arabs. The episode does not need the descriptor to make the point and can paraphrase it as “a man with a sword”. See “Passages that need care”.

The sentence spoken from within the household gives the principle its weight. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá was a prisoner of the Ottoman state at the time (see “The setting”). The man who says “I would in no wise oppose him” had been exiled since childhood and was living under renewed confinement.

¶11. What prisons do

“One final point: The body politic is engaged day and night in devising penal laws and in providing for ways and means of punishment. It builds prisons, acquires chains and fetters, and ordains places of exile and banishment, of torment and hardship, seeking thereby to reform the criminal, whereas in reality this only brings about the degradation of morals and the subversion of character.” (¶11)

This is the chapter’s sharpest criticism, and it is aimed at the community, not the criminal. The penal system claims to reform; in fact it degrades. The alternative is that the body politic “should instead strive night and day, bending every effort to ensure that souls are properly educated, that they progress day by day, that they advance in science and learning, that they acquire praiseworthy virtues and laudable manners, and that they forsake violent behaviour, so that crimes might never occur.”

The paragraph ends with a picture of a state that prepares only for punishment: it is “ever seeking to strengthen penal laws and securing means of punishment, instruments of death and chastisement, and places of imprisonment and exile, and then waiting for crimes to be committed. This has a most detrimental effect.”

In the Persian, “seeking thereby to reform the criminal” says the penal system aims to educate (tarbiyat) criminals by these means, and the next sentence uses the same word for what the community should do instead. The contrast is between two kinds of education. Two further features are easy to miss. First, “prisons ... exile and banishment” were not abstractions to the speaker; ‘Akká was a penal city and he had been sent there as a prisoner. Second, the paragraph does not say that criminals cannot be reformed. It says that chains and exile do not reform them, and that the effort should go into education before the crime.

¶12. If the masses were educated

“But if the masses were educated so that knowledge and learning increased day by day, understanding was broadened, perceptions were refined, morals were rectified and manners reformed—in a word, that progress was made with respect to every degree of perfection—then the occurrence of crime would subside.” (¶12)

The claim is that crime “would subside”, not that it would vanish. “Education” is defined broadly: knowledge, understanding, perception, morals and manners together. Schooling in the narrow sense is one part of it.

¶13. Civilization and crime

“Experience has shown that crime is less prevalent among civilized peoples—that is, among those who have acquired true civilization. And true civilization is divine civilization, the civilization of those who combine material and spiritual perfections. As ignorance is the root cause of crime, the more knowledge and learning advance, the less crime will be committed.” (¶13)

The paragraph defines “civilized” before using it, and the definition is not European: “true civilization is divine civilization”, which combines “material and spiritual perfections”. By this definition no nation of 1904 qualified simply by being wealthy or European. (The same distinction runs through The Secret of Divine Civilization.)

The paragraph then gives a comparison: “Consider the lawless tribes of Africa: How often they kill one another and even consume each other’s flesh and blood! Why do such savageries not take place in Switzerland? The reason, clearly, is education and virtue.” This sentence repeats reports that were common in European writing about Africa at the time, and its picture of Africa cannot be accepted today as a description of African peoples, then or now. Its stated explanation, however, is education and virtue, not race or descent; it is the opposite of the criminal anthropology of the same decade, which held that criminals and “savages” shared an inherited nature (see “The debate these chapters entered”). See “Passages that need care” for how the episode treats it. Chapter 3 ¶3 contains a parallel sentence about “men in the Sudan” with the same argument (that education, not nature, makes the difference), and release 1 of the series dealt with it by ending the narrated passage before it.

¶14. The conclusion

“Therefore, the body politic must seek to prevent crimes from being committed in the first place, rather than devise harsh punishments and penalties.” (¶14)

The chapter that began by asking whether to punish or forgive ends on neither. Its last instruction is to prevention.

Chapter 78: “Strikes”

¶1. Two sources of the trouble

“YOU HAVE ASKED about strikes. Great difficulties have arisen and will continue to arise from this issue. The origin of these difficulties is twofold: One is the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners, and the other is the gratuitous demands, the greed, and the intransigence of the workers. One must therefore seek to address both.” (¶1)

The chapter is addressed to the questioner directly (“You have asked”). It begins by refusing to take a side: greed on both sides, and both must be addressed. The prediction that “great difficulties ... will continue to arise” was made in the years of the largest strikes then known (see “The debate these chapters entered”).

¶2–3. The root cause, and the animals

“Now, the root cause of these difficulties lies in the law of nature that governs present-day civilization, for it results in a handful of people accumulating vast fortunes that far exceed their needs, while the greater number remain naked, destitute, and helpless. This is at once contrary to justice, to humanity, and to fairness; it is the very height of inequity and runs counter to the good-pleasure of the All-Merciful.” (¶2)

Having blamed both sides, the chapter puts the deeper cause in the system: “the law of nature that governs present-day civilization”. Elsewhere in the book, “nature” in human affairs is what education is meant to lift people above: “if man is abandoned to the rule of nature, he sinks even lower than the animal, whereas if he is educated he becomes even as an angel” (ch. 3 ¶3). Present-day civilization, on this reading, has been left to that rule. [Interpretation]

¶3 then turns the expected comparison around. Among animals, “a certain kind of justice and equality prevails”: there is “equality within a shepherd’s flock, or within a herd of deer in the wilderness, or among the songbirds”. The extreme inequality of human society is not natural in the sense of being found in nature; it “is confined to the human race”.

¶4. A country made a colony

“Thus you can observe, on the one hand, a single person who has amassed a fortune, made an entire country his personal colony, acquired immense wealth, and secured an unceasing flow of gains and profits, and, on the other, a hundred thousand helpless souls—weak, powerless, and wanting even a mouthful of bread.” (¶4)

The phrase “made an entire country his personal colony” fits one contemporary case closely: King Leopold II of Belgium, who held the Congo Free State as his personal possession from 1885 to 1908, and whose regime was the subject of the Casement Report in 1904. The text names no one, and the identification is a comparison, not a claim about what the speaker meant. [Inference]

The paragraph ends with the first practical instruction of the chapter: “One must therefore enact such laws and regulations as will moderate the excessive fortunes of the few and meet the basic needs of the myriad millions of the poor, that a degree of moderation may be achieved.” The remedy is law, not appeal.

¶5. Not equality: moderation, and a share of the profits

“However, absolute equality is just as untenable, for complete equality in wealth, power, commerce, agriculture, and industry would result in chaos and disorder, disrupt livelihoods, provoke universal discontent, and undermine the orderly conduct of the affairs of the community.” (¶5)

The chapter rules out the socialist answer as firmly as it ruled out the existing order. Its middle term is “moderation”, defined as “the enactment of such laws and regulations as would prevent the unwarranted concentration of wealth in the hands of the few and satisfy the essential needs of the many.”

The example is a factory, and the proposal is specific:

“Therefore, laws and regulations should be enacted which would grant the workers both a daily wage and a share in a fourth or fifth of the profits of the factory in accordance with its means, or which would have the workers equitably share in some other way in the profits with the owners. For the capital and the management come from the latter and the toil and labour from the former.” (¶5)

Four things should be read carefully:

  1. “A share in a fourth or fifth of the profits”: the workers together share in a fourth or fifth; it is not a figure per worker. (See “Persian” in the setting for the original.)
  2. “In accordance with its means”: the figure depends on what the factory can bear.
  3. “Or ... in some other way”: the fraction is one example of an equitable arrangement, not the only one.
  4. The justification is a division of contributions: capital and management from the owners, “toil and labour” from the workers. Both have a claim on the profit.

The paragraph ends with two alternative arrangements: either a wage that meets daily needs plus a right to a share of the revenues “when they are injured, incapacitated, or unable to work”, or a wage high enough to “satisfy their daily needs and save a little for times of weakness and incapacity”.

¶6. Wealth as a burden

“If matters were so arranged, neither would the factory owners amass each day a fortune which is absolutely of no use to them—for should one’s fortune increase beyond measure, one would come under a most heavy burden, become subject to exceeding hardships and troubles, and find the administration of such an excessive fortune to be most difficult and to exhaust one’s natural powers—nor would the workers endure such toil and hardship as to become incapacitated and to fall victim, at the end of their lives, to the direst need.” (¶6)

The argument here is addressed to the owners’ own interest. A fortune beyond use is a burden to manage. This is a reason the owners might accept moderation, not the reason for it; the reason is justice (¶2).

¶7–8. Profits, pensions and orphans

¶7 restates the conclusion: concentration “is unfair and unjust”, “absolute equality would also disrupt” the life of society, so “the best course is therefore to seek moderation”. It asks the wealthy “to recognize the advantages of moderation in the acquisition of profits”, and repeats the practical form: “to fix a daily wage for the workers and also to allot them a share of the total profits of the factory”.

“In brief, insofar as the mutual rights of the factory owners and the workers are concerned, laws must be enacted that would enable the former to make reasonable profits and the latter to be provided with their present necessities and their future needs, so that if they become incapacitated, grow old, or die and leave behind small children, they or their children will not be overcome by dire poverty but will receive a modest pension from the revenues of the factory itself.” (¶8)

The paragraph is a list of what a modern reader would call disability insurance, an old-age pension and a survivors’ pension, funded “from the revenues of the factory itself”. Owners are entitled to “reasonable profits”; the word “rights” is used of both sides.

¶9. No strikes, and a court with power

“For their part, the workers should not make excessive demands, be recalcitrant, ask for more than they deserve, or go on strike. They should obey and comply and make no demands for exorbitant wages. Rather, the mutual and equitable rights of both parties should be officially fixed and established according to the laws of justice and compassion, and any party that violates them should be condemned after a fair hearing and be subject to a definitive verdict enforced by the executive branch, so that all affairs may be appropriately ordered and all problems adequately resolved.” (¶9)

This is the paragraph that most needs to be read whole. Its first two sentences tell workers not to strike. Its third sentence says what replaces the strike: rights “officially fixed” in law, a “fair hearing”, a “definitive verdict” and enforcement by “the executive branch” against “any party” that violates the rights, owners included. The instruction not to strike assumes that this machinery exists. The paragraph describes a system of compulsory adjudication of industrial disputes, of the kind New Zealand had legislated in 1894 and Australia in 1904 (see “The debate these chapters entered”).

The paragraph does not say what workers should do where no such court exists, or where it is captured by one side. Later guidance addresses the believer’s position on lawful strikes (see “The same themes elsewhere”).

¶10–11. Why the government may intervene

“The intervention of the government and the courts in the problems arising between owners and workers is fully warranted, since these are not such particular matters as are ordinary transactions between two individuals, which do not concern the public and in which the government should have no right to interfere.” (¶10)

This is the same private/public distinction as chapter 77 ¶10, applied to the economy. A dispute between owner and workers “may appear to be a private matter”, but “the commercial, industrial, and agricultural affairs, and even the general business of the nation, are all intimately linked together: An impairment to one is a loss to all.” ¶11 adds that even a private dispute between two people needs a third party to settle it; “How, then, can the problem of strikes, which entirely disrupt the country ... remain neglected?”

¶12–13. What the law cannot do

“Gracious God! How can one see one’s fellow men hungry, destitute, and deprived, and yet live in peace and comfort in one’s splendid mansion? How can one see others in the greatest need and yet take delight in one’s fortune?” (¶12)

After eleven paragraphs on law, the chapter ends on the heart. Religions have “decreed ... that the wealthy should offer up each year a portion of their wealth for the sustenance of the poor”. The final sentence draws the distinction the chapter has been working toward: “since in this regard one is not outwardly compelled or obliged by the government, but rather aids the poor at the prompting of one’s own heart and in a spirit of joy and radiance, such a deed is most commendable, approved, and pleasing.” ¶13: “This is the meaning of the righteous deeds mentioned in the heavenly Books and Scriptures.”

Voluntary giving is not offered as a replacement for the laws of ¶4–9. It is what remains for the individual once the law has done its part, in the same way that forgiveness remains for the victim once the state has done its part (ch. 77 ¶2, ¶6).

How the three chapters fit together

One distinction runs through all three chapters: what is owed, and what is given beyond it. Chapter 76 ¶1 defines justice as rendering each his due and bounty as giving what is not due, and gives one economic example (the wage) and one personal example (Christ’s forgiveness). The two later chapters apply the distinction to crime and to labour:

Justice: what is owed, and can be requiredBounty: what is given beyond it, and cannot be required
A day’s work (76 ¶1)the wagea reward without work; alms
A wrong done to me (76 ¶5–6; 77)the community’s punishment, for protectionmy forgiveness; help to the aggressor “if possible”
A factory (78)by law: a wage, a share of profits, pensions, an enforced verdictfree giving by the wealthy “at the prompting of one’s own heart”

The second distinction is between private and public. Christ’s words “refer to private transactions between two individuals” (77 ¶10), and a person may forgive what is done to himself. But “the body politic must safeguard the rights of man”, and standing aside while another is attacked “would be sheer oppression”. Chapter 78 ¶10 applies the same line to the economy: disputes between owners and workers “are not such particular matters as are ordinary transactions between two individuals”, because “an impairment to one is a loss to all”, so “the government and the courts have therefore the right to intervene”.

The two distinctions line up. The individual’s part is bounty: forgiveness of wrongs to oneself, generosity with one’s wealth. The community’s part is justice: protection, punishment without grudge, laws on wages and profits, courts. Neither part replaces the other. A victim’s forgiveness does not stop the trial (77 ¶6); voluntary giving does not replace the laws of 78 ¶4–9; and the law does not make forgiveness or generosity unnecessary.

The third thread is education. Chapter 77 puts its main hope not in punishment but in education (¶3, ¶11–14), and chapter 78 begins its diagnosis with a “law of nature” governing present civilization (¶2), the condition that in chapter 3 education is meant to lift humanity above. Chapter 75, just before these, had said that for a person of conscience a vice is “a torment in itself”; chapter 77 ¶3 hopes for a society in which people “regard the crime itself as the greatest chastisement”.

What the chapters do not say. They give no sentencing tariff, no rules of evidence, no account of how to detect a biased court, and no method for deciding who is dangerous. They give no definition of an “excessive” fortune, no schedule of wages, and no instruction for workers where the fair tribunal of 78 ¶9 does not exist. Later guidance says these details are left to institutions and to experts: “Bahá’u’lláh has given us the general moral and social principles to guide our lives, but their application to the rehabilitation of criminals is left to the experts in that field to develop in the same way that economics is left to economists” (letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 3 September 1974). The House of Justice wrote in 2017: “Although Bahá’u’lláh does not set out in His Revelation a detailed economic system, a constant theme throughout the entire corpus of His teachings is the reorganization of human society.”

The same themes elsewhere in the Writings

Bahá’u’lláh

Justice. “O SON OF SPIRIT! The best beloved of all things in My sight is Justice; turn not away therefrom if thou desirest Me, and neglect it not that I may confide in thee. By its aid thou shalt see with thine own eyes and not through the eyes of others, and shalt know of thine own knowledge and not through the knowledge of thy neighbor.” (Hidden Words, Arabic 2). “The light of men is Justice. Quench it not with the contrary winds of oppression and tyranny. The purpose of justice is the appearance of unity among men.” (Words of Paradise, sixth leaf).

The same definition of justice as chapter 76 ¶1. In a Tablet answering questions of the Zoroastrian Mánikchí Ṣáḥib, Bahá’u’lláh writes: “Justice, which consisteth in rendering each his due, dependeth upon and is conditioned by two words: reward and punishment. From the standpoint of justice, every soul should receive the reward of his actions, inasmuch as the peace and prosperity of the world depend thereon” (The Tabernacle of Unity 2.37). Whether the Persian formula is the same as 76 ¶1’s could not be checked; the original is not in the corpus.

Reward and punishment as pillars. “The structure of world stability and order hath been reared upon, and will continue to be sustained by, the twin pillars of reward and punishment.” (Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd; Gleanings CXII). The Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd continues: “Justice hath a mighty force at its command. It is none other than reward and punishment for the deeds of men. By the power of this force the tabernacle of order is established throughout the world, causing the wicked to restrain their natures for fear of punishment.” This is the deterrence argument of 77 ¶6 and ¶9. The Ishráqát (eighth Ishráq) adds that “That which traineth the world is Justice, for it is upheld by two pillars, reward and punishment”, and refers the details to the House of Justice: “Inasmuch as for each day there is a new problem and for every problem an expedient solution, such affairs should be referred to the House of Justice”. The verb is “traineth”, the same idea as chapter 77’s tarbiyat.

Penalties in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas. “Should anyone intentionally destroy a house by fire, him also shall ye burn; should anyone deliberately take another’s life, him also shall ye put to death. ... Should ye condemn the arsonist and the murderer to life imprisonment, it would be permissible according to the provisions of the Book.” (¶62). The notes, by the Universal House of Justice, say that the law is “designed for a future state of society”, and that “degrees of offense, whether extenuating circumstances are to be taken into account, and which of the two prescribed punishments is to be the norm are left to the Universal House of Justice to decide in light of prevailing conditions when the law is to be in operation” (note 86). They also summarise chapter 77: “In His Tablets ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá explains the difference between revenge and punishment. He affirms that individuals do not have the right to take revenge, that revenge is despised in the eyes of God, and that the motive for punishment is not vengeance, but the imposition of a penalty for the committed offense.” For theft, ¶45 decrees “exile and imprisonment” and warns “Beware lest, through compassion, ye neglect to carry out the statutes of the religion of God”, describing the law as education: “We school you with the rod of wisdom and laws, like unto the father who educateth his son, and this for naught but the protection of your own selves”. The Universal House of Justice wrote in 1980 that these punishments “are intended for a future condition of society and will have to be supplemented and applied by the Universal House of Justice” (to the National Spiritual Assembly of Germany, 7 May 1980), and in 1974 that “the development of the Administrative Order has not progressed to the point where Bahá’í institutions enforce criminal laws” (7 May 1974).

His own forgiveness and the law. Shoghi Effendi quotes Bahá’u’lláh in The Advent of Divine Justice: “God is My witness! were it not contrary to the Law of God, I would have kissed the hand of My would-be murderer, and would cause him to inherit My earthly goods. I am restrained, however, by the binding Law laid down in the Book”. This is the closest parallel in the Writings to 77 ¶10: the wronged person’s wish is to forgive; the law, which protects others, prevails.

The rich and the poor. “O YE RICH ONES ON EARTH! The poor in your midst are My trust; guard ye My trust, and be not intent only on your own ease.” (Hidden Words, Persian 54). “Tell the rich of the midnight sighing of the poor” (Persian 49). “It is unlawful to beg, and it is forbidden to give to him who beggeth. All have been enjoined to earn a living, and as for those who are incapable of doing so, it is incumbent on the Deputies of God and on the wealthy to make adequate provision for them.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶147). “It is enjoined upon every one of you to engage in some form of occupation, such as crafts, trades and the like. We have graciously exalted your engagement in such work to the rank of worship unto God” (¶33). The laws of Zakát (¶146) and Ḥuqúqu’lláh (¶97) are the Bahá’í laws closest to 78 ¶12’s yearly giving, though chapter 78 names neither. “In all matters moderation is desirable. If a thing is carried to excess, it will prove a source of evil.” (Words of Paradise, ninth leaf). “Blessed is the ruler who succoureth the captive, and the rich one who careth for the poor, and the just one who secureth from the wrong doer the rights of the downtrodden” (Words of Paradise).

Education as a public duty. “Regard man as a mine rich in gems of inestimable value. Education can, alone, cause it to reveal its treasures” (Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd). Every father must have his children taught to read and write; if he cannot, “the matter devolveth upon the House of Justice. Verily, have We made it a shelter for the poor and needy.” (Ishráqát, seventh Ishráq).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: other chapters of Some Answered Questions

  • Chapter 7 ¶7–8 makes the private/public distinction of 77 ¶10 about Muḥammad: “If this oppression had been directed towards Him alone, He would have forgiven them ... but had He seen that cruel and bloodthirsty murderers were intent upon killing, pillaging, and tormenting a number of defenceless souls and taking captive the women and children, it is certain that He would have defended the oppressed and stayed the hand of the oppressors.” And: Christ in the same circumstances “would have undoubtedly delivered ... those men, women, and children from the claws of such ravenous wolves.” There, restraining the aggressor is called “pure bounty”.
  • Chapter 11 ¶10 places penalties in the changeable part of religion: “penalties and punishments for murder, assault, theft, and injury” are “changed and altered in every prophetic Dispensation ... for policies, transactions, punishments, and other laws are bound to change according to the exigencies of the time.” The unchanging part (¶7) includes “justice”, “mercy to the poor, assistance to the oppressed, generosity to the needy, and upliftment of the fallen.”
  • Chapter 57 ¶8–9: “Through education barbarous nations become civilized”; “It is not on account of their innate capacity and disposition that one reproaches the wicked, but rather on account of that which they themselves have acquired.” (Discussed in this series’ episode on evil.)

  • Chapter 62 ¶7: “grace is to bestow without desert, and justice is to give that which is deserved”, the definitions of 76 ¶1 applied to the soul after death.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: other writings

Selections 105 is His own written version of 77 ¶3 and ¶11–14, and the most useful single text for this episode: “material civilization, through the power of punitive and retaliatory laws, restraineth the people from criminal acts; and notwithstanding this, while laws to retaliate against and punish a man are continually proliferating, as ye can see, no laws exist to reward him. In all the cities of Europe and America, vast buildings have been erected to serve as jails for the criminals. Divine civilization, however, so traineth every member of society that no one, with the exception of a negligible few, will undertake to commit a crime. There is thus a great difference between the prevention of crime through measures that are violent and retaliatory, and so training the people, and enlightening them, and spiritualizing them, that without any fear of punishment or vengeance to come, they will shun all criminal acts. They will, indeed, look upon the very commission of a crime as a great disgrace and in itself the harshest of punishments.” It adds two points to chapter 77: that society has laws to punish but none “to reward”, and that the exception is “a negligible few”, not none.

Selections 83, to someone who had worked with prisoners: “Kindness, training and education extended to prisoners is exceedingly important. ... Convey on my behalf to the two prisoners in San Quentin the utmost kindness, and tell them: ‘That prison in the sight of wise souls is a school of training and development.’” Chapter 77 ¶11 condemns what penal systems do to people; this Tablet says what a prisoner can still make of prison.

Selections 79, to Andrew Carnegie, who had sent Him The Gospel of Wealth: “the Teachings of Bahá’u’lláh advocate voluntary sharing, and this is a greater thing than the equalization of wealth. For equalization must be imposed from without, while sharing is a matter of free choice.” And: “the rich should extend assistance to the poor ... but of their own free will, and not because the poor have gained this end by force. For the harvest of force is turmoil and the ruin of the social order.” The First Tablet to The Hague (1919) says the same of “voluntary sharing”, that it “should not be introduced by coercion so that it becomes a law”, and that the community must pay for a child’s education when parents cannot. These texts reject compulsory equalization, as 78 ¶5 rejects “absolute equality”; they do not reject the laws on wages, profit shares and pensions that chapter 78 requires. [Interpretation] The connecting idea is that law sets a floor and limits extremes; sharing beyond that is voluntary.

The Secret of Divine Civilization (1875), written for Persian readers, asks whether it would be ruinous if governors’ “sentences involving capital punishment, imprisonment and the like were contingent on confirmation by the Sháh and by higher courts in the capital, who would first duly investigate the case and determine the nature and seriousness of the crime”, and if “bribery and corruption, known today by the pleasant names of gifts and favors, were forever excluded”. It cites a governor who “without a trial, and without obtaining any higher sanction” beheaded thirteen innocent men in an hour. It traces “oppression and injustice” to the people’s “lack of religious faith and the fact that they are uneducated”, the thesis of 77 ¶13. On wealth: “Wealth is most commendable, provided the entire population is wealthy. If, however, a few have inordinate riches while the rest are impoverished, and no fruit or benefit accrues from that wealth, then it is only a liability to its possessor”, the argument of 78 ¶6.

Shoghi Effendi

Among the principles ‘Abdu’l-Bahá proclaimed in the West, Shoghi Effendi lists “the abolition of the extremes of wealth and poverty” and “the glorification of justice as the ruling principle in human society” (God Passes By). He describes a future world community in which “the interdependence of Capital and Labor” is “definitely recognized” and “Destitution on the one hand, and gross accumulation of ownership on the other, will disappear” (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh).

  • Strikes (letter on its behalf, 23 June 1985, Lights of Guidance no. 2121): “‘Abdu’l-Bahá in general disapproved of strikes. The Bahá’í attitude is that when the law recognizes strikes as legal, as when called by a properly constituted authority such as a trade union, the Bahá’í teaching neither requires nor forbids an individual to participate in the strike but leaves him free to decide for himself what is the proper course of action in the particular circumstances.” The Guardian and the House of Justice read 78 ¶9 as a statement of disapproval and of the goal (rights fixed by law, enforced by courts), not as a rule binding the individual where strikes are lawful.
  • Criminal justice (letter on its behalf, 3 September 1974): principles are given; “their application to the rehabilitation of criminals is left to the experts in that field to develop in the same way that economics is left to economists.”
  • Forgiveness does not extend to leaving others at risk. On abuse in families (letter on its behalf, 24 January 1993): “the Bahá’í institutions must be uncompromising and vigilant in their commitment to the protection of the children entrusted to their care”, and a parent aware of abuse “should not remain silent, but must take all necessary measures, with the assistance of the Spiritual Assembly or civil authorities if necessary”. A wife who suffers abuse “has the right to turn to the Spiritual Assembly for assistance and counsel, or to seek legal protection.” This is 77 ¶10 applied: one may forgive what is done to oneself, not leave another person to the wrongdoer.
  • Economic life (1 March 2017, to the Bahá’ís of the World): “There is no justification for continuing to perpetuate structures, rules, and systems that manifestly fail to serve the interests of all peoples. The teachings of the Faith leave no room for doubt: there is an inherent moral dimension to the generation, distribution, and utilization of wealth and resources.” “Every choice a Bahá’í makes—as employee or employer, producer or consumer, borrower or lender, benefactor or beneficiary—leaves a trace”. “The extremes of wealth and poverty in the world are becoming ever more untenable.”
  • 78 ¶4 applied to the global economy (letter on its behalf, 27 November 2001): it quotes the passage on the man who amassed treasures “by colonizing a country for his profit” and says the benefits of globalization “demand of government and civil society comparable efforts to ensure a fair distribution of its benefits to the whole of humankind.”
  • Neither extreme (letters on its behalf, 13 November 1985 and 31 July 2002): of two extremes of economic theory, “neither extreme is workable”; criticism of current practice “should not be misconstrued as simply a denunciation of capitalism, nor should it be taken as an endorsement of socialism.”
  • The Promise of World Peace (October 1985): “The inordinate disparity between rich and poor, a source of acute suffering, keeps the world in a state of instability, virtually on the brink of war.”

The debate these chapters entered

None of the three chapters names a contemporary writer, event or law, and nothing in the corpus shows that Barney or ‘Abdu’l-Bahá had any of the following in mind. They are the arguments a well-read questioner in 1904–1906 would have known. Quotations are from period texts saved in `table-talks/research/texts/`.

What is punishment for? (76 ¶5–6; 77 ¶2, ¶6)

The penal reformers of the late nineteenth century had already turned against revenge. The National Prison Congress at Cincinnati in 1870 adopted a Declaration of Principles that shaped American and British reform for forty years: “The treatment of criminals by society is for the protection of society. ... Hence the supreme aim of prison discipline is the reformation of criminals, not the infliction of vindictive suffering.” Chapter 77 agrees on protection and on the absence of vindictiveness, but names deterrence of others as the first purpose of punishment where Cincinnati named reformation.

The argument was older. Beccaria (1764): “The object, therefore, of punishment is simply to prevent the criminal from injuring anew his fellow-citizens, and to deter others from committing similar injuries”, and “It is better to prevent crimes than to punish them”, and “the surest but most difficult means of preventing crimes is to improve education”. Chapter 77 follows the same order of argument (deterrence, then prevention, then education). This was the common inheritance of European penal reform; there is no suggestion of a direct source. [Observation]

Kropotkin, who had been imprisoned in Russia and at Clairvaux in France, answered that neither deterrence nor reform worked: “Figures tell us loudly enough that the supposed double influence of prisons — the deterring and the moralizing — exist only in the imagination of lawyers.” (In Russian and French Prisons, 1887).

Do prisons reform people or corrupt them? (77 ¶11)

Howard had written in 1777 that confinement “doth notoriously promote and increase the very vices it was designed to suppress”. Kropotkin in 1887: “prisons have remained nurseries of criminal education”, and “Prisons do not moralize their inmates; they do not deter them from crime.” (The phrase “universities of crime”, often attributed to him, does not occur in that book.) Oscar Wilde, after two years in prison, wrote in The Ballad of Reading Gaol (1898): “The vilest deeds like poison weeds / Bloom well in prison-air: / It is only what is good in Man / That wastes and withers there”. In Britain a departmental committee chaired by Herbert Gladstone (1895) led to reformation being added to deterrence as an aim of prisons and to a reduction in unproductive labour such as the treadmill; the Prison Act 1898 followed.

Chapter 77 ¶11 makes the same observation as these critics: the penal apparatus claims to reform and in fact degrades. Its remedy differs from theirs. The reformers wanted better prisons; Kropotkin and Tolstoy wanted none; chapter 77 keeps imprisonment for protection (¶6) and moves the main effort to education before the crime (¶11–14).

Is the criminal born or made? (77 ¶11–13)

The most influential criminologist of the period, Cesare Lombroso, held at his most extreme that some people are “born criminals, doomed even before birth to a career of crime, whom all educational efforts fail to redeem”. His daughter’s 1911 summary of his work states: “Contrary to general belief, the influence of education on crime is very slight.” Chapter 77 ¶13 says the opposite: “As ignorance is the root cause of crime, the more knowledge and learning advance, the less crime will be committed.”

Lombroso’s critics in these years said much the same as the chapter. Kropotkin blamed “the abominable conditions under which thousands and thousands of children grow up”. In Resurrection (1899) Tolstoy’s hero reads “Lombroso, Garofalo, Ferry, List, Maudsley, Tard” and finds in the prisoners “no trace of the criminal type written about by the Italian school”; two thieves were “richly endowed by nature, but had been neglected and crippled like uncared-for plants.” Institutions followed the “made” view: the first juvenile court, in Illinois, in 1899; the Borstal sentence for young offenders in England in 1908.

Lombroso’s own books compared criminals with “savages”, and the comparison between “savage” and “civilized” peoples was the ordinary vocabulary of European criminology. Chapter 77 ¶13 uses that vocabulary in its example about Africa. Its argument runs against Lombroso: it locates the difference in “education and virtue”, not in inherited nature. The example remains the prejudice of its period. [Interpretation]

Should a Christian state punish at all? (77 ¶7–10)

The most famous reading of “Resist not evil” (Matthew 5:39) in these years was Tolstoy’s. The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894) argued that the verse forbids force by the state as well as by the individual: courts, prisons and armies included. Tolstoy answered in advance the case chapter 77 ¶10 puts: “I see that a man I know to be a ruffian is pursuing a young girl. I have a gun in my hand—I kill the ruffian and save the girl. But the death or the wounding of the ruffian has positively taken place, while what would have happened if this had not been I cannot know.” Resurrection ends with its hero concluding “that we should forgive always an infinite number of times because there are no men who have not sinned themselves, and therefore none can punish or correct others”, and with the objection he has learned to dismiss: “What is one to do with the evil doers? Surely not let them go unpunished?”

That objection is close to the question recorded at 77 ¶1: “Should a criminal be punished, or should he be forgiven and his crime overlooked?” Chapter 77 gives the answer Tolstoy rejected: forgive what is done to you; the community must protect others, and Christ “most certainly would have prevented” the wolf. Nothing found shows that Barney had read Tolstoy; the episode can say that this was among the most argued religious questions of the decade. [Inference]

The death penalty

In France, Presidents Loubet and Fallières pardoned nearly all those condemned to death between 1898 and 1907, and in 1908 the government asked the Chamber to abolish the death penalty. On 8 December 1908 the Chamber voted 330 to 201 to keep it. Lombroso favoured it for those who repeatedly attempted murder; Kropotkin opposed it. Chapter 77 does not address it directly; its ¶11 counts “instruments of death” among the means a state accumulates “and then waiting for crimes to be committed”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá said in San Francisco in 1912 (talk notes) that capital punishment “is now under discussion by wise men who are debating its advisability.”

Strikes, and who should settle them (78 ¶1, ¶9–11)

The years around these conversations saw the largest strikes then known. In the American anthracite coal strike of 1902, “nearly the entire body of mine workers, which numbers about 147,000, abandoned their employment” from May to October, until President Roosevelt appointed a commission (Report, 1903). In Britain the House of Lords’ Taff Vale judgment of 1901 made a union liable for an employer’s losses from a strike; the Trade Disputes Act 1906 reversed it. In Russia a railway strike in October 1905 became a general strike and forced the Tsar’s October Manifesto. In France more than 60,000 miners struck after the Courrières mine disaster of 10 March 1906, which killed 1,099, and the state sent troops.

There were four answers to the question of who should settle such disputes. [The grouping is this page’s.]

  1. A court with compulsory powers. New Zealand’s Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act of 1894 sent disputes to a state court whose award was binding. Its author, William Pember Reeves, wrote in 1900 that it had “substituted orderly and methodical hearing and adjudication by impartial state tribunals for the loose, violent and haphazard methods of the strike and the lockout”. The American reformer Henry Demarest Lloyd called New Zealand A Country Without Strikes (1900). Australia’s Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act of 15 December 1904 gave as its first object “To prevent lock-outs and strikes in relation to industrial disputes” and made striking over such a dispute an offence (“Penalty: One thousand pounds”). This is the nearest period model to 78 ¶9: rights fixed by law, a hearing, a binding award enforced by the state, and no strike. It was then a proposal of labour reformers, not of conservatives. [Comparison; the chapter names no country.]
  2. Investigation and publicity without compulsion. The 1903 Anthracite Commission refused to recommend compulsory arbitration as “any such drastic measure” and proposed instead “the compulsory investigation of controversies”. Charles Francis Adams, in a paper appended to its report, made 78 ¶11’s argument that “the public being a third party to the controversy” must act, but conceded his board “can compel obedience on neither side”. Chapter 78 goes further, to “a definitive verdict enforced by the executive branch”.
  3. The Church’s answer. Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum (1891) said strikes are “extremely injurious to trade and to the general interests of the public” and that “The laws should forestall and prevent such troubles from arising”; wages “ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner”. It preferred “societies or boards” to state interference.
  4. Leave it to the parties, with the unions protected (Britain after 1906): neither compulsory arbitration nor a ban on strikes.

The New Zealand system came under strain soon after: at Blackball in 1908, miners sacked for taking more than fifteen minutes for a meal break struck in defiance of the arbitration system, and “a country without strikes” was no longer true.

Profit sharing and pensions (78 ¶5–8)

Profit sharing was a reform with a long record by 1904. John Stuart Mill described the Paris house-painting firm of Edme-Jean Leclaire, where workers were paid “by fixed wages or salaries” and “the surplus profits are divided among the body”, after interest on capital and “a fixed allowance for his labour and responsibility as manager” (Principles of Political Economy, IV.vii). That is the structure of 78 ¶5: a wage, then a share of profit, with capital and management rewarded separately. By 1889 Leclaire’s firm divided net profit into a quarter for the managing partners, a quarter for the workers’ mutual aid society and a half among the workmen. The society paid pensions: at fifty, after twenty years, “a retiring pension of 1,200 francs per annum”, half of it continued “to the widow during her life, or to the children” while young, and “Even the workman who is not a member of the society ... receives a pension if disabled while in the service of the house.” These are the three cases of 78 ¶8 (incapacity, old age, dependants), paid from the firm’s own profits. Leclaire told his workmen: “Pensions will put an end to the hostility of master and man”. Jean-Baptiste Godin’s Familistère at Guise shared profits from 1877 and set out to transfer ownership to the workers.

Profit sharing could also be used against unions. In November 1889 George Livesey offered profit sharing at the South Metropolitan Gas Company in London, months after the gas workers had unionised; the union demanded its abolition, over 2,000 stokers struck in December 1889, and the company replaced them. The British Board of Trade published a report on profit sharing in 1894.

Pensions by law were new. Germany introduced compulsory old-age and invalidity insurance in 1889, New Zealand old-age pensions from taxation in 1898, Britain in 1908 (“The first old age pensions were paid to 490,000 people on 1 January 1909”). Chapter 78 ¶8 puts the pension on “the revenues of the factory itself”, like Leclaire, not on the state.

Concentrated wealth and giving (78 ¶2–7, ¶12)

Andrew Carnegie’s essay “Wealth” (1889) argued that “The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth”, that the rich man should be “but a trustee for the poor”, and that “The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced”; he welcomed heavy death duties. But he held that “Neither the individual nor the race is improved by almsgiving”, and preferred libraries and parks. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá later wrote to Carnegie in reply to The Gospel of Wealth (see “The same themes elsewhere”). Rerum Novarum held that “unequal fortune is a necessary result of unequal condition”, and that giving from one’s surplus is “a duty, not of justice (save in extreme cases), but of Christian charity -a duty not enforced by human law”. Chapter 78 shares the rejection of absolute equality (¶5) and the voluntary character of giving (¶12), and differs in asking for laws that limit “excessive fortunes” and fix profit shares (¶4–5).

Ida Tarbell’s History of the Standard Oil Company appeared in 1904. The best-known case of a man who had “made an entire country his personal colony” (78 ¶4) was Leopold II of the Belgians, who held the Congo Free State as his personal possession from 1885; Roger Casement’s report on its abuses was laid before Parliament in February 1904, and Belgium took the territory from him in 1908. The Persian is general (“made a region his own colony”), and the chapter names no one. [Comparison, not identification.]

Objections a judge would raise

Each item gives the objection as an experienced criminal judge might put it, what the texts say, and what is left open. The replies are drawn from the texts and, where stated, from outside sources. They are not the only possible ones. Outside sources describe the field only.

1. “You want Paul to help the man who killed his son?”

The objection. “I have sat through victim personal statements from parents of murdered children. If anyone had stood up in my court and told the mother that she should forgive the killer and ‘if possible, even be of some assistance to’ him (77 ¶5), I would have stopped them. Victims are already told they are bitter, that they should move on. This text adds a religious duty to that pressure.”

What the texts say. The duty in 77 ¶5 is placed on the conscience of the wronged person, not on the state, and it is set against one specific act: “If Peter kills the son of Paul, Paul has no right to kill the son of Peter.” The example forbids blood feud against the offender’s kin, which every modern legal system also forbids. The same chapter keeps the public response intact: “the body politic has the right to punish the criminal” (77 ¶2); “the body politic must safeguard the rights of man” (77 ¶10). Forgiveness is described as what is “praiseworthy” (76 ¶5) and “most approved in the sight of God” (76 ¶6), and the help to the aggressor is qualified “if possible”. Chapter 77 ¶7 sets a limit: Christ’s teaching does not mean “that one should assist a wolf that has fallen upon a flock of sheep”. In ¶10 ʻAbdu’l-Bahá applies the rule to Himself: He would forgive an attack on Himself but would prevent an attack on someone else. Nothing in the three chapters asks a victim to withdraw a complaint, refuse to give evidence, stay silent at sentencing, or restore contact or trust. [Inference] The texts distinguish three things that a judge would also distinguish: forgiveness (an inner act of the wronged person), the public process (which continues regardless), and reconciliation or renewed trust (which the texts do not require, and which ¶7’s wolf rules out where the danger continues).

Modern practice draws similar lines (field description only). In England and Wales, Right 7 of the Victims’ Code gives a victim the right to make a Victim Personal Statement “to explain in your own words how a crime has affected you”, which the judge considers at sentence. Restorative justice is voluntary; the Restorative Justice Council has written that victims do not need to forgive in order to take part, and that treating forgiveness as the goal of a meeting is a misconception (Collins, 2015). Randomised trials of face-to-face conferences, where both sides consented before random assignment, found victims more satisfied, more likely to receive an apology, less wanting revenge, and with fewer post-traumatic stress symptoms (Strang et al., Campbell review, 2013; Angel et al., 2014: 49% fewer victims at clinical stress levels, in a London sample of 192 robbery and burglary victims, measured within one month). Women’s and victims’ organisations have objected to restorative meetings in domestic abuse cases precisely because of pressure to forgive (Northumbria PCC Vera Baird, 2016).

What is left open. Whether a teaching that some victims should forgive becomes, in practice, pressure on all victims. The text addresses the wronged person’s own conscience; it says nothing about what others may ask of them. A real case shows forgiveness offered freely: Gee Walker, whose son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Huyton, Merseyside, in July 2005, said “I’ve got to forgive them” after the verdict. She chose it. The text cannot make a judge comfortable with anyone else choosing it for a victim, and Arthur should not try.

2. “Your theory of punishment has no room for desert.”

The objection. “Section 57 of the Sentencing Act 2020 lists five purposes, and the first is the punishment of offenders. Chapter 77 recognises only deterrence and protection. A purely forward-looking theory has a well-known defect: if the only aim is to deter others, you can justify an exemplary sentence out of all proportion to the offence, or even punishing the wrong man if the public believes he did it. Proportion to the wrong done is what protects the defendant.”

What the texts say. Chapter 76 opens with a desert principle: “justice consists in rendering to each his due” (76 ¶1). Chapter 77 ¶2 distinguishes “revenge and retaliation” from “punishment and requital”, and what it excludes is “that inner gratification that results from returning like for like”, not the idea that the offender has earned a penalty. Chapter 77 ¶8: “The canopy of existence rests upon the pole of justice and not of forgiveness.” Chapter 77 ¶14 rejects “harsh punishments and penalties”, and ¶11 criticises the state that is “ever seeking to strengthen penal laws”. [Inference] The chapters therefore contain material that limits exemplary punishment, even though they state the purpose of punishment in deterrent and protective terms.

What is left open. How “requital” and “each his due” become a proportionate sentence. The chapters give no tariff. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas notes leave “degrees of offense, whether extenuating circumstances are to be taken into account” to the Universal House of Justice (note 86). A judge may reasonably say the chapters state a purpose and leave the safeguards to institutions not yet described.

3. “Severity does not deter. Certainty does.”

The objection. “Every serious review I read on the bench said the same thing: the chance of being caught matters; the length of sentence barely does. If punishment is justified by deterrence, the text has to accept that most of the punishment we hand out deters very little.”

What the texts say. The chapters agree with the conclusion more than the objection assumes. They justify punishment by deterrence and protection (76 ¶6; 77 ¶2–3, ¶6), but they do not argue for harsher sentences; ¶14 says the body politic must prevent crime “rather than devise harsh punishments and penalties”. The deterrence claim in ¶6 is about the existence of a penal system: “were both the victim’s heirs and the community to forgive and return good for evil, the wrongdoers would never cease their onslaught”.

Field description: the US National Institute of Justice summary of Nagin (2013) says “the certainty of being caught is a vastly more powerful deterrent than the punishment”, “increasing the severity of punishment does little to deter crime”, and “there is no proof that the death penalty deters criminals” (NIJ, 5 June 2016). Nagin’s own formulation is narrower: the evidence concerns certainty of apprehension, and the policing literature is “silent” on mandatory incarceration. On ¶6’s narrower claim, the Montreal police and firefighters’ strike of 7 October 1969 is often cited: in about 16 hours there were bank robberies, widespread looting, one police officer killed and over 100 arrests (Canadian Encyclopedia). It is a single event with other causes at work, not a controlled study.

What is left open. The chapters’ claim (no punishment at all would be disastrous) and the evidence (marginal increases in severity add little) are compatible. Neither tells a sentencer what length of sentence is enough.

4. “And the innocent man you hang?”

The objection. “Chapter 77 sounds humane. But the Kitáb-i-Aqdas prescribes death for murder and arson. I am from the generation that grew up with Timothy Evans, hanged in 1950 and later pardoned, and Derek Bentley, hanged in 1953, whose conviction was quashed in 1998. Mahmood Mattan was hanged in Cardiff in 1952; his was the first conviction the Criminal Cases Review Commission got quashed, in 1998. Andrew Malkinson, from my own city’s courts, served 17 years for a rape he did not commit; his conviction was quashed in 2023. Any system that executes will execute innocent people. Then I read in the notes to that book: ‘You cannot give up a salutary law just because on rare occasions the innocent may be punished.’ That is the sentence I cannot accept.”

What the texts say. Chapter 77 does not mention the death penalty directly. Its ¶11 lists “instruments of death and chastisement” among the means that a state relies on in place of education, and calls the emphasis “most detrimental”. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62 prescribes death for deliberate murder and arson and adds: “Should ye condemn the arsonist and the murderer to life imprisonment, it would be permissible according to the provisions of the Book.” Note 86 quotes a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi containing the sentence Elias quotes, preceded by: “If a man were falsely condemned to die, can we not believe Almighty God would compensate him a thousandfold, in the next world, for this human injustice?” Note 87 records Shoghi Effendi’s statement that life imprisonment is provided “whereby the rigors of such a condemnation can be seriously mitigated”, and that “Bahá’u’lláh has given us a choice and has, therefore, left us free to use our own discretion within certain limitations imposed by His law.” Note 86 says this is “a law designed for a future state of society”, and that “which of the two prescribed punishments is to be the norm” is left to the Universal House of Justice. In The Secret of Divine Civilization ʻAbdu’l-Bahá asks that sentences “involving capital punishment, imprisonment and the like” be “contingent on confirmation by ... higher courts”, after they “duly investigate the case”, with “bribery and corruption ... forever excluded”.

Field description: in the United States, Gross, O’Brien, Hu and Kennedy (PNAS, 2014) estimated that at least 4.1% of defendants sentenced to death between 1973 and 2004 would be exonerated if they all stayed under sentence of death indefinitely; they call it a conservative estimate. The Death Penalty Information Center counted 203 death-row exonerations since 1973 (updated September 2026). The National Research Council (2012) concluded that research to date “is not informative about whether capital punishment decreases, increases, or has no effect on homicide rates”. In England and Wales, the CCRC referred 31 cases in 2024/25, of 27 appeals heard after a referral 18 were allowed, and it has referred more than 870 cases since 1997 (CCRC, 17 July 2025).

What is left open. This is the objection with the most weight, and the texts do not remove it. Arthur can show three things honestly: the law itself provides a non-capital alternative; which punishment is the norm is not yet decided; and ʻAbdu’l-Bahá elsewhere asks for appellate confirmation of capital sentences. He cannot show that the letter’s reasoning, which rests on compensation in the next world, is one a secular court could use. [Inference] A judge may note that the alternative of life imprisonment removes his objection only if it becomes the norm, and that the corpus leaves that open.

5. “’The body politic holds no grudge.’ I have seen the state with a grudge.”

The objection. “Chapter 77 ¶6 describes a state that ‘acts without prejudice’. The Lammy Review (2017) found that for every 100 white women given custody at the Crown Court for drug offences, 227 Black women were. The Post Office prosecuted sub-postmasters for years on the evidence of a faulty computer system, and Parliament had to quash the convictions by statute in

  1. The state you describe is not the one I worked in.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 6 states what public punishment must be, not a claim that every state meets it: “The body politic is not prompted by ill will in meting out its punishment; it acts without prejudice and does not seek to gratify a sense of vengeance.” The measure the text gives can be used against a state that fails it. The Secret of Divine Civilization attacks provincial governors who “function exactly as they please” and calls for review by higher courts and the exclusion of bribery. In the Arabic Hidden Words no. 2, justice lets a person “see with thine own eyes and not through the eyes of others”.

What is left open. The chapters say nothing about how to detect or correct a prejudiced prosecution or court. [Inference] The honest position is that ¶6 is a standard. Elias’s evidence shows how often states fall short of it; it does not show the standard is wrong.

6. “Most of the people I sentenced were not wolves.”

The objection. “’Some men are like bloodthirsty wolves’ (77 ¶9). In twenty-odd years I met perhaps a handful. The rest were young, drunk, addicted, frightened or poor. When we started sentencing on predicted dangerousness, we got Imprisonment for Public Protection: abolished in 2012, not retrospectively, and on 31 March 2026 there were still 896 people serving IPP who had never been released. The label of ‘wolf’ was the trouble.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 9 says “some men”, not most, and the wolf passage is in the context of protection: what happens “if they were to see no punishment ahead” (¶9) and if “both the victim’s heirs and the community” forgave (¶6). The examples given are tyrants (a “tyrant of Persia”, the caliph Mutavakkil), not ordinary offenders. Chapter 77’s main remedy for most crime is education (¶3, ¶11–14), not incapacitation.

Field description: NIJ cites Mulvey (2011) that about 2 to 5 percent of offenders commit 50 percent or more of crime, and Sampson, Laub and Eggleston (2004) that offending declines steeply at about age 35. A meta-analysis of general-population studies (Sanz-García et al., 2021) put psychopathy prevalence at 1.2% when measured with the PCL-R and 4.5% across all instruments. A group of experts (DeMatteo et al., 2020) argued that the PCL-R “cannot and should not be used” to predict serious institutional violence in capital sentencing; Olver et al. replied that this overstated the case. UN human rights experts urged review and resentencing of IPP prisoners in July 2026.

What is left open. The text supplies no procedure for deciding who is a “wolf” and for how long. IPP is a real example of a protective sentence, justified in language close to ¶6, that held people long past their tariff. [Inference] Arthur can point out that ¶6 and ¶11 together condemn both failures: abandoning the flock, and building prisons that degrade the people held in them.

7. “’Ignorance is the root cause of crime.’ I sentenced solicitors, accountants and a GP.”

The objection. “The most expensive frauds I tried were committed by very well-educated people. Sutherland said in 1939 that theories explaining crime by poverty and ignorance leave out the crimes of the respectable. Education did not stop them; it made them better at it.”

What the texts say. “Knowledge” in 77 ¶12 is not schooling alone: “understanding was broadened, perceptions were refined, morals were rectified and manners reformed”. Paragraph 11 lists “praiseworthy virtues and laudable manners” alongside “science and learning”. Paragraph 13 defines the civilisation that reduces crime as “true civilization”, which “is divine civilization, the civilization of those who combine material and spiritual perfections”.

Field description: Lochner and Moretti (2004), using changes in US compulsory-schooling laws, found that schooling significantly reduces the probability of arrest and incarceration, and that self-report data point to a change in behaviour, not only in the chance of being caught. Machin, Marie and Vujić (2011) found that raising the school-leaving age in England and Wales reduced property crime. A later review notes that part of the short-run effect may be incapacitation (young people in school are not on the street). Sutherland’s 1939 presidential address to the American Sociological Society introduced “white-collar crime”.

What is left open. [Inference] The statement that “ignorance is the root cause of crime” is testable only if “ignorance” means lack of schooling, and in that sense it is too strong: the evidence shows schooling reduces some kinds of crime at the margin, not that crime is absent among the educated. If “ignorance” includes moral ignorance, the claim becomes harder to test. Arthur should say which reading he means and admit that the strong reading is not supported.

8. “’No crimes would occur.’ That is not a sentencing policy.”

The objection. “77 ¶3 says a people can be educated so that ‘no crimes would occur in the first place’. A judge works with the world as it is.”

What the texts say. The next paragraph says the same: “We must speak only of that which is practically feasible in the world. There is indeed an abundance of lofty ideals and sentiments that cannot be put into effect” (77 ¶4). The chapter then keeps punishment (¶6), and in ¶12 makes the weaker claim: “the occurrence of crime would subside”. The First Tablet to The Hague makes education a public duty: “If the parents are able to provide the expenses of this education, it is well, otherwise the community must provide the means”.

Field description: the HighScope Perry Preschool study (randomised, Ypsilanti, Michigan, 1962–67, small sample) found 36% of the programme group and 55% of the control group arrested five or more times by age 40 (Schweinhart et al., 2005). The Chicago Child-Parent Centers (matched comparison, not randomised; 1,539 children born in 1980) reported juvenile arrest rates of 16.9% against 25.1% (Reynolds et al., JAMA, 2001, as reported by the University of Wisconsin). For education in prison, RAND (Davis et al., 2013) found 43% lower odds of reoffending among participants in the higher-quality studies; the 2018 update (Bozick et al.) found participants 28% less likely to reoffend and no difference in post-release employment. In England and Wales, Justice Data Lab analyses of Prisoners’ Education Trust grants found lower one-year reoffending among grant recipients (e.g. 19% against 26% for a matched group in the first analysis).

What is left open. The evidence supports ¶12 (crime falls) and does not test ¶3 (crime ends). The preschool studies are small and specific to their populations; their authors caution against generalising.

9. “Prison degrades. Agreed. So why does Norway’s work?”

The objection. “I agree with ¶11 more than you might think. Two in three adults I sent down for under twelve months were back within a year. But the best study I know, from Norway, found that prison reduced reoffending. So it is not prison as such. It is what kind of prison.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 11 is about a particular kind of prison: “prisons ... chains and fetters ... places of exile and banishment, of torment and hardship”, which “only brings about the degradation of morals and the subversion of character”. Paragraph 6 still provides for the state “to imprison or punish him solely to ensure the protection of others”, and ¶11 says the state should be “seeking thereby to reform the criminal” in fact rather than in name.

Field description: the Ministry of Justice bulletin published 30 July 2026 (July–September 2024 cohort) gives an overall proven reoffending rate of 29.9%; 40.6% for adults released from custody; 68.2% for adults released from sentences of less than 12 months; 36.2% for adults on court orders. In the US, BJS found 82% of state prisoners released in 24 states in 2008 were arrested at least once within ten years. Nagin, Cullen and Johnson (2009), as summarised by NIJ, found imprisonment had a null or mildly criminogenic effect on reoffending. In Norway, Bhuller, Dahl, Løken and Mogstad (Journal of Political Economy, 2020), using random assignment of cases to judges, found that imprisonment lowered the probability of reoffending within five years by 27 percentage points, with large effects among the previously non-employed; the authors attribute the difference from US findings to shorter sentences (about six months on average in their sample) and to prison conditions and rehabilitation programmes. Halden prison (opened 2010) is built on the “normality” principle, under which loss of liberty is the punishment. Recidivism comparisons between countries are unreliable: Andersen and Skardhamar (2017) obtained Norwegian rates from 9% to 53% depending on definition and follow-up.

What is left open. [Inference] The Norwegian evidence can be read two ways: as a correction of ¶11 (some prisons reform) or as support for it (the prisons that reform are the ones without “torment and hardship”). Both readings are defensible. Pratt (2008) ties Scandinavian penal practice to egalitarian welfare states, which would connect ch. 77 to ch. 78, but that is his argument, not a finding.

10. “’The lawless tribes of Africa’ ... ‘consume each other’s flesh’. My grandparents were described in that language.”

The objection. “77 ¶13 contrasts ’the lawless tribes of Africa’ with Switzerland. The British in India passed the Criminal Tribes Act 1871, which declared whole communities criminal by birth; it was repealed only in 1952. In February 1904, the same years as these talks, the Casement Report described the Congo Free State’s soldiers and agents, under King Leopold II, killing and mutilating Africans over rubber quotas. Who was lawless? And the cannibalism reports came mostly from the people who wanted the land.”

What the texts say. The paragraph’s argument is that education and virtue reduce crime. Its definition of civilisation is moral, not European: “true civilization is divine civilization, the civilization of those who combine material and spiritual perfections” (77 ¶13). On those terms, the colonial violence Elias cites fails the test. Elsewhere ʻAbdu’l-Bahá rejected racial hierarchy directly: “God does not look at colors; He looks at the hearts” (Howard University, 23 April 1912, Promulgation of Universal Peace). Chapter 78 ¶4 condemns the man who has “made an entire country his personal colony”.

Field description: William Arens’s The Man-Eating Myth (Oxford University Press, 1979) argued that most reports of customary cannibalism rest on hearsay by outsiders; its strong thesis was largely rejected, while its criticism of the sources was influential. Later work treats some prehistoric cannibalism as established (Marlar et al., Nature, 2000, on a Puebloan site of about AD 1150; disputed by Dongoske, Martin and Ferguson, 2000). On Africa specifically, historians describe cannibalism accusations as a recurring colonial trope used to justify slaving and rule (Heintze; Staller, Converging on Cannibals, reviewed by Herrmann, African Studies Review, 2022), and Keefer’s study of the 1913 Sierra Leone “Human Leopard” trials argues cannibalism was not really at issue in those prosecutions.

What is left open. [Inference] The sentence repeats a general description of Africa that was common in European writing of 1904 and that historians now treat as unreliable. Arthur should say so plainly and not defend it as a description of Africa. The claim the paragraph is making (education and virtue reduce crime) does not depend on it. The Persian is barábirih-yi Afríqá, “the barbarians of Africa”.

11. “’A savage Arab.’ In my court that would be a prejudicial description.”

The objection. “The hypothetical attacker in ¶10 is given an ethnicity. If counsel did that to a jury, I would have stopped him.”

What the texts say. The paragraph’s point is the duty to protect a third party: “if someone were to attack Siyyid Manshádí here, I would of course prevent him ... Were I to abandon you to that man, this would be oppression, not justice.” [Inference] The speaker was living in ʻAkká, an Arab town, and His companions were mostly Persian; the phrase most likely refers to the armed raiders of the region in his own experience rather than to Arabs in general, but the Persian has not been checked and this cannot be confirmed here.

What is left open. The descriptor adds nothing to the argument (see “Passages that need care”). If Elias raises it, Arthur should concede the wording and keep the principle: forgive harm done to you; prevent harm done to others.

12. “’They should obey and comply.’ Workers have a right to strike.”

The objection. “78 ¶9 tells workers not to strike and to ‘obey and comply’. On 21 May 2026 the International Court of Justice gave its advisory opinion that ILO Convention 87 protects the right to strike. The European Court has held that Article 11 covers strike action (Enerji Yapı-Yol Sen v Turkey, 2009). A worker’s only real power against an employer is to withdraw his labour. Tell him to obey, and you have told him to accept whatever he is given.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 9 does not stand alone. The chapter opens by naming “the excessive greed and rapacity of the factory owners” first (¶1), calls the concentration of wealth “the very height of inequity” (¶2), and requires laws for wages, profit shares and pensions (¶4–8). Paragraph 9 then makes those rights enforceable on both sides: “the mutual and equitable rights of both parties should be officially fixed and established according to the laws of justice and compassion, and any party that violates them should be condemned after a fair hearing and be subject to a definitive verdict enforced by the executive branch.” Paragraph 11 treats strikes as caused by either side: “whether they arise from the inordinate demands of the workers or the excessive greed of the factory owners”. [Inference] The English “obey and comply” does not name what is to be obeyed; in context it can be read as obeying the terms fixed by that process, not the employer’s will. The Persian has not been checked.

Later guidance qualifies the instruction for Bahá’ís. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 11 July 1956, accepted the British National Spiritual Assembly’s understanding that where the law recognises strikes “when called by properly constituted authorities such as a Trade Union”, the teaching, “in spite of ʻAbdu’l-Bahá’s express disapproval of strikes, neither requires nor forbids an individual to strike”. The letter adds: “We should avoid becoming rigid and laying down any more rules and regulations of conduct.” A letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 23 June 1985, restates this (Lights of Guidance §2121). An earlier letter on the Guardian’s behalf, 30 June 1937, said “Non-cooperation too, even though not accompanied by acts of violence, is ineffective” and advised Bahá’ís to avoid “as much as they can” getting involved in strikes (Lights of Guidance §1404).

What is left open. Paragraph 9 presupposes a fair tribunal with an executive that enforces its verdict against owners as well as workers. Where that does not exist, the condition fails and the text does not say what workers should do. Elias can supply the cases: at Grunwick (1976–78), Acas recommended recognition of the union, the employer refused, the House of Lords upheld the employer, and the strike ended in defeat after about 670 days. In Belarus, the ILO applied Article 33 of its Constitution in 2022–23 over imprisoned trade unionists, which unions describe as the first such use on freedom of association. Arthur should concede that the chapter’s model depends on institutions that are often absent or captured.

13. “Compulsory arbitration was tried in your own decade. It did not end strikes.”

The objection. “New Zealand passed compulsory arbitration in 1894 and was called ‘the country without strikes’. Australia passed the Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act in

  1. Both had strikes again. When Australia used the arbitration court’s penal powers to jail a union official, Clarrie O’Shea, in 1969, about a million workers stopped work. And the ILO’s Committee on Freedom of Association says compulsory arbitration is acceptable only in narrow cases.”

What the texts say. Paragraphs 9–11 describe something close to the Australasian model: a public body fixes mutual rights, hears disputes and issues a binding verdict. The justification in ¶10 is that “commercial, industrial, and agricultural affairs, and even the general business of the nation, are all intimately linked together: An impairment to one is a loss to all.” [Inference] The resemblance to the New Zealand Act of 1894 and the Commonwealth Act of 1904 is close, but nothing in the corpus shows that ʻAbdu’l-Bahá or Laura Barney had them in mind.

Field description: Te Ara (the New Zealand government encyclopedia) says the 1894 Act’s aim was to stop strikes, that larger unions disliked giving up the right to strike and employers disliked a judge setting pay, and that the arbitration system lasted for most of the next 80 years. Lloyd’s A Country Without Strikes (1900) praised it; the label rests on there being no significant stoppages from 1894 to 1906. In Australia, the Harvester judgment (1907) set a “fair and reasonable” wage by “the normal needs of the average employee, regarded as a human being living in a civilised community”, which is close to 78 ¶5’s “wage that adequately meets their daily needs”. The ILO Committee on Freedom of Association has held that compulsory arbitration “is only acceptable in relation to public servants engaged in the administration of the State ... or in essential services in the strict sense of the term or in cases of acute national crisis” (as quoted in a Fiji Times report of a CFA observation; the 2018 Compilation paragraph was not reached).

What is left open. The record is mixed: arbitration produced living-wage standards and long periods of industrial peace, did not abolish strikes, and its enforcement against unions produced Australia’s largest post-war strike. Whether the chapter’s model includes penalties against workers who strike anyway is not stated; ¶9 says the verdict is “enforced by the executive branch” against “any party”.

14. “Profit sharing has been used to break unions.”

The objection. “A fourth or a fifth of profits sounds generous. In 1889 George Livesey introduced profit sharing at the South Metropolitan Gas Company in the same weeks his stokers joined the gas workers’ union, and about 2,000 men struck against it. And profit shares depend on profits. The John Lewis Partnership paid no bonus for three years before 2026.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 5 gives the share “in accordance with its means” and offers alternatives: workers could “equitably share in some other way”, or receive “a wage that adequately meets their daily needs” plus a right to share in revenues when “injured, incapacitated, or unable to work”, or a wage that lets them “save a little for times of weakness and incapacity”. Paragraph 9 makes the arrangement a matter of law and public enforcement, not of an owner’s discretion.

Field description: a meta-regression of 56 studies (Doucouliagos, Laroche, Kruse and Stanley, British Journal of Industrial Relations, 2020) found profit sharing positively associated with productivity on average, more strongly where unionisation was higher and where it was combined with employee participation in decisions. Kruse, Freeman and Blasi (eds., Shared Capitalism at Work, NBER/University of Chicago Press, 2010) report an average productivity effect of about 4.5% across studies, with wide variation. The John Lewis Partnership paid a 2% bonus in March 2026, its first in four years (last 3% in 2022). Mondragon reported €11.322 billion in sales for 2025, but its Fagor Electrodomésticos co-operative went bankrupt in 2013 with about 5,600 jobs at risk after the group refused a €170 million rescue.

What is left open. [Inference] Livesey’s scheme was voluntary, set by the employer, and offered against the union; ¶9 envisages shares fixed by law and enforced against owners. The evidence suggests profit sharing works better with worker voice, which supports reading ¶5 together with ¶9’s “both parties”. What workers receive in a year without profits, beyond the guaranteed wage, is not stated.

15. “A pension from ‘the revenues of the factory itself’ dies with the factory.”

The objection. “78 ¶8 wants pensions paid by the factory. BHS’s pension schemes were about £350 million short by the time of its sale in 2015. Carillion had thirteen defined-benefit schemes and more than 27,000 members when it collapsed. That is why we built the Pension Protection Fund and a state pension.”

What the texts say. Paragraph 8 asks that workers who “become incapacitated, grow old, or die and leave behind small children” receive “a modest pension from the revenues of the factory itself”, fixed by law (¶8: “laws must be enacted”). Paragraph 4 also gives the state a general duty to “meet the basic needs of the myriad millions of the poor”.

Field description: the UK Old Age Pensions Act 1908 (in effect 1 January 1909) paid five shillings a week to people over 70 with low incomes and of “good character”; it was passed four years after the earliest of these talks. The Pension Protection Fund was created by the Pensions Act 2004 and began in April 2005, funded by levies on defined-benefit schemes.

What is left open. [Inference] The principle in ¶8 (the enterprise pays for its workers’ old age and dependants, by law) can be met by firm contributions into pooled or insured funds; the text does not specify the mechanism. Read literally, a pension paid only from one factory’s revenues has the weakness Elias describes.

16. “Is it law or charity? The chapter says both.”

The objection. “Paragraph 4 says ‘enact such laws and regulations as will moderate the excessive fortunes of the few’. Paragraph 12 praises giving that is not ‘outwardly compelled or obliged by the government’. And the Hague Tablet says sharing ‘should not be introduced by coercion’. Which is it?”

What the texts say. The chapter assigns different things to each. Law covers wages, profit shares, pensions, limits on concentration, and enforcement (¶4–9: “laws and regulations should be enacted”; “laws must be enacted”). Voluntary giving is in addition (¶12–13). The First Tablet to The Hague describes “voluntary sharing” as something “greater than equality”: that a person “should not prefer himself to others, but rather should sacrifice his life and property for others”. [Inference] What may not be coerced is that self-sacrifice, not the legal minimum in ¶4–9. The Universal House of Justice, 1 March 2017: “Bahá’u’lláh does not set out in His Revelation a detailed economic system.”

Field description, with what each measures: the World Inequality Report 2026 (World Inequality Lab, published December 2025) estimates that the top 10% of adults own about three-quarters of global personal wealth, the bottom 50% about 2%, and the top 1% about 37% (2025, at purchasing power parity). Oxfam’s January 2026 report, using Forbes billionaire estimates, put billionaire wealth at $18.3 trillion at the end of 2025, up more than 16% in the year and 81% since 2020; this measures the wealth of about 3,000 individuals, not a distribution. The UBS Global Wealth Report 2025 (June 2026) found total personal wealth up about 10.8% in 2025 while median wealth fell in most countries; it counted 3,302 billionaires in 47 markets. These use different data and definitions and should not be combined.

What is left open. What level of concentration ¶4 would call “excessive”, and what laws would “moderate” it. The texts reject both destitution and “absolute equality” (¶5, ¶7) and leave the measure to legislation.

Optional, minor: “Animals are not equal either.”

If Elias raises 78 ¶3 (“among the animals, a certain kind of justice and equality prevails”): dominance hierarchies are well documented in animals since Schjelderup-Ebbe’s description of the “pecking order” in hens (1921–22). The text’s claim is narrower than it first sounds: animals of a species “do not differ greatly from one another in their means of existence”. [Inference] That is a claim about access to subsistence, not rank, and it is the comparison the paragraph needs. This is a naturalist’s objection rather than a judge’s; it can be dropped.

Passages that need care

Four passages need a decision before they are read aloud. For each: what the text says, the problem, and the options.

  1. 77 ¶13, “the lawless tribes of Africa” and cannibalism, against Switzerland. The Persian is barábirih-yi Afríqá, “the barbarians of Africa”. The sentence repeats a description common in European writing of 1904 that historians now treat as unreliable and often self-serving (see objection 10). The paragraph’s argument (education and virtue, not race, reduce crime) and its definition of civilization (“divine civilization ... material and spiritual perfections”) do not depend on the example. Chapter 3 ¶3 and chapter 29 ¶5 contain similar remarks; release 1 of the series ended its reading of 3 ¶3 before the Sudan sentence. Options: (a) narrate ¶13 to “the less crime will be committed” and have the host say plainly that the paragraph goes on to an example about Africa that repeats the colonial reporting of its day, and that he will not defend it as a description of Africa; (b) read it whole and give it the full discussion in objection 10. Option (a) keeps the argument and is honest about the omission.
  2. 77 ¶10, “a savage Arab ... brandishing a sword”. The descriptor adds nothing to the point (the duty to protect a third party). The Persian has not been checked. Options: narrate ¶10 to “But the body politic must safeguard the rights of man” and let the host paraphrase the rest (“if a man came in with a sword...”); or omit ¶10 from narration and quote its sentence about Siyyid Manshádí in conversation.
  3. 76 ¶2, the Jewish dietary example. The gloss (“’trefah’ in Hebrew means unclean, while ‘kosher’ means clean”) simplifies Jewish usage. It is not offensive but it invites a correction the episode has no time for. Option: omit ¶2 from narration; the host summarises reason versus religious law.
  4. Aqdas ¶62 and the letter in note 86 (“You cannot give up a salutary law just because on rare occasions the innocent may be punished”). Not in the three chapters, but a judge will raise capital punishment, and an honest episode should not hide the Aqdas penalty. The 1956 letter asked that the subject “be given no publicity” at that time; the Kitáb-i-Aqdas and its notes have since been published (1992) with this note in them. Option: let Elias raise it; Arthur states the law (death or life imprisonment, for a future society, the norm left to the House of Justice) and concedes that the letter’s answer to wrongful execution is a theological one that no court could use. Do not quote the note’s sentence on screen without its full context.

Two further passages need care in tone rather than a decision. 77 ¶5 (Paul should “if possible, even be of some assistance to his aggressor”) must not be voiced as an instruction to victims; it is about the wronged person’s own conscience, and the episode should say so. 78 ¶9 (“They should obey and comply”) must be read to the end of the paragraph, so the condition (rights fixed by law, a fair hearing, a verdict enforced against “any party”) is heard with it.

What could not be verified

  • The Persian readings. Every transliteration on this page is from the machine-read 1908 Persian edition in the corpus, normalised and reversed. They should be checked against the bahai.org Persian text before any is shown on screen, in particular qiṣáṣ in 77 ¶2–6, ḥaqqáníyyat in 78 ¶9 (rendered “compassion”) and gáhí (“sometimes”) in 78 ¶11. Whether the corpus Persian is the 1908 Brill printing could not be confirmed (the year is blank in the scan).
  • The Islamic-law background (qiṣáṣ as the heirs’ right of retaliation, pardon and blood-money; farḍ-i ’ayn) is general knowledge supported by a secondary web source, not by the corpus.
  • The date of chapter 78. It is absent from the 1908 English editions and present in the 1918 Chicago edition; Barney’s 1937 letter survives only in Afroukhteh’s back-translation. Why it was left out of the 1908 English is not known.
  • Why Barney asked about strikes. No source says.
  • The tyrant who “killed his tutor” and al-Mutawakkil’s scorpions (77 ¶9): no source for either story was found.
  • The date and recipient of the letter quoted in Kitáb-i-Aqdas note 86 (“You cannot give up a salutary law ...”) are not given in the corpus.
  • The original Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh’s “I would have kissed the hand of My would-be murderer” is not identified in the corpus; it is cited from The Advent of Divine Justice.
  • Whether 76 ¶1’s justice formula quotes Bahá’u’lláh’s Persian, and whether 77 ¶8’s tent-and-pillar image echoes the Ishráqát: the Persian and Arabic originals of those Tablets are not in the corpus.
  • The talk note on “two thousand shares” (Star of the West 7, 1916): its date and occasion are unclear. The corpus copy of Promulgation places it in the Montreal address of 3 September 1912 alongside a paragraph on “Bolshevistic principles” that cannot come from 1912; Esslemont attributes it to Dublin, New Hampshire, 1912.
  • The dates of the Ottoman commissions of inquiry conflict between Afroukhteh and God Passes By. The chapters cannot be assigned to a month.
  • Ethel Rosenberg’s note of 9 August 1904 (“Prisoners and Punishment”) contains statements not in chapter 77, including “We have no right to torture prisoners” and “The prisoner should have everything necessary for his comfort and ease and should only be deprived of his freedom.” These are a pilgrim’s English notes and may not be quoted as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s words.
  • Period facts taken from secondary summaries: the Gladstone Committee’s report (1895) was not read; the treadmill figures, the Taff Vale damages, the Russian strike figures, Livesey’s dates and the Leopold annexation date come from secondary sources and differ between them. Lacassagne’s maxim “societies have the criminals they deserve” is unverified and not used.
  • Modern evidence: several statistics come from press releases or summaries rather than the papers themselves (Chicago Child-Parent Centers, Perry Preschool, Norway imprisonment effects, the Criminal Cases Review Commission’s 2025/26 figures, the reported vote in the International Court of Justice’s advisory opinion of 21 May 2026). Figures for 2025–2026 are recent and may be revised.
