Judging Readiness: How Bahá’í Institutions Decide a Community Is Ready for the Next Stage

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

Bahá’í institutions decide that a community is ready for the next stage by reading a state that has already come into being — not by conferring a status, not by reaching a date, and not by satisfying a checklist. The vocabulary the Universal House of Justice uses when it makes such a judgement is the vocabulary of recognition, not of promotion: a milestone is “passed”, an epoch is “recognized”, an intensive programme of growth is “launched” on the basis of “conscious recognition that all the elements necessary… are not only in place but also functioning with an adequate degree of effectiveness.” Readiness, in this framework, is a fact about capacity that an institution notices and names — usually somewhat after the fact.

That is the design. But it is a demanding design, because it puts the burden of honesty on the people closest to the work, who are also the people with the strongest reasons to flatter it.

My position on the central question. The characteristic failure in judging readiness is not impatience and it is not sloth. It is substituting form for function — treating the existence of a structure as evidence of the capacity the structure was built to house. An Assembly exists, therefore the locality has an Assembly’s capacities. A committee has been appointed, therefore the work it was appointed for is being done. A milestone has been reported, therefore the reality the milestone describes obtains. Every institutional correction the Bahá’í world has made in this area over the last fifty years — restoring the first-day-of-Riḍván election in 1997, retiring the four-category cluster scheme in 2010, defining every milestone qualitatively rather than numerically, refusing to name a fourth milestone even while describing what lies past the third — has been a move in the same direction: away from the countable sign and back toward the thing signified.

The second position, and the one more often needed in practice. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá settles the balance explicitly, and He does not split the difference: “If haste is harmful, inertness and indolence are a thousand times worse.” The community that will not act until it feels ready is making the larger error, and the guidance says so repeatedly — most bluntly in a 1996 letter warning that “over-concern about the role of the local Assembly in community affairs” must not “inhibit the friends from doing those things that can be done.” Honesty about readiness is not the same as caution about action. The Writings ask for both an unflattering assessment and an unhesitating step, and they treat the combination as normal rather than paradoxical.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest. If you serve on an institution or in a cluster agency, the single highest-leverage habit is to describe capacity in the past tense and in the third person: not “we are ready to launch” but “for the last four cycles these five people have sustained these activities without visitors, and here is who counted it.” Almost every premature declaration in the record dissolves when the claim is restated that way, and almost every genuine readiness survives it. The instruments and figures that make such restatement possible belong to truthfulness-foundation; this page is about the judgement they serve.

The Revelation itself is metered to the capacity of its recipients

The habit of calibrating what is given to what can be borne is not an administrative convenience the Bahá’í community adopted; it is a divine method described in the Writings, and every institutional practice downstream is an imitation of it. Bahá’u’lláh states the principle with an image drawn from the sky:

“Know of a certainty that in every Dispensation the light of Divine Revelation hath been vouchsafed unto men in direct proportion to their spiritual capacity. Consider the sun. How feeble its rays the moment it appeareth above the horizon. How gradually its warmth and potency increase as it approacheth its zenith, enabling meanwhile all created things to adapt themselves to the growing intensity of its light… Were it, all of a sudden, to manifest the energies latent within it, it would, no doubt, cause injury to all created things.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings XXXVIII

The passage is usually read as an argument for progressive revelation across dispensations. It is also, read at ground level, a statement about damage: a good thing delivered ahead of the capacity to receive it does not merely fail to help — it injures. Shoghi Effendi cites the same passage twice in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh and sets beside it Christ’s sentence to the disciples: “I have yet many things to say unto you, but ye cannot bear them now.”

The same metering governs Bahá’u’lláh’s own laws. The introduction to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas states plainly that “the society for which certain of the laws of the Aqdas are designed will come only gradually into being,” and quotes Bahá’u’lláh on the temper required of those who apply them:

“Indeed, the laws of God are like unto the ocean and the children of men as fish, did they but know it. However, in observing them one must exercise tact and wisdom… Since most people are feeble and far-removed from the purpose of God, therefore one must observe tact and prudence under all conditions, so that nothing might happen that could cause disturbance and dissension or raise clamor among the heedless.” — Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in the Introduction to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi in 1935 draws the operative distinction: some laws “should be regarded by all believers as universally and vitally applicable at the present time”; others “have been formulated in anticipation of a state of society destined to emerge from the chaotic conditions that prevail today.” The Lawgiver Himself deferred provisions until the society they presuppose exists.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions — which is why this subject is difficult rather than obvious. Readiness is real: capacity is a fact, and pretending it is present when it is not causes injury. And readiness is not a gate God stands at: the sun rises anyway, and its warmth increases whether or not anyone certifies that the earth is ready. The institutions’ task is to read the hour, not to decide it.

Where the Text draws a line, nobody has to judge at all

A striking feature of the Bahá’í framework is how few readiness questions are left open to judgement — and how deliberately the closed ones are closed. Where a threshold matters most and error would be most costly, the Text supplies a number, and the number ends the discussion.

Obligatory prayer and the Fast become binding at fifteen, and the exemptions run by age and condition, not by assessment of spiritual seriousness. The right to vote and to serve on a Spiritual Assembly begins at twenty-one. A Local Spiritual Assembly is to be formed wherever the number of resident adult declared believers reaches nine — and Shoghi Effendi’s language on the point is emphatic in a way that forecloses deliberation:

“It is of the utmost importance that in accordance with the explicit text of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, the Most Holy Book, in every locality, be it city or hamlet, where the number of adult… declared believers exceeds nine, a local ’Spiritual Assembly’ be forthwith established. To it all local matters pertaining to the Cause must be directly and immediately referred for full consultation and decision.” — Shoghi Effendi, 12 March 1923 (The Local Spiritual Assembly compilation)

Forthwith. Not when the friends feel prepared, not when a National Assembly judges the community mature, not after a course of training. The threshold is a headcount, and the headcount is the whole test. The design consequence is worth stating directly: a bright line removes the gatekeeper. Where the criterion is countable and public, no one can withhold an institution from a community on a private estimate of its worthiness, and no community can defer its own responsibilities on a private estimate of its weakness. In a Faith with no clergy, that matters more than efficiency.

What the bright line does not do is confer the capacity. A nine-member body elected on the first day of Riḍván in a village where nobody has served before is an Assembly in the full sense — divinely ordained, its decisions binding, its station exactly that described in ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablets. It is also, on the first morning, an Assembly that cannot yet do most of what Assemblies do. The Bahá’í framework holds both of these at once, and refuses to resolve the tension by delaying the institution until competence arrives. Competence arrives through the institution.

Everything outside these few bright lines is judgement — and the rest of this page is about how that judgement is made, by whom, and against what.

The largest readiness decision in Bahá’í history was a decision to wait

The Universal House of Justice could have been elected decades earlier than 1963, and the reason it was not is the clearest statement in the corpus of what institutional readiness means. Writing on 27 February 1929, Shoghi Effendi first disposes of the criterion most people would assume — popular acceptance:

“The establishment of the Supreme House of Justice is in no way dependent upon the adoption of the Bahá’í Faith by the mass of the peoples of the world, nor does it presuppose its acceptance by the majority of the inhabitants of any one country.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh

He notes in the same paragraph that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá “in one of His earliest Tablets, contemplated the possibility of the formation of the Universal House of Justice in His own lifetime.” Numbers were never the obstacle. Then comes the criterion that was:

“Not until they are themselves fully representative of the rank and file of the believers in their respective countries, not until they have acquired the weight and the experience that will enable them to function vigorously in the organic life of the Cause, can they approach their sacred task, and provide the spiritual basis for the constitution of so august a body in the Bahá’í world.” — Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh

Two conditions, both about the electors rather than the elected: that the National Spiritual Assemblies genuinely represent their communities, and that they have acquired weight and experience enough “to function vigorously.” This is a functional test, and it is a test of the body doing the choosing. The Guardian is saying that an institution constituted by bodies that cannot yet function would inherit their incapacity, whatever its own station.

The consequence was a forty-two-year delay, and the Guardian filled it with exactly the work the criterion named. The House of Justice later described the sequencing without euphemism: the Divine Plan was “held in abeyance for two decades while local and national organs of the Administrative Order were being raised up and strengthened,” and was “formally inaugurated in 1937 with the commencement of the first stage of its first epoch” (Riḍván 2021). The Tablets of the Divine Plan had been revealed in 1916–17. They sat for twenty years — not because they were unclear, and not because nobody wanted to act on them, but because the instrument that would carry them did not yet exist.

By Riḍván 1963 fifty-six National Spiritual Assemblies stood ready, and the first International Bahá’í Convention elected the Universal House of Justice. The pattern established there recurs at every scale afterwards: the threshold is the functioning of the layer below, and the wait is spent building it.

What the mass-teaching decades cost, and what the community did about it

The Bahá’í world’s own account of its most painful readiness error is unusually candid, and it is worth reading before any theory is built on it. Century of Light, quoted at length in Turning Point, describes what happened when enrolments in the 1960s and 1970s outran the community’s capacity to absorb them:

“The principal effect of the setbacks, however, was that they brought home to communities that the high expectations of the early years were in some respects quite unrealistic. Although the easy successes of the initial teaching activities were encouraging, they did not, by themselves, build a Bahá’í community life that could meet the needs of its new members and be self-generating.” — Century of Light, quoted in Turning Point

The questions that followed are listed in the same passage, and the first one is precisely a readiness question about institutions: “How were Local Spiritual Assemblies to be established—and once established, how were they to function—in areas where large numbers of new believers had joined the Cause overnight, simply on the strength of their spiritual apprehension of its truth?” Assemblies existed on paper in their thousands. The form had been achieved; the function had not.

The institutional response, when it came, ran against every incentive an organisation normally has. In its message of 31 December 1995 to the Conference of the Continental Boards of Counsellors, the House of Justice reviewed the accommodations that had accumulated — a 1977 permission for certain localities to elect on any day of the Riḍván festival, and a further permission for first-time Assemblies to be elected at any point in the year — and then reversed them:

“With these thoughts in mind, we have decided that, beginning at Riḍván 1997, the practice of electing all Local Spiritual Assemblies on the first day of Riḍván will be reinstituted. We recognize that the immediate result may be a reduction in the number of Local Spiritual Assemblies at Riḍván 1997, but we are confident that subsequent years will witness a steady increase.” — The Universal House of Justice, 31 December 1995 (muhj-1986-2001)

The reasoning given in the preceding paragraph is the substantive part: “in principle, the initiative and responsibility for electing a Local Spiritual Assembly belong primarily to the Bahá’ís in the locality, and assistance from outside is ultimately fruitful only if the friends become conscious of this sacred responsibility.” An Assembly elected because visiting teachers arranged the election is an Assembly the locality has not yet claimed. The accommodation had been generous and had produced experience — the same message says so — but it had also allowed the count to detach from the reality.

Three features of this decision are worth naming, because together they constitute a working definition of institutional honesty about readiness. The House of Justice predicted the bad number before it arrived, in the same sentence as the decision. It accepted a worse-looking statistic in exchange for a truer one. And it reported the outcome afterwards, in the Riḍván 2000 message, without spin: “While the restriction of the formation of Local Spiritual Assemblies to the first day of Riḍván, which took effect in 1997, produced the anticipated decrease in the number of these institutions, the fall was not drastic. The number has since held its ground and a sound process of consolidation is in place.”

An institution that would rather have a smaller accurate figure than a larger flattering one has solved the hardest problem in readiness assessment. Everything else is technique.

Milestones are read from the ground, not awarded from above

The cluster milestones are the Bahá’í world’s most developed instrument for judging readiness, and their twenty-five-year evolution is a sustained retreat from classification toward description. The four broad categories introduced on 9 January 2001 were already being defended against misuse two years later, in the 17 January 2003 message:

“To assign a cluster to one or another category is not to make a statement about status. Rather, it is a way of evaluating its capacity for growth, in order that an approach compatible with its evolving development can be adopted. Rigid criteria are obviously counterproductive, but a well-defined scheme to carry out evaluation is essential.” — The Universal House of Justice, 17 January 2003 (turning-point)

The same message concedes that “the task of refining the criteria needed for valid assessments is proving to be an ongoing challenge to institutions” — a sentence more useful than it looks, because it establishes that the criteria were understood from the outset to be provisional. Two of them are named as “especially important”: the strength of the human resources raised up by the training institute, and the ability of the institutions to mobilise them. Both are capacities, and both are demonstrated only in action.

By 28 December 2010 the categories were retired outright, and the reason given is the crux of the whole subject:

“A decade ago, when we introduced the concept of a cluster… we provided an outline of four broad stages along the path of its development… The abundant experience which has since accrued enables the believers now to conceive of the movement of a population, propelled by mounting spiritual forces, in terms of a rich and dynamic continuum. A brief review of the process that unfolds in a cluster… will serve to underscore its fundamentally organic nature.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (framework-action)

A category is a box you are in. A continuum is a distance you have travelled. The change of frame changes what an institution is even asking: not what kind of cluster is this but how far has this population moved, and what would carry it further. And the milestones the message then defines are stated in terms of what can be perceived, never in terms of what can be counted. The first is reached when “in whatever combination and however small in number, devotional gatherings, children’s classes and junior youth groups are being maintained by those progressing through the sequence of institute courses,” such that “a nascent programme for the sustained expansion and consolidation of the Faith can be perceived.” The second is the “launching” of an intensive programme of growth, which the message defines with unusual precision:

“That moment which has come to be known as the ‘launching’ of an intensive programme of growth represents conscious recognition that all the elements necessary to accelerate the expansion and consolidation of the Faith are not only in place but also functioning with an adequate degree of effectiveness.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (framework-action)

Read the sentence slowly and it turns out to contain the entire method. Conscious recognition — a judgement, deliberately made, not a form processed. All the elements necessary — a completeness test, not a threshold count. Not only in place but also functioning — the anti-form clause, and the one that does the work. With an adequate degree of effectiveness — a standard that is honest about being a matter of degree rather than pretending to a bright line the reality does not have.

The 29 December 2015 message added a third milestone in the same register — “the appearance of a system for extending, in centre after centre, a dynamic pattern of community life that can engage a people—men and women, youth and adults—in the work of their own spiritual and social transformation” — and grounded it in an inference rather than a measurement: “For the movement of a population to have come this far demonstrates that the process which brought it about is strong enough to achieve and sustain a high degree of participation in all aspects of the capacity-building endeavour and manage the complexity entailed.” The milestone is not the achievement; it is what the achievement proves about the process underneath.

The International Teaching Centre’s Insights from the Frontiers of Learning pushed the same discipline downward, to the least-developed clusters, and its formulation is the one most often needed in the field:

“A new programme of growth begins as two nascent capacities develop. First, one or more friends in a cluster must be able to help individuals study the institute’s sequence of courses and accompany them as they initiate core activities. Then, these individuals must be able to attract others to participate in the core activities. As efforts along these lines have borne fruit in various parts of the world, the institutions concerned set aside exaggerated expectations of what must be achieved before a new programme of growth can be said to have emerged.” — Insights from the Frontiers of Learning, quoted in Reflections on Growth 33

Two capacities — accompaniment and attraction — and a warning that the commonest error at this end of the continuum is over-demanding, not under-demanding. The symmetry with the mass-teaching correction is exact, and it is the reason a single rule (“be stricter”) will not serve: the honest reading is stricter at the top of the continuum and more generous at the bottom.

The 31 December 2025 message restates the principle for the Nine Year Plan’s second phase in a single clause that could stand as this page’s thesis: “growth is an organic process, not a mechanical one.” And it adds a sentence that quietly re-anchors the third milestone against inflation: “The third milestone is a measurement of a journey whose beginnings are well understood.” A milestone measures distance from a known origin. It is not a rank.

Structure follows load: the rule against building ahead of the community

The most operationally useful readiness rule in the corpus concerns when a new institutional arrangement should appear, and the House of Justice states it as a two-sided prohibition. Describing how an Area Teaching Committee comes into being — “arrangements for supporting the friends in these endeavours, which may have been largely informal, eventually prove inadequate, signalling the need for an Area Teaching Committee to appear” — the 29 December 2015 message instructs the Counsellors:

“You will need to encourage National Spiritual Assemblies and Regional Bahá’í Councils, as much as training institutes, to remain alert to when conditions in a cluster call for organizational arrangements to assume a definite shape—neither acting prematurely nor unduly delaying the appearance of formal structures.” — The Universal House of Justice, 29 December 2015 (muhj-2001-2022)

The verb governing the structure is signalling: informal arrangements prove inadequate, and the inadequacy is the signal. Structure is a response to a load already being carried, not a bet on a load someone hopes will arrive. The 31 December 2025 message generalises the rule to every level of the administrative order — “a new feature would not be introduced unless the needs of growth demand it” — while pairing it, in the same sentence, with the opposite vigilance: “we look to you and your auxiliaries to be alert to when existing arrangements at any level of the community need to evolve to meet the requirements of growth.”

The same logic governs the community’s engagement with the wider society, and here the guidance is sharper still because the temptation is greater. The Riḍván 2010 message is explicit:

“Further involvement in the life of society should not be sought prematurely. It will proceed naturally as the friends in every cluster persevere in applying the provisions of the Plan through a process of action, reflection, consultation and study, and learn as a result.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (Social Action compilation)

A letter of 9 April 2017 written on behalf of the House of Justice names the cost of getting this wrong: development projects “introduced prematurely in a cluster… risk distracting and dissipating the energies of the friends who should be attending primarily to advancing the process of expansion and consolidation,” and community schools “have proven more sustainable when they emerge in localities with a strong institute process, as a natural extension of the community-building activities under way.” The failure mode being described is not that the school is a bad thing. It is that a good thing, resourced from a capacity that does not exist, consumes the capacity that does.

Riḍván 2013 puts the general principle in a parenthesis that deserves to be a rule of thumb: “the emergence of social action happens naturally, as a growing community gathers strength.” The corollary is uncomfortable and worth stating: a community that has to organise itself into social action, rather than finding social action breaking out of what it is already doing, has probably answered a readiness question wrongly. The fuller treatment of what follows once it does emerge belongs to social-action-guidance.

The opposite error, which ‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls a thousand times worse

Everything above can be misread as a counsel of caution, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá forecloses that reading in The Secret of Divine Civilization more directly than any other passage in the corpus. He begins by conceding the case for deliberation — “It is certain that momentous undertakings cannot be brought to a successful conclusion in haste; that in such cases haste would only make waste” — and offers the embryological image that grounds it: the political world, like a human being, “cannot instantaneously evolve from the nadir of defectiveness to the zenith of rightness and perfection.” And then He turns:

“If, however, by delay and postponement they mean this, that in each generation only one minute section of the necessary reforms should be attended to, this is nothing but lethargy and inertia, and no results would be forthcoming from such a procedure, except the endless repetition of idle words. If haste is harmful, inertness and indolence are a thousand times worse. A middle course is best.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, The Secret of Divine Civilization

The asymmetry is deliberate and it is quantified. Both errors are errors; one is a thousand times worse. Any account of readiness that leaves a reader more hesitant than before has misrepresented the Writings.

The pastoral form of the same correction arrives in a letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 29 August 1996, at the very moment the training-institute system was being built and friends were worrying that it would come at the Assembly’s expense:

“Care must be taken, however, that over-concern about the role of the Local Assembly in community affairs not inhibit the friends from doing those things that can be done and which, in the long run, will lead to the development of the local community and its institutions… At this stage, the friends everywhere should not allow their anxieties, which arise only from their intense love for the Faith, to hamper them and should act with confidence.” — On behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 29 August 1996 (muhj-1986-2001)

The example given is a children’s class: yes, ideally it would be established under the Assembly’s guidance as part of a comprehensive approach — “yet the lack of the desired method at any given moment should not be used as an argument against pursuing programs that help individuals to establish children’s classes in the villages when this is perhaps their only developed skill.” Readiness-anxiety here is not identified as false modesty or as laziness. It is identified as love, misapplied — which is why it is so persistent and so hard to argue with.

The 28 December 2010 message closes off the other refuge of the over-cautious, which is theory:

“Yet the imposition of formulas on the process will not result in a pattern of growth characterized by the desired equilibrium. Temporary imbalances in the progress of different activities are intrinsic to the process, and they can be adjusted over time, if dealt with patiently. Scaling back one activity that is flourishing, on the basis of theoretical conceptions of how balanced growth can be achieved, often proves counterproductive.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (framework-action)

That last sentence is the specific antidote to a real and recognisable behaviour: the cluster that suppresses its one thriving junior youth programme because its children’s classes lag, in the name of balance. The message adds the frame that makes setbacks legible rather than damning — “Progress is achieved through the dialectic of crisis and victory, and setbacks are inevitable” — and then names the only thing that actually generates readiness: “it is only through continued action, reflection and consultation on their part that they will learn to read their own reality, see their own possibilities, make use of their own resources.”

Capacity is not a precondition of action in this framework. It is a product of action, and the only reliable one. The mode is developed at length in learning-in-action.

The same two-sided caution runs all the way down to the individual’s passage through the institute courses, where the 29 December 2015 message states it as a navigation problem: “Efforts to usher in the first cadre of tutors from the cluster should steer a path between two undesirable outcomes. If individuals proceed through the courses of the institute too hastily, the capacity to serve is not sufficiently developed; conversely, if study is overly prolonged, the process is robbed of the dynamism essential to its advancement.”

Who judges, and on what evidence

Readiness judgements in the Bahá’í system are distributed by scale, and each level is given a different instrument and a different kind of evidence.

The friends in the cluster judge their own state, in the reflection meeting. The Riḍván 2010 message describes the innovation exactly: “a space has been created, in the agency of the reflection meeting, for those engaged in activities at the cluster level to assemble from time to time in order to reach consensus on the current status of their situation, in light of experience and guidance from the institutions, and to determine their immediate steps forward.” Consensus on the current status — the phrase is worth dwelling on, because it locates the primary readiness judgement not in an institution above the work but among the people doing it.

The Area Teaching Committee holds the cluster-wide picture. By the third milestone it is “engaged in a more thorough reading of circumstances in the whole cluster: on the one hand, accurately assessing the capacities of the community and the effects being produced by sustained growth, and on the other, understanding the implications of various social realities for community building in the long term” (29 December 2015).

Auxiliary Board members teach the friends to see the threshold at all. The 28 December 2010 message makes this a defining function: “To help the friends visualize this first important milestone, and the multiplicity of ways in which it can be reached, is central to the functioning of every Auxiliary Board member and an increasing number of his or her assistants.” Note multiplicity of ways — the Board member’s job is partly to prevent the friends from measuring themselves against a single imagined template. The learned arm’s characteristic mode of influence is treated in protection-without-command.

The Local Spiritual Assembly is judged by a criterion it cannot fake. The Riḍván 2010 message sets it out:

“On previous occasions we have explained that the maturity of a Spiritual Assembly cannot be assessed by the regularity of its meetings and the efficiency of its functioning alone. Rather its strength must be measured, to a large extent, by the vitality of the spiritual and social life of the community it serves—a growing community that welcomes the constructive contributions of both those who are formally enrolled and those who are not.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

This is the anti-form principle in its sharpest form. Regularity and efficiency are the two things an institution can most easily produce without producing anything else, and the House of Justice explicitly declines to accept them as the measure. The same message adds, against any inference that a young Assembly should therefore stand back: “current approaches, methods and instruments are providing the means for Local Spiritual Assemblies, even those newly formed, to fulfil these responsibilities.” The Assembly’s evolving role in the cluster era is worked out in lsa-role-in-cluster-era.

And the evidence, at every level, is meant to be experience rather than assumption. The Riḍván 2026 message reports what the Nine Year Plan’s institutional meetings have sounded like, and the compliment it pays is precise: “a profound, insightful conversation based on direct experience of building vibrant communities rather than on assumptions or theory.” When that discipline fails, the field record shows what the failure looks like. In Sanniquellie, Liberia, a stalled programme of growth was diagnosed in a cluster reflection meeting that reached an unflattering conclusion: “we realized that our goals did not match the available human resources. To devise more realistic plans, we needed to establish a system to collect more reliable statistics” (Reflections on Growth 35). In Hamilton, Ontario, the same issue records the structural symptom in one line: “The cluster agencies were no longer functioning, and the spirit of collective action had weakened.”

Both diagnoses were made locally, by the friends themselves, and both begin with an admission. Writing on behalf of the House of Justice to the National Spiritual Assembly of India on 23 July 2013, the Secretariat placed the numbers in their proper relation to the thing they measure: “the collection of data is the result of the organic process of growth in each cluster and constitutes only one means, albeit a critical one, for assessing the progress of the Cause and the community-building process under way.” Data is downstream of growth, not a substitute for reading it. The instruments, their design, and the discipline of reporting them straight belong to truthfulness-foundation.

Epochs: the same judgement made at the scale of a century

At the largest scale the Bahá’í world marks readiness by declaring epochs, and the way these declarations are worded tells you what kind of act they are. Shoghi Effendi established the frame, dividing the Bahá’í Dispensation into the Heroic, Formative and Golden Ages and the Formative Age itself into “a succession of epochs.” His own accounting was retrospective and exact: “The last twenty-three years of the first Bahá’í century may thus be regarded as the initial stage of the Formative Period of the Faith” (Citadel of Faith, 5 June 1947) — a period identified, in his words, “with the rise and establishment of the Administrative Order.” An epoch, in the Guardian’s usage, is named after the thing that constituted it has happened.

The Universal House of Justice has kept that usage, and the evidentiary language is consistent across sixty years.

October 1963 — the third epoch of the Formative Age. Announced with the election of the House of Justice itself and framed as a demand rather than a laurel: “the Cause of God… now enters a new epoch, the third of the Formative Age. It must now grow rapidly in size, increase its spiritual cohesion and executive ability, develop its institutions and extend its influence into all strata of society.”

2 January 1986 — the fourth epoch. The trigger was a change in how plans are made: national goals for the Six Year Plan were, for the first time, to be formulated by National Spiritual Assemblies in consultation with the Counsellors rather than issued from the World Centre. The House of Justice’s wording is that of a court recognising a fact: “Our beloved Guardian anticipated a succession of epochs during the Formative Age of the Faith; have no hesitation in recognizing that this new development in the maturation of Bahá’í institutions marks the inception of the fourth epoch of that Age.” The Riḍván 1987 message adds the crucial detail that the maturity preceded the announcement — “The Administrative institutions of this growing Cause of God had already begun to show signs of an increasing maturity” — and describes the devolution itself as “this expectation of maturity challenging the national communities.” Readiness was not certified and then trusted; it was trusted, and the trusting was the certificate.

16 January 2001 — the fifth epoch. Here the House of Justice describes its own process of recognition almost step by step: there had been “signs that the Faith had arrived at a point in its development beyond which a new horizon opens,” those “intimations” had been reported at Riḍván 2000 and elaborated days earlier to the Counsellors, “but the extraordinary dynamics at work throughout the conference crystallized these indications into a recognizable reality.” Signs, then intimations, then a recognisable reality — three degrees of confidence, named in order, before the announcement.

Riḍván 2021 — the third epoch of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Divine Plan. The criterion offered is the most explicit statement of evidentiary standard in the whole series:

“The phenomenon of sizeable numbers swelling the activities of the community, catching the spark of faith and swiftly arising to serve at the leading edge of the Plan has moved from being a forecast sustained by faith to a recurring reality. Such a pronounced and demonstrable advance demands to be marked in the annals of the Cause.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

A forecast sustained by faith is not evidence. A recurring reality is. The distinction is the whole of the discipline this page is describing, stated by the body that practises it at the highest level — and note that the advance “demands to be marked,” which reverses the usual direction of institutional agency. The House of Justice is not deciding to promote the community; it is acknowledging something the community has already done.

Riḍván 2022 — the sixth epoch of the Formative Age. “In the extraordinary rise in capacity that has occurred in the last two decades—and which has made it possible for the Bahá’í world to view its endeavours in terms of the release of the society-building power of the Faith—we see incontrovertible evidence that the Cause of God has entered the sixth epoch of its Formative Age.” Two decades of accumulated capacity, and the epoch declared at the end of them.

The pattern across all five is one thing: epochs are announced late, on demonstrated capacity, in the language of recognition. No epoch in the record has been declared to inspire an advance that had not yet occurred. That restraint is itself a form of honesty, and it is the model the smaller judgements are meant to imitate.

Forecasting without pretending: how the Bahá’í world plans under uncertainty

If readiness can only be read after the fact, how does anyone plan? The Nine Year Plan’s launch shows the method, and it is more interesting than either a target-setting exercise or a refusal to quantify.

The 30 December 2021 message asked the Counsellors to work with National Assemblies and Regional Councils “to forecast the numbers of clusters where progress could be made past the first, second, and third milestones, respectively, during the Plan” — and then immediately deflated the exercise:

“It should be borne in mind that these are only intended to be well-informed estimates; they can be refined later as necessary and need not be laboured over at length.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 December 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

Need not be laboured over at length. A body that believed forecasts were measurements would not write that sentence. The forecasts were gathered by Naw-Rúz and reported at Riḍván 2022, alongside the outcome of a simultaneous reassessment of cluster boundaries that raised the world total “by a quarter” to over 22,000. The aggregate: a programme of growth in around 14,000 clusters by 2031, intensive in 11,000, with the third milestone passed in more than 5,000 — against a baseline at the close of the One Year Plan of programmes of growth in over 6,000 clusters, close to 5,000 past the second milestone and 1,300 past the third. The House of Justice then characterised the whole set in a phrase that is the best available description of what a readiness forecast is:

“Yet we find these to be worthy aspirations towards which to strive, for they represent an ambitious but serious appraisal of what lies within reach.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2022 (muhj-2001-2022)

Ambitious but serious; an appraisal, not a promise; of what lies within reach, not of what would be splendid. And the justification offered for believing the numbers at all is a readiness argument in itself: “Such objectives could not be realistically contemplated if administrative institutions and agencies had not evolved markedly.” The forecast is credible because of a capacity already demonstrated, not because of a commitment newly made.

The Plan was then divided into two phases — four years to Riḍván 2026, five to Riḍván 2031 — explicitly “for the purpose of gauging the progress made over time.” At the first hinge, the 31 December 2025 message reported an outcome that is genuinely striking against the 2021 baseline: a third-milestone cluster is anticipated by Riḍván 2026 in “around half of the 160 countries and regions where, at the start of the Plan, still no such cluster existed.” The same message pairs the achievement with an unflattering assessment in the next breath: “gratified as we are by the progress made, it is evident that a most formidable task lies ahead if each national Bahá’í community is to fulfil the aspirations for the movement of clusters that it set for itself when the Plan began. There is an urgent need to cultivate more broadly the capacities that are required to intensify a programme of growth.”

That is what an honest mid-term readiness report looks like: the accomplishment stated exactly, the shortfall stated in the same paragraph, and the shortfall attributed to a named missing capacity rather than to effort or will.

The same test at the smallest scale: a single soul

The discipline that governs epochs governs the recognition of faith in one person, and the continuity is not rhetorical. The 31 December 2025 message names the learning question in terms that would be at home in a cluster reflection meeting: the friends have been attending to “how to recognize when the city of the heart is open,” and “much remains to be learned in this regard, both about how to discern receptivity in different settings and how to acknowledge when it has already matured into faith.”

Acknowledge when it has already matured. Once again the institution’s act is recognition of an accomplished fact, and once again the House of Justice reports the question as open rather than solved.

There is no gate here either. Writing on behalf of the House of Justice to the National Spiritual Assembly of the Hawaiian Islands on 9 November 1989, the Secretariat stated that “there is no universally applicable procedure that a new believer must follow in making a declaration of faith. In essence, it is simply the act of making it known to the Bahá’ís that he too believes in Bahá’u’lláh, accepts the Faith, and wishes to be recognized as a member of the Bahá’í community,” and that the process of considering and accepting such a declaration “should follow as swiftly as possible.” The 28 December 2010 message removes the remaining pressure from the timing: whether a first conversation “elicits an invitation for them to enroll in the Bahá’í community or to participate in one of its activities is not an overwhelming concern” — what matters is “commencing a path of service to humanity on which, at the outset or further along, formal enrollment can occur.”

And the test of whether faith has taken hold is the same test as the first cluster milestone, expressed in an image the House of Justice gave to all National Spiritual Assemblies on 25 May 1975, in a message asking each of them “to pause in order to appraise its position” a fifth of the way through the Five Year Plan:

“Teaching may also be likened to kindling a fire, the fire of faith, in the hearts of men. If a fire burns only so long as the match is held to it, it cannot truly be said to have been kindled; to be kindled it must continue to burn of its own accord.” — The Universal House of Justice, 25 May 1975 (muhj-1963-1986)

The same message calls a declaration of faith “merely a milestone along the way—albeit a very important one.” Set that beside the field signature of the first milestone as Reflections on Growth 32 records it — the crossing is visible at the moment a local nucleus takes ownership of activities that outsiders had been sustaining — and the two are one criterion at two scales. Does it continue when the match is withdrawn? That single question, asked without self-flattery, resolves more readiness disputes than any framework.

What the wider world has learned about the same problem

The corpus supplies the principle; it cannot describe how the same failure appears in secular institutions, and the parallel is worth naming carefully — as description of a field, never as authority for a Bahá’í principle.

The most developed external treatment is Matt Andrews, Lant Pritchett and Michael Woolcock’s Building State Capability (Oxford University Press, 2017; open access), which gives two names to failures the Bahá’í record encountered independently. Isomorphic mimicry is the adoption of the outward forms of functional organisations elsewhere — the ministry, the audit office, the anti-corruption commission — in a way that camouflages a persistent absence of function; the organisation looks like what it is meant to be. Premature load bearing is the assignment to a fledgling system of responsibilities heavier than its actual capacity, which the authors summarise as being asked to do “too much of too little too soon too often,” and whose characteristic result is not slow progress but collapse of the legitimacy the young system needed in order to grow.

The convergence should not be over-read: Bahá’í institutions are not development bureaucracies, and the milestone framework was not derived from this literature. But the overlap is instructive in one direction. It tells you that the form-versus-function error is not a Bahá’í weakness or a failure of devotion; it is what happens to any organisation that measures itself by the structures it has erected, and avoiding it takes a deliberate counter-practice rather than good intentions. The Bahá’í counter-practice — defining every threshold in terms of what a population is doing rather than what a body has been constituted to do — is rarer than it looks.

Taking a position: a working discipline for judging readiness

For anyone who has to make one of these judgements — an Assembly member, a coordinator, an Area Teaching Committee, a reflection meeting reaching consensus on its own situation — the guidance yields a short set of rules with real teeth.

State the claim in the past tense. Readiness is a fact about what has been happening, so any assertion of it should survive translation into a completed-action sentence: not “we are ready to launch an intensive programme” but “for the last four cycles, these people have sustained these activities, on these dates, without outside help.” The 28 December 2010 test — every necessary element “not only in place but also functioning with an adequate degree of effectiveness” — is satisfied by the second sentence and untestable against the first.

Apply the withdrawn-match test. Would this continue if the visiting team, the pioneer, the enthusiastic coordinator stopped coming next month? If the answer is no, the milestone has not been crossed, however many activities are running. This is the single most reliable discriminator in the whole framework and it costs nothing to apply.

Name the missing capacity, not the missing outcome. “We are behind on our goals” is not a diagnosis. Sanniquellie’s “our goals did not match the available human resources” is. So is the 31 December 2025 message’s “urgent need to cultivate more broadly the capacities that are required to intensify a programme of growth.” Every genuine readiness gap is a gap in a specific capacity held by specific people, and can be named as such.

Let structure follow load, in both directions. Do not appoint a committee to create work that is not yet happening; do appoint one the moment the informal arrangement visibly strains. “Neither acting prematurely nor unduly delaying” is a two-sided instruction, and in most communities the delay side is the live risk.

Do not scale back what is flourishing in the name of balance. The 28 December 2010 warning is specific and it is routinely ignored. Temporary imbalance is “intrinsic to the process.” Feed the thing that is growing.

Treat readiness-anxiety as a symptom, not a signal. The 1996 letter’s diagnosis — anxieties “which arise only from their intense love for the Faith” — is generous and accurate, and the prescription is to “act with confidence.” If the reason for not starting is that the community does not feel adequate, that is not a readiness finding. It is a mood, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá has already ruled on which error costs more.

And do not let a milestone become an identity. The 17 January 2003 caution — assignment “is not to make a statement about status” — has aged into the most common misuse of the whole vocabulary. A cluster that has passed the second milestone is not thereby exempt from the work the second milestone describes; a cluster that has not is not thereby lesser. The continuum measures distance travelled, and distance travelled is not a rank.

If you work with data or build software, the leverage here is specific and mostly counterintuitive. Nearly every reporting instrument in existence is optimised to make totals grow, because a rising line is what its users want to see. The instrument this framework actually needs does something different: it records function over time rather than existence at a moment. A useful cluster instrument would show, for each activity, how many consecutive cycles it has run, who sustained it in each, and whether that person lives in the locality — because those three facts, and not the count of activities, are what the first and second milestones are actually about. It would make the withdrawal of external support a first-class recorded event, so that continuity after withdrawal becomes visible evidence rather than an impression. It would attach a date and a method to every figure. And it would resist the strongest product instinct in the field, which is to summarise a cluster’s state as a single level or score — precisely the four-category thinking the House of Justice retired in 2010, and which a well-designed dashboard would smuggle back in under a new name. A tool whose output is a paragraph a reflection meeting can argue with is more use than one whose output is a number nobody can.

The final measure is the one the Riḍván 2010 message gave for Assemblies and which generalises to every institution and every scale: not the regularity of the meetings, not the efficiency of the functioning, but “the vitality of the spiritual and social life of the community it serves.” Anything that can be produced without the community changing is not evidence about the community.

Open questions / honest limits

  • No source specifies who formally “declares” a milestone passed, or how. The messages describe milestones as read, perceived and recognised; they do not establish a procedure, a deciding body, or a record. In practice the judgement appears to be reached in cluster reflection and institutional meetings and reported upward through Regional Councils or National Growth Committees, but this page has not found authoritative guidance fixing that process, and the absence may well be deliberate.
  • “An adequate degree of effectiveness” is not further defined, and the sources decline to quantify it. This page has not invented a threshold. The 17 January 2003 message’s own admission — that “the task of refining the criteria needed for valid assessments is proving to be an ongoing challenge to institutions” — was written twenty-three years ago and has not, so far as the corpus shows, been superseded by a more precise formulation.
  • The published glossaries disagree about the epochs of the Divine Plan. The glossary in Messages 1963–1986 gives the first epoch as 1937–63 with the second continuing; the glossary in Messages 1986–2001 maps four epochs onto 1937–46, 1946–63, 1963–86 and 1986–2001 — the periodisation of the Formative Age. The messages themselves are consistent with the former, and the Riḍván 2021 announcement of a third epoch of the Divine Plan confirms it. Readers encountering the latter should treat it as a compilation error rather than a competing account.
  • How a cluster is judged to have receded is much less documented than how it advances. Reflections on Growth 35 records the symptoms — agencies no longer meeting, ownership collapsing, statistics unreliable — but no guidance in this corpus states whether or how a cluster’s milestone status is revised downward, and the milestone vocabulary as published is asymmetric.
  • The evidence base for the field cases is the community’s own reporting. The Reflections on Growth corpus is internal, published by the International Teaching Centre, and consists of accounts contributed by participants. It is candid — its stalled-cluster issue is unusually so — but it is not independent, and no external ethnography of cluster milestone assessment exists that this page could weigh against it.
  • The external literature cited here describes a different kind of institution. Building State Capability concerns public administration in developing states; the resemblance to the form-versus-function problem in Bahá’í community life is analytical, not evidential, and nothing in it grounds a Bahá’í principle.
  • This page does not settle what a Local Spiritual Assembly should do when it judges its own community unready for a step the regional institutions are urging, how the milestone framework applies in countries where the Faith’s activity is restricted, or the relationship between third-milestone status and the eventual raising of a local Mashriqu’l-Adhkár (mashriqul-adhkar-unique-role).
