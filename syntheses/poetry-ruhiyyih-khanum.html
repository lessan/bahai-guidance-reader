Amatu’l-Bahá Rúḥíyyih Khánum as a poet

Last updated 17 October 2026

Amatu’l-Bahá Rúḥíyyih Khánum (born Mary Maxwell) was a Hand of the Cause of God and the wife of Shoghi Effendi. She wrote in English. She wrote poetry from the age of thirteen, but most of it was never printed. Three pieces can be identified from the corpus: the short poem “This Is Faith” (4 April 1954), the elegy “On Hearing of Enoch’s Murder” (17 September 1979), and the collection Poems of the Passing (George Ronald, 1996), written after the passing of Shoghi Effendi. Only the first two are available in full in the corpus; the book appears only in quoted lines. This page is part of the series introduced in Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

Her poems are not Bahá’í scripture and carry no authority in matters of belief (tier C). They are the work of a devoted Bahá’í, and this page reads them as that.

The poet

Background. Her wedding to Shoghi Effendi took place on 24 March 1937 in Haifa, and on that occasion he gave her the name Rúḥíyyih Khánum. Shoghi Effendi raised her to the rank of Hand of the Cause in 1952. A tribute by Violette Nakhjavani says that he had called her “my helpmate”, “my shield” and “my tireless collaborator in the arduous tasks I shoulder” (tribute in The Bahá’í World, 2000).

Beginnings. The scholar who interviewed her in Haifa on 17 February 1995 (the article in World Order gives only the initials SH) reports:

Amatu’l-Bahá began to write poetry at a very young age—thirteen—and was encouraged to continue doing so by winning a poetry prize (SH, Feb. 1995).

The same article says that, alongside her duties, she found time for writing that she called “more inspirational,” including a great deal of poetry (World Order S2 V31 I02). Poetry was one of several forms. The article lists plays (she began writing them at sixteen), didactic prose, literary criticism, history, biography, the epistle and film scripts.

Most of it unpublished. The same article states:

Unfortunately, the majority of her lyric poetry has never appeared in print, remaining a subterranean layer of private emotion in a very public life.

It adds that “for the time being” Poems of the Passing “stands as solitary testimony” to that inner life. The article was written after her death on 19 January 2000, and it says nothing of any later volume. Whether the manuscripts were later published is not shown by the corpus.

Shoghi Effendi’s reading of her poems. Two accounts of one event exist. In the 1995 interview she recalled that when he saw her copying some of her poems into a book, he asked to read them, and the next morning told her that some of them “made him weep” (World Order). In the tribute by Nakhjavani, which says Rúḥíyyih Khánum told it to the people around her, the words are different: “I read them all. They are beautiful, they made me cry.” Both accounts are her recollection, reported by others; neither is a written statement of the Guardian. No text in the corpus quotes Shoghi Effendi or the Universal House of Justice commenting on her poems themselves.

Her view of poetry. She said that poetry is a “marvelous emotional medium” and “can express things nothing else can express.” The interviewer notes that her published work does not usually take her own feelings as its subject: “her literary gifts have been wed to the dominating purpose of her life: service to her faith.” Poems of the Passing is the exception she describes: the poems “came out of the bottom of my sorrow.”

The three works

“This Is Faith” (4 April 1954)

The date comes from the tribute, which introduces the poem as “the outpouring of her heart years later” than her early years of marriage and says it “exemplifies the depth of her understanding” of faith. The World Order article gives the same date, citing her secretary, Nell Golden, and says the poem was “published most recently” in the tribute printed in Bahá’í Canada, May 2000, which implies earlier publication. Where it first appeared is not stated in the corpus.

It has five stanzas, each ending with the line “This is Faith.” Four of them are lists of paradoxes. The last turns to address God directly.

“On Hearing of Enoch’s Murder” (Limassol, 17 September 1979)

This is an elegy for Enoch Olinga, Hand of the Cause, killed in Kampala, Uganda, in 1979 with his wife and three of his children (The Bahá’í World XVIII records him as “murdered in most brutal circumstances in his own home”). It was printed in The Bahá’í World, volume XVIII (1979–1983), page 983, in a section headed “Verse.”

Attribution. One scanned copy makes the attribution look uncertain. The two copies show:

  • In [source] the OCR runs the page in an order that puts the heading “Tribute” immediately after the title, and the line crediting Rúḥíyyih Khánum with the place and date (“Limassol, Cyprus, 17 September 1979”) comes after a second, unrelated poem. A reader of that copy could attach the credit to the second poem.
  • In [source] the same page is in reading order. The credit follows the last line of the Enoch poem. Then comes “Tribute”, introduced as “a translation from Persian of a poem written by a Muslim woman who was a cellmate of one of the recent Bahá’í women martyrs,” whose name is withheld. That second poem ends “Translated by Hushmand Fatheazam.”

The World Order article independently gives the Enoch poem to her and the same place and date, citing her secretary. The attribution is therefore settled; the second poem is not hers.

Poems of the Passing (George Ronald, 1996)

The Bahá’í World volume XXV describes it as occasioned by “the sudden passing in 1957 of Shoghi Effendi” and as “this poetic expression of grief by his widow … which is now published for the first time” (136 pages). Volume XXVIII calls it “an outpouring of her broken heart.” The World Order article gives the purpose from the back cover, which she approved: “that in sharing [the poems] with others they may echo the grief of separation in this world from our loved ones and the confident hope of reunion with them in an eternal realm of spiritual progress and mercy.”

The poems are not in the corpus. The article quotes only a few lines. The poem “What Is the Use” is on page 24.

A poem for the men of the Andes

Bahá’í News reports that, at Machu Picchu, “a poem by The Hand of the Cause of God Rúḥíyyih ánum, which was dedicated to the men of the Andes and had been translated into Quechua, was recited.” Title, date and text are not given. (The word “Khánum” is damaged in the corpus copy.)

The text in English

All three works were written in English, so there is no translation. They are in copyright. The licence for printing any of them in a published lyric is permission-needed; the copyright holder is her estate or the publishers named above. The saved copy of “This Is Faith” is marked “Permission: fair use” by the site it came from, which is not a licence. The extracts below are short, for discussion only.

“This Is Faith”, first stanza (tribute in The Bahá’í World, 2000; also [source]):

To walk where there is no path To breathe where there is no air To see where there is no light – This is Faith.

Part of the third stanza:

To hold pebbles and see jewels To raise sticks and see forests

The last stanza turns to address God:

Hidden Thou art yet there! Veil Thy face and mute Thy tongue Yet I see and hear Thee, Love,

“On Hearing of Enoch’s Murder”, opening (The Bahá’í World XVIII, p. 983):

The sunlight is black The sunlight is black What raven wing Covered my sun at noonday?

and the farewell:

Go Enoch go! Go to Musa on the hill Go to your Master Go to your Guardian Go to the Kingdom of Light!

Poems of the Passing (as quoted in World Order): “The airs are cleft / The seas are dry, / Day is dark / And night burns.” The article also reports a “strange music” the poet “dare[s] not… name.”

Themes and images

“This Is Faith.” Faith is defined by what is not there: no path, no air, no light, no echo, no answer. Each stanza adds an act that continues anyway: to “believe and believe again and again,” to say “God, I believe” when others deny, to “smile with weeping eyes.” The final stanza changes from list to prayer. God is “Hidden” and “Veil Thy face”; the speaker is beaten “down to the bare earth” and still rises and loves. The poem does not say that the answer arrives. It describes faith as continuing without one. The tribute introduces it in connection with firmness in the Covenant.

“On Hearing of Enoch’s Murder.” Grief as darkness at midday (“The sunlight is black”), the taste of tears (“the salt of tears”), questions put to the murderers, and a command to the dead man to go to “your Master” and “your Guardian” in “the Kingdom of Light.” The poem also mourns Africa and addresses the murderers: it names the dead man “Abu’l-Futúh” and glosses it “The Father of Victories” (the OCR copies garble the first word). It does not call for revenge; the closing lines call for weeping.

Poems of the Passing. The World Order article describes a movement “from desolation to consolation, from halting denial to the heartfelt acceptance of loss,” ending in an affirmation of “the enduring life of the soul.” This is the article’s reading of the book as a whole, not something this page can check against the text.

What is not settled

  • Where and when “This Is Faith” was first printed. The corpus has the 2000 tribute and a note that it appeared “most recently” there.
  • Whether other poems of hers appeared in The Bahá’í World or Bahá’í News. No search for this page found any, but not every mention of her name was read.
  • The full text and first lines of Poems of the Passing. Only fragments are in the corpus.
  • The exact words of Shoghi Effendi on her poems. The two recollections differ (“weep” and “cry”), and both are her recollection.
  • The title and date of the Andes poem.
  • The licence for any use beyond short quotation: it requires permission from the rights holder.

For songwriting

These are notes. The poems are in copyright, so any lyric that quotes them needs permission from the rights holder.

Shortest exact phrases (source: “This Is Faith”; licence permission-needed, short extract only):

  • “To walk where there is no path”
  • “To hold pebbles and see jewels”
  • “To smile with weeping eyes”
  • “And believe again and again”
  • “Yet I rise and love Thee, Love!”

From “On Hearing of Enoch’s Murder” (permission-needed): “The sunlight is black”; “Go to the Kingdom of Light!”

Images. Path, air and light that are absent; pebbles and jewels, sticks and forests; the silence of the night; a veil on the face of God; bare earth; a black sun at noon; a raven’s wing; salt of tears; a jewelled crown rolled into a grave.

A possible arc (taken from “This Is Faith”): a series of short statements of what faith asks, each closing on one refrain; then a turn from description to direct address; then the speaker, struck down, rising. The refrain structure suits singing, and the poem’s own list could be paraphrased in new words without quoting it.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not present the poems as scripture or as words of Shoghi Effendi or of the House of Justice.
  • It must not say that Shoghi Effendi wrote or said anything about the poems beyond her own recollection that he was moved to tears.
  • It must not attribute the second poem on page 983 of The Bahá’í World XVIII (“Do you remember that you told me…”) to her.
  • It must not claim that “This Is Faith” describes the author’s own state at a particular event; the sources say only that she wrote it on 4 April 1954.
  • It must not use lines from Poems of the Passing beyond the fragments printed in the article without access to the book.
