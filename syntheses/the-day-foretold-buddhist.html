The Day Foretold: The Buddhist Promise of Maitreya, the Fifth Buddha

Last updated 17 October 2026

This page is one of a series on how the Bahá’í Writings relate the coming of Bahá’u’lláh to the promises of earlier religions. The hub is the-day-foretold; related pages are promises-of-the-future, the-day-foretold-hindu, the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-zoroastrian, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each tradition. For Buddhists he writes: “to the Buddhists the fifth Buddha” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11). A few lines later he names the prophecy: “He alone is meant by the prophecy attributed to Gautama Buddha Himself, that “a Buddha named Maitreye, the Buddha of universal fellowship” should, in the fullness of time, arise and reveal “His boundless glory.”” (God Passes By, GPB VI.15)

The page keeps four kinds of source apart: Buddhist scripture (public-domain translations), the Bahá’í Writings with the statements of Shoghi Effendi and the House of Justice, history, and Bahá’í commentary. The authoritative Bahá’í material is small: the House of Justice notes that Bahá’u’lláh made no mention of the Buddha, and the Guardian’s statement on the Buddhist prophecies is two sentences in God Passes By. Most of the page is therefore the Buddhist text, the commentary, and the record of people who became Bahá’ís from a Buddhist background.

1. The promise

The Pali texts: Metteyya, “even as I am now”

The Pali canon calls the coming Buddha Metteyya (Sanskrit Maitreya). The clearest canonical passage is in the Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Suttanta (Dīgha Nikāya 26), translated by T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids in Dialogues of the Buddha, Part III (1921; public domain). The Buddha describes a far future in which people have grown in virtue and in length of life. Then a righteous king, Sankha, will rule at Ketumati, the future Benares, “having conquered it not by the scourge, not by the sword, but by righteousness.” (Rhys Davids 1921, DN 26, para. 24)

The Buddha then speaks of the one who arises at that time:

“At that period, brethren, there will arise in the world an Exalted One named Metteyya, Arahant, Fully Awakened, abounding in wisdom and goodness, happy, with knowledge of the worlds, unsurpassed as a guide to mortals willing to be led, a teacher for gods and men, an Exalted One, a Buddha, even as I am now.” (Rhys Davids 1921, DN 26, para. 25)

The paragraph continues that he will proclaim “lovely in its origin, lovely in its progress” the truth, and “the higher life will he make known, in all its fulness and in all its purity, even as I do now.” It ends: “He will be accompanied by a congregation of some thousands of brethren, even as I am now accompanied by a congregation of some hundreds of brethren.” (DN 26, para. 25)

The next paragraph says that King Sankha will give away his palace, “hand it over as a gift to recluses and brahmins, to the destitute, wayfarers and beggars,” and “leave his home for the life that is homeless under Metteyya the Exalted One.” (DN 26, para. 26) The sutta closes with the Buddha’s instruction to his monks to “live with the Norm as your island, with the Norm as your refuge, take none other as your refuge.” (DN 26, para. 27)

The Buddha’s own words in this passage describe Metteyya as a Buddha of the same kind as himself, who will teach the same truth in full and pure form. They do not use the phrase “universal fellowship.”

In this passage the future Buddha appears in a time of prosperity, after a long decline in morals and length of life and a recovery (para. 23 says villages will then be so close that “a cock could fly from each one to the next”). Section 3 returns to this, because Bahá’í authors have disagreed about whether the texts place Metteyya in a dark age or a bright one.

The count: why Metteyya is the fifth

The Pali texts give a count that makes Metteyya the fifth Buddha of this aeon. In the Mahāpadāna Suttanta (Dīgha Nikāya 14), translated by T. W. Rhys Davids (Dialogues of the Buddha, Part II, 1910; public domain), the Buddha lists the Buddhas of the past. Three belong to earlier aeons: Vipassi, Sikhi and Vessabhu. Then, of this aeon:

“It was in this present auspicious aeon, brethren, that Kakusandha, the Exalted One, Arahant, Buddha Supreme, arose in the world. It was in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that Konagamana, … arose in the world. It was in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that Kassapa, … arose in the world. It is in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that now I, an Arahant, Buddha Supreme, have arisen in the world.” (Rhys Davids 1910, DN 14, para. 4)

Gotama is the fourth Buddha of this aeon. A later text, the Anāgatavaṃsa (“History of Future Events”), puts Metteyya next in the same aeon. In Henry Clarke Warren’s Buddhism in Translations (1896, § 102; public domain), the Buddha answers Sāriputta’s question about his successor:

“Our cycle is a happy one, Three Leaders have already lived, Kakusandha, Kouagamaua, And eke the leader Kassapa. The Buddha now Supreme am I, But after me Metteyya comes, While still this happy cycle lasts” (Warren 1896, § 102, from the Anāgatavaṃsa)

(“Kouagamaua” is an OCR error for Konāgamana in the saved copy.) Three leaders, then Gotama, then Metteyya: Metteyya is the fifth Buddha of this aeon. This count comes from the Buddhist texts. Shoghi Effendi’s phrase is “the fifth Buddha”; he does not give a count.

The Anāgatavaṃsa: five disappearances

The same text, in the account by J. Minayeff that Warren translates, describes how the teaching will fade after the Buddha’s death in five stages: the disappearance of the attainments, the method, learning, the symbols and the relics. At the last stage, when “the dispensation has disappeared, the relics will come from every place”, gather at the great Bo-tree, “make an effigy of The Buddha and perform a miracle resembling the double-miracle, and will teach the Doctrine.” (Warren 1896, § 102) This passage is the basis of the “Twin-miracle” reading described in section 3. It is not in the Writings.

The Metteyya of Carus: “he whose name is kindness”

The Gospel of Buddha (1894) is Paul Carus’s compilation of Buddhist material into one narrative, not a translation of one text. Its chapter XCVI (public domain) joins the setting of the Buddha’s last days to a Metteyya passage; its source note reads “MPN, v, 1-14, concerning Metteyya see EH s.v. RhDB, pp. 180, 200”, that is, the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta for the setting, with the Metteyya material drawn from elsewhere. Ānanda asks who will teach after the Buddha:

“I am not the first Buddha who came upon earth, nor shall I be the last. In due time another Buddha will arise in the world, a Holy One, a supremely enlightened One” (Carus 1894, ch. XCVI, para. 13)

Ānanda asks, “How shall we know him?” The Blessed One answers: “He will be known as Metteyya, which means ‘he whose name is kindness.’” (Carus 1894, ch. XCVI, paras. 14 and 15) Metteyya and Maitreya come from mettā, maitrī, friendliness, so this is the saved text closest to the meaning of the name. A Star of the West article of August 1931 retells the scene: “He will be known as ‘Maitreyeh,’” which means kindness. (vol. 22, no. 5; author not identified here)

2. The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The title: the fifth Buddha, Maitreya

Shoghi Effendi’s statements are the two already quoted. He writes that Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is, “to the Buddhists the fifth Buddha” (GPB VI.11), and that “He alone is meant by the prophecy” of the Buddha Maitreya (GPB VI.15). Both come in the passage in which he names, tradition by tradition, what the Day of Bahá’u’lláh is to each. The words “the fifth Buddha” are his; the Writings do not explain the number.

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 24 June 1947 describes how much the Guardian has said: “There is no mention in the Bahá’í writings of any connection between the Near Eastern and Far Eastern Prophets. There are a very few references made to Buddha, which you have evidently seen. In “God Passes By” you will find that Bahá’u’lláh is the return of the Fifth Buddha, etc., and this is all the information the Guardian has, on this subject of Bahá’u’lláh’s fulfilling Buddhistic prophecies, at present.” This is a letter by a secretary on the Guardian’s behalf, not the Guardian’s own text.

The wording of God Passes By VI.15: what can be traced

Shoghi Effendi puts the whole clause “a Buddha named Maitreye, the Buddha of universal fellowship” in quotation marks, and says the prophecy is “attributed to” the Buddha. Searching the Pali translations and the Bahá’í sources saved in this repository gives the following.

The first clause has a source. Mrs. C. A. F. Rhys Davids wrote in Buddhism: a study of the Buddhist norm (Home University Library, London, 1912), p. 243: “But that a Buddha named Metteyya should in the fulness of time arise, is recorded in the Canon as a prophecy made by Gotama himself.” The same sentence is quoted, with a footnote to “Mrs. Rhys Davis, Buddhism”, in George Townshend’s article “Constructive Religion” in The Bahá’í World, vol. 8 (the volume for 1938–1940; Part Four, “Articles and Reviews”, no. 1): “but we are told “that a Buddha named Metteyya should in the fullness of time arise is recorded in the Canon as a prophecy made by Gotama himself.”” Townshend goes on to quote Rhys Davids’s next argument, that if a Metteyya Buddha “arose here and now he would recast their Dhamma”. An article of the same title and text, listed under the initials “A. G. B.”, is in World Order for March 1939; this page did not resolve the difference in bylines.

The words match. Shoghi Effendi does not give his source, so it is likely, not proved, that he drew on Townshend’s article or on Mrs. Rhys Davids directly. The earliest printing of the Guardian’s wording found is God Passes By (1944).

The epithet is not traced. “The Buddha of universal fellowship” and “His boundless glory” were not found in Mrs. Rhys Davids (1912), Carus (1894), T. W. Rhys Davids’s Buddhism (1894 edition, checked from a copy that is not saved), Warren’s § 102, DN 26, or any Bahá’í text before 1944 that was searched. Other Bahá’í sources give other renderings without a source: William Hatcher calls Maitreya “the Buddha of universal righteousness”, and a World Order article by Keith de Folo on village Buddhists in Ceylon calls him “the long-awaited Buddha of Universal Love.” The meaning of the name supports “friendliness” or “kindness” (Carus). “Fellowship” may be Shoghi Effendi’s own rendering of that meaning. That is a possibility; no text confirms it.

The spelling. He writes “Maitreye”, with a final “e”. The Pali form is Metteyya and the Sanskrit form is Maitreya. No reason for the spelling was found.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the prophecies are in symbols

‘Abdu’l-Bahá speaks about the Buddhist books in a Tablet. The compilation Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster and Related Subjects gives it as no. 3, from Tablets of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Abbas, vol. 3, p. 565:

“There are prophecies concerning this Manifestation in the Buddhistic books, but they are in symbols and metaphors, and some spiritual conditions are mentioned therein, but the leaders of religion do not understand. They think these prophecies are material things; yet those signs are foreshadowing spiritual occurrences.”

In a talk in New York on 5 July 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, he says that the Day when “the Promised One of all the Books would appear” and “the banner of the Most Great Peace and conciliation be hoisted” is recorded in “the Torah, or Old Testament, in the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta, and the books of Buddha.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.1)

The Buddha as a Manifestation of God

The Bahá’í texts also state that the Buddha was a Manifestation of God. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Some Answered Questions, answers a question on the two kinds of Prophets: “Buddha also established a new religion and Confucius renewed the ancient conduct and morals, but the original precepts have been entirely changed and their followers no longer adhere to the original pattern of belief and worship. The founder of Buddhism was a precious Being Who established the oneness of God” (Some Answered Questions, SAQ 43.8). In a talk of 7 October 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, he names the Buddha among the “Blessed souls—whether Moses, Jesus, Zoroaster, Krishna, Buddha, Confucius or Muḥammad” who “were the cause of the illumination of the world of humanity.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 109.11)

In London, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá told a story of the Buddha and his disciples. This is from the compilation’s selection from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in London: Addresses and Notes of Conversations (1987, pp. 63–64), so it is a record of what he said, taken down by others. The Buddha asked his disciples what they would do if people refused them, then mocked, imprisoned and killed them. At each step they said they would be thankful that it was not worse. At the last: “What more glorious fate is there than this, to die for the glory of God?” And the Buddha said: “Well done!”

Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (by his secretaries, not the Guardian’s own words) say the same about the Buddha’s station. On 26 December 1941 (to the National Spiritual Assembly of Australia and New Zealand): “The Buddha was a Manifestation of God, like Christ, but His followers do not possess His authentic writings.” Another letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (undated in the compilation) says that Buddha and Zoroaster were “both divinely appointed and fully independent Manifestations of God.” A letter of 4 October 1950 adds: “Buddha is mentioned, and Zoroaster, in our Scriptures — both non-Jewish Prophets or non Semitic Prophets.”

The House of Justice on the Buddha

A letter of 27 October 1986 is from the Department of the Secretariat, to an individual, on the instruction of the Universal House of Justice. It is not a letter by the House of Justice itself, and its subject is the equality of women and men. In the course of it, the letter says: “Bahá’u’lláh made no mention of Buddha, and if it had not been for ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s statement we would not have been in a position to state deﬁnitely that Buddha had been a Manifestation of God.” (Messages from the Universal House of Justice 1986–2001, no. 7) The letter does not discuss the Guardian’s identification of Bahá’u’lláh with Maitreya.

The Bahá’í International Community’s summary

Bahá’u’lláh (1992), a statement of the Bahá’í International Community prepared at the request of the Universal House of Justice, lists the titles that Bahá’u’lláh’s followers give Him in other faiths, and has “to Buddhism, the Maitreya Buddha.” It is a summary, not an interpretation.

3. How Bahá’í authors have read the prophecies

None of the readings in this section is in the Writings. They are the work of individual authors, and they differ. Conner (below) observes that the Buddhist canon, unlike the Bible or Muslim tradition, “yields no specific predictions” that can be read as referring to Bahá’u’lláh, and the disagreement among the authors reflects that.

Jamshed Fozdar: Maitrya-Amitabha and the Twin-miracle

Jamshed K. Fozdar argues in Buddha Maitrya-Amitabha Has Appeared (Fozdar calls it a 500-page thesis) that Bahá’u’lláh is the promised Buddha. Fozdar’s shorter paper “The Revivification of the Buddha’s Dharma” gives the outline: every religion begins “like the dawn lighting up the night sky”, reaches its noon and sets, and “the night of the spirit follows to await yet another Avatar, a new Buddha-rising to bring forth a new awakening, new hope.” Only “the advent of the promised Buddha Maitrya-Amitabha” can restore the Dharma, Fozdar writes.

Others describe two features of the book. According to Bruce Burrill, Fozdar reads the “Twin-miracle” in the Anāgatavaṃsa’s account of the relics as “the contemporaneous appearance of Two Avatars”, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh, over four chapters. According to Jonah Winters, Fozdar “spends over 100 pages providing detailed and exact calculations” of events in Bahá’u’lláh’s life that Fozdar says the Buddha foretold.

Burrill disputed this in the Talisman discussion of 1995 (an informal listserver exchange, not an edited essay). Burrill argues that Fozdar’s claim that the Anāgatavaṃsa is the “oldest passage” attributable to the Buddha has no source, that in the Pali sentence the relics are the subject and not two Avatars, and that “relics” and “twin-miracle” have specific meanings in Pali literature. Juan Cole replied in 1996 that Fozdar’s approach and Momen’s should not be lumped together. This page records the disagreement and does not decide it.

Moojan Momen: the Pali passage, and what a Bahá’í reading needs

Momen’s encyclopedia article “Buddhism and the Bahá’í Faith” (saved from momen.org) states the position as belief: “Bahá’ís consider that Bahá’u’lláh is the fulfillment of the prophecy of the Buddha that in due time another Buddha would come to the world, the Mettaya (Maitreya) Buddha.” He quotes the DN 26 passage in a translation that differs from the Rhys Davids text above, and cites it as “Mahaparinibbana-Suttana 3:76” (see section 6). Momen adds that Mahayana sources have “many more prophecies”, including one that places Maitreya five epochs of five hundred years after the Buddha, a period that “was completed in 1956 C.E. according to the traditional Buddhist calendar”. Momen says the Maitreya prophecy of “an era of universal peace and tranquillity” is regarded by Bahá’ís as fulfilled by Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings on world peace, and that Bahá’ís compare the name Amitabha, “Light of the Infinite”, to Bahá’u’lláh, “Glory or Light of God”.

Winters’s review of Momen’s book says that Momen keeps to the Pali canon, which “is taken by almost all schools of Buddhism to be normative”, and shows only “that the future Buddha would appear sometime before AD 1943”. Winters adds that many of Momen’s quotations and calculations come from Fozdar, to whom Momen acknowledges a debt.

The 1998 exchange on the time of Metteyya. In the Talisman dialogue Burrill wrote to Momen in January 1998 that the sutta does not support the statement in Momen’s book that the world is degraded when Metteyya comes: “Once the life span reaches 10 years, some individual put a stop to the immorality and the life span increases to 80,000 years, and it is in the period of morality and prosperity that Metteyya comes, not the degraded state of the world.” Momen answered on 10 February 1998: “I think you have a valid point here.” Momen gave three lines of response.

  1. Scriptural: other traditions, such as the Five Disappearances in the Anāgatavaṃsa, “undoubtedly state that the Buddha will appear at the time when the world is in a degraded state”.
  2. Interpretive: Bahá’ís hold that where a prophecy “appears to refer to events that are contra-indicated by science”, a spiritual meaning is likely. The example given is Bahá’u’lláh’s reading of the falling stars in the Kitáb-i-Íqán. The 80,000-year life spans may call for the same, but Momen “would not like to advance an interpretation” without a thorough study.
  3. Historical: many Buddhists have expected Maitreya in a dark time, some taking the nineteenth or early twentieth century, about 2,500 years after the Buddha, to be that time. Momen cites “perhaps as many as a dozen millennialist movements” from Burma to China, and sees two Buddhist traditions on the timing, like pre-and post-millennialist views in Christianity.

Momen closes by saying that Momen has “no great interest in the question of prophesy” and included it “for reasons of completeness”. This is Momen’s own view, not a Bahá’í position.

Daniel Conner (World Order, Winter 1971–72)

Conner’s article “Buddhism and the Baha’i Faith” says that Buddhism “has an apocalyptic tradition and expects the return of its Founder, although there is evidence that this concept arose much later than the Buddha’s lifetime and is not attributable to him.” Some Mahayana sects take the return to be of the Buddha’s spirit and not his physical presence, which “makes it easier for many Buddhists to accept the appearance of the Maitreya in circumstances other than those which are traditional.” Conner calls some Maitreya texts “obviously allegorical, even fantastic”, and offers Nichiren’s scheme of three periods of about a thousand years, which in his dating puts a new era “at hand”. Conner concludes that Bahá’u’lláh “may be accepted as the Maitreya awaited by the Buddhists, in that He is the ‘Bringer of a New Law,’ ‘One who Brings Peace,’ and ‘One who Enlightens and Unites the World.’” This argues from the meaning of the titles to the Bahá’í teachings. It does not rest on a verse in the Pali.

Coleman and Warwick: popular statements

Two short popular presentations repeat the identification without argument. Coleman’s Common Grounds between Buddhism, Quantum Physics, and the Bahá’í Faith calls Bahá’u’lláh “the fifth Buddha, the tenth Avatar after Krishna” and “the Buddha Maitrya-Amitabaha”; the identification with Amitabha is not in the Writings. The Warwick Bahá’í Bookshop leaflet “Buddhism and the Bahá’í Faith” (2002) says Bahá’ís “recognise Him as the Fifth Buddha, the ‘Buddha of Universal Fellowship’” and follows the Carus wording of the Buddha’s reply.

Winters on the scholarly view of the Buddha

Winters’s review states a difficulty. Winters writes that “Both Buddhists and scholars of Buddhism are practically unanimous in believing that the Buddha felt himself to be an ordinary human”, and that for him “to have prophesied a future world-redeemer such as Maitrya Buddha would have been entirely out of character with his teachings.” Winters adds that some Bahá’í scholars hold this view too. This is Winters’s account of the scholarly picture. The Bahá’í texts place the Buddha among the Manifestations of God; this page records both accounts.

4. Those who recognised it

The sources for this section are history, not the Writings. None of them says that a person became a Bahá’í because of the Maitreya promise. Where an account gives a reason, it is stated here, and where it gives none, none is supplied.

Burma. Shoghi Effendi writes of the early years of the Faith in the East that the first fruits of itinerant teachers in Persia, India and Burma included “the conversion of a considerable number of the adherents of the Jewish, Zoroastrian and Buddhist Faiths” (God Passes By, GPB XI.30). Later, describing the expansion of Bahá’í activity in India and Burma, he writes of “a steadily growing community, now including among its members representatives of the Zoroastrian, the Islamic, the Hindu and the Buddhist Faiths, as well as members of the Sikh community,” reaching “as far as Mandalay and the village of Daidanaw Kalazoo, in the Hanthawaddy district of Burma, at which latter place no less than eight hundred Bahá’ís resided, possessing a school, a court, and a hospital of their own” (God Passes By, GPB XX.17).

Jamál Effendi and Siyyid Muṣṭafá Rúmí. According to a brief history of the Faith in Myanmar (from thebahaifaith-myanmar.com, unattributed), “The first Bahá’í teacher sent by Bahá’u’lláh to India and Burma was Jamal Effendi. He arrived in India in 1876 and met Siyyid Mustafa Roumi’s family, who were settled in Madras.” The two went on to Mandalay, then the capital, where, the same history says, they stayed with a silk merchant, Abdu’l-Sattar (U Koo), whose nephew Ko Thwin had heard of them. “U Koo and his nephew became the first believers in Mandalay.” The history does not say what religion U Koo held, and the first believers in Mandalay should not be assumed to be Buddhists. Momen’s encyclopedia article says that “the first converts were probably a number of Buddhists in Burma who were taught by Sayyid Mustafa Rumi in about 1905”, and that large-scale conversions followed in the 1960s and 1970s in Southeast Asia, “particularly in Vietnam, where some 100,000 people became Baha’is.” That is Momen’s summary, without sources named.

Sprague in Mandalay. Sydney Sprague visited the communities in A Year With the Bahá’ís of India and Burma (1908). In Mandalay Sprague found meetings held in a private house, “that of a Burmese widow where I was staying,” too small for the number who came twice a week, so that some met out of doors. Women took part: “in Mandalay the Buddhist women have always been accustomed to a good deal of liberty and freedom, and now that they have become Bahá’ís they naturally do not abandon that.” Sprague’s book is a traveller’s account. It does not say what moved each person, nor that Maitreya was the reason.

Mandalay, 1952. Bahá’í News no. 259 (September 1952) prints a Mandalay report on the year’s teaching. Of the public meetings on Holy Days it says: “One great feature of these meetings was that they were attended by large numbers of Buddhist monks who asked very intelligent questions regarding the teachings of the Faith.” The report records attendance and questions. It does not record enrolments.

Burma, 1964. A photograph caption in Bahá’í News no. 408 (March 1965) shows the Bahá’ís of Rangoon listening to Hand of the Cause Collis Featherstone on 19 November 1964 and says: “Over 2000 new believers of Buddhist background have recently embraced the Faith in Burma.” The caption gives a number and no reasons.

Japan. According to The Bahá’í World, vol. 2 (1926–1928), the 1920 Japanese edition of a small compilation of the teachings was made by “two Japanese friends, one a Buddhist priest and the other a former Christian evangelist”. The report quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s message to the two: “likewise a priest lives there, endowed with great capacity”, and a hope that they “may shine like unto two heavenly stars from the horizon of Japan and may be the cause of its enlightenment.” It does not name the priest. The Bahá’í World, vol. 7 (1936–1938), in a travel letter, names “Mr. D. Inouye, the Buddhist priest who is a devoted Baha’i” who translated Bahá’u’lláh and the New Era into Japanese. Vol. 11 (1946–1950) calls Daiun Inouye “a Baha’i who was formerly a Buddhist priest”, and says ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s first Tablet to Tokujiro Torii sent love to Inouye and his friend with the same words about two heavenly stars. These sources give no reasons for his becoming a Bahá’í.

Malaysia. The obituary of Choo Yeok Boon in The Bahá’í World, vol. 17 (1976–1979), from the Spiritual Assembly of the Bahá’ís of Malaysia, says he accepted the Faith in the early 1960s. It gives one reason for his delay and one for his decision. He “postponed officially affiliating with the Baha’i community through loyalty to his mother who had vowed that he would shave his head and serve as a monk for one month at the Buddhist temple.” Then, “when one of the Baha’is assured him that there was no conflict arising from his obligation to his mother and his service to the Baha’i community he immediately declared his acceptance of the Faith of Bahá’u’lláh”. The account does not mention Maitreya.

The saved sources give no account in which a Buddhist became a Bahá’í because of the Maitreya promise.

5. The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

These notes are descriptions from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0), saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/buddhist/`. They are for choosing an idiom, not for lyrics.

  • Buddhist music in general (Wikipedia, “Buddhist music”): “Most Buddhist music includes chanting or singing, accompanied by instruments.” The texts chanted include sutras, mantras, parittas and verse compositions. Instruments range “from solely relying on the human voice, to many types of classic instruments used in Asian music (such as the ancient Indian veena).”
  • Theravada chant: usually in Pali, “sometimes with vernacular translations interspersed.” A common form is paritta (pirit) chanting, “one of the oldest forms of Buddhist chanting,” including the Metta Sutta on loving-kindness. Parittas (Wikipedia, “Paritta”) are verses recited aloud for protection; all-night recitations by monks are believed to bring “safety, peace and well-being to a community.”
  • Myanmar: the same article describes a modern genre of Buddhist songs, dhamma thachin, composed in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries and sung at monasteries and festivals; some use “traditional Burmese instruments and vocal stylings associated with the Mahāgīta, a genre of Burmese classical music.”
  • Japanese shōmyō (Wikipedia, “Shōmyō”): chant used mainly in the Tendai and Shingon traditions, using “the yo scale, a pentatonic scale,” passed on orally from teacher to student.

The Mandalay history above is Burmese, so the Theravada and Myanmar descriptions are the closest fit to it.

6. What the sources do not settle

How much the Guardian said. The letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi of 24 June 1947 says the God Passes By passage “is all the information the Guardian has” on the fulfilment of Buddhist prophecies. The Bahá’í texts give no verses, no dates and no further identification.

The source of the Guardian’s wording (partly settled). The first clause, “a Buddha named … should … in the fulness of time arise … a prophecy made by Gotama himself”, is in Mrs. Rhys Davids (1912, p. 243), and the same sentence is in Townshend’s Bahá’í World article (vol. 8). That the Guardian drew on one of them is likely and is not proved. Still unsettled: where “the Buddha of universal fellowship” and “His boundless glory” come from. They are not in Carus, either Rhys Davids, Warren, or DN 26 as saved here, and no Bahá’í text before 1944 was found with them. It is also not settled why he spells “Maitreye”. Nor is it known whether the Guardian meant “universal fellowship” as a translation of the name Maitreya or as a title taken from somewhere else.

The Mahāparinibbāna citation. Momen cites the Metteyya passage as “Mahaparinibbana-Suttana 3:76”. The saved Rhys Davids DN 26 prints the passage at page 76 of Dialogues of the Buddha vol. 3 (the page break “[76]” falls just before “there will arise in the world an Exalted One named Metteyya”), so “3:76” may mean that page. This is a guess: Momen’s wording differs from that translation, and DN 16, the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, is not among the saved texts, so it was not checked. The sutta in the saved texts that names Metteyya is DN 26.

The time of Metteyya. DN 26 places Metteyya in an age of prosperity after a recovery from moral decline; the Five Disappearances describe the decline of the teaching before him. Momen’s 1998 reply says both traditions exist. The Writings do not say which a Bahá’í reading should follow, or whether the long numbers (80,000 years) are literal.

The Twin-miracle. The reading as the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh is Fozdar’s. The Writings do not make it, and Burrill disputes it.

The authenticity of the scriptures. Two letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi bear on the Buddhist texts in section 1. On 26 December 1941: the Buddha’s followers “do not possess His authentic writings.” On 25 November 1950: “We cannot be sure of the authenticity of the scriptures of Buddha and Krishna.” The Pali texts are therefore the Buddhist community’s own scripture, not texts a Bahá’í source authenticates word for word. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s statement is that the Buddhist books contain prophecies in “symbols and metaphors.”

Buddhist views. This page does not say how Buddhist teachers or institutions understand Metteyya, and does not claim that they accept the Bahá’í reading. Winters writes that to accept the Bahá’í interpretation of the Buddha’s teaching, Buddhism “will have to be radically reevaluated”.

Concrete images from the sources.

  • The twin sāla trees “full of bloom with flowers out of season” at the Buddha’s last bed (Carus 1894, ch. XCVI, para. 3).
  • Ānanda asking “How shall we know him?” and the answer that the name is kindness (Carus, paras. 14 and 15).
  • A royal city, Ketumati, with villages so close that a cock could fly from each to the next; a king who rules by righteousness and gives his palace to the destitute (DN 26, paras. 23, 24 and 26).
  • The relics gathering at the great Bo-tree to make an effigy of the Buddha (Warren 1896, § 102).
  • A village Buddhist in Ceylon whose “cherished desire is to be reborn for the last time in the age of the Maitreya Buddha” (Keith de Folo, World Order; a magazine description, not scripture).
  • A house in Mandalay too small for those who came, with the overflow meeting outdoors (Sprague 1908); monks at the public meetings asking “very intelligent questions” (Bahá’í News no. 259).
  • Daidanaw Kalazoo with a school, a court and a hospital of its own (GPB XX.17).
  • A young man who waits to join because his mother vowed he would shave his head and serve a month as a monk (Bahá’í World vol. 17).
  • Two men in Japan who “may shine like unto two heavenly stars from the horizon of Japan” (Bahá’í World vol. 2).
  • The disciples thankful at each step down to martyrdom, and the Buddha saying “Well done!” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá in London).

Two possible arcs.

  1. The question and the answer. Ānanda asks who will teach after the Buddha, and the answer is a name that means kindness. A long wait. A house in Mandalay too small for those who came. The last line returns to the name, now with a place and a people.
  2. The righteous king. A king conquers “not by the scourge, not by the sword, but by righteousness” and gives his palace away. The same line carries into a village school and hospital in Burma, and ends on peace as something the Buddha’s own promise already holds.

A lyric must not:

  • say that Buddhist teachers or institutions accept that Bahá’u’lláh is Maitreya;
  • attribute the phrase “Buddha of universal fellowship” to a Pali text, or give it a Pali source;
  • say that the Writings name Amitabha, give dates for Maitreya, or interpret the Twin-miracle;
  • say that Bahá’u’lláh named the Buddha;
  • say that the Pali scriptures are authentic word for word, or that the Bahá’í Writings explain Maitreya’s thousand-year signs;
  • present Carus’s “he whose name is kindness” as a line from the Pali canon;
  • say that any of the converts named above became Bahá’ís because of the Maitreya promise;
  • put the Buddhist community’s current teachings in the Bahá’í voice, or call Buddhist practice a corruption (the Writings’ statements on this are not for a song to repeat).
