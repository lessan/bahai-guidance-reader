The Day Foretold: The Buddhist Promise of Maitreya, the Fifth Buddha

Last updated 16 October 2026

This page is one of a series on how the Bahá’í Writings relate the coming of Bahá’u’lláh to the promises of earlier religions. The hub is the-day-foretold; related pages are promises-of-the-future, the-day-foretold-hindu, the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-zoroastrian, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-islamic and the-day-foretold-babi.

In God Passes By, Shoghi Effendi lists what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each tradition. For Buddhists he writes: “to the Buddhists the fifth Buddha” (God Passes By, GPB VI.11). A few lines later he names the prophecy: “He alone is meant by the prophecy attributed to Gautama Buddha Himself, that “a Buddha named Maitreye, the Buddha of universal fellowship” should, in the fullness of time, arise and reveal “His boundless glory.”” (God Passes By, GPB VI.15)

The page keeps four kinds of source apart: the Buddhist scriptures (public-domain English translations), the Bahá’í Writings and the Guardian’s statements, history, and scholarship. The Bahá’í texts on this subject are few. Section 5 states what they leave open.

1. The promise

The Pali texts: Metteyya, “even as I am now”

The Pali canon calls the coming Buddha Metteyya (Sanskrit Maitreya). The clearest canonical passage is in the Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Suttanta (Dīgha Nikāya 26), translated by T. W. and C. A. F. Rhys Davids in Dialogues of the Buddha, Part III (1921; public domain). The Buddha describes a far future in which people have grown in virtue and in length of life. Then a righteous king, Sankha, will rule at Ketumati, the future Benares, “having conquered it not by the scourge, not by the sword, but by righteousness.” (Rhys Davids 1921, DN 26, para. 24)

The Buddha then speaks of the one who arises at that time:

“At that period, brethren, there will arise in the world an Exalted One named Metteyya, Arahant, Fully Awakened, abounding in wisdom and goodness, happy, with knowledge of the worlds, unsurpassed as a guide to mortals willing to be led, a teacher for gods and men, an Exalted One, a Buddha, even as I am now.” (Rhys Davids 1921, DN 26, para. 25)

(The saved copy is OCR of the printed book; two line-break and punctuation slips in this sentence were corrected by hand and are noted in the file’s header.) The paragraph continues that he will proclaim “lovely in its origin, lovely in its progress” the truth, and “the higher life will he make known, in all its fulness and in all its purity, even as I do now.” It ends: “He will be accompanied by a congregation of some thousands of brethren, even as I am now accompanied by a congregation of some hundreds of brethren.” (DN 26, para. 25)

The next paragraph says that King Sankha will give away his palace, “hand it over as a gift to recluses and brahmins, to the destitute, wayfarers and beggars,” and “leave his home for the life that is homeless under Metteyya the Exalted One.” (DN 26, para. 26)

The Buddha’s own words in this passage describe Metteyya as a Buddha of the same kind as himself, who will teach the same truth in full and pure form. They do not use the phrase “universal fellowship.”

The count: why Metteyya is the fifth

The Pali texts give a count that makes Metteyya the fifth Buddha of this aeon. In the Mahāpadāna Suttanta (Dīgha Nikāya 14), translated by T. W. Rhys Davids (Dialogues of the Buddha, Part II, 1910; public domain), the Buddha lists the Buddhas of the past. Three belong to earlier aeons: Vipassi, Sikhi and Vessabhu. Then, of this aeon:

“It was in this present auspicious aeon, brethren, that Kakusandha, the Exalted One, Arahant, Buddha Supreme, arose in the world. It was in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that Konagamana, … arose in the world. It was in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that Kassapa, … arose in the world. It is in this auspicious aeon, brethren, that now I, an Arahant, Buddha Supreme, have arisen in the world.” (Rhys Davids 1910, DN 14, para. 4)

Kakusandha, Konāgamana, Kassapa and Gotama are four Buddhas of this aeon, and Gotama is the fourth. A later text, the Anāgatavaṃsa (“History of Future Events”), puts Metteyya next in the same aeon. In Henry Clarke Warren’s Buddhism in Translations (1896, § 102; public domain), the Buddha answers Sāriputta’s question about his successor:

“Our cycle is a happy one, Three Leaders have already lived, Kakusandha, Kouagamaua, And eke the leader Kassapa. The Buddha now Supreme am I, But after me Metteyya comes, While still this happy cycle lasts” (Warren 1896, § 102, from the Anāgatavaṃsa)

(“Kouagamaua” is an OCR error for Konāgamana in the saved copy.) Three leaders, then Gotama, then Metteyya: Metteyya is the fifth Buddha of this aeon. This count comes from the Buddhist texts. Shoghi Effendi’s phrase is “the fifth Buddha”; he does not give a count.

Where the Bahá’í texts quote the promise

The only Bahá’í rendering of the promise is Shoghi Effendi’s, quoted above: “a Buddha named Maitreye, the Buddha of universal fellowship,” who will “reveal “His boundless glory.”” He introduces it as “the prophecy attributed to Gautama Buddha Himself” (God Passes By, GPB VI.15). The saved Pali passages do not contain the words “universal fellowship” or “boundless glory.” The source of the Guardian’s wording is not identified (section 5).

2. The fulfilment in the Bahá’í Writings

The title: the fifth Buddha, Maitreya

Shoghi Effendi’s statements are the two already quoted. He writes that Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is, “to the Buddhists the fifth Buddha” (GPB VI.11), and that “He alone is meant by the prophecy” of the Buddha Maitreya (GPB VI.15). Both come in the passage in which he names, tradition by tradition, what the Day of Bahá’u’lláh is to each.

A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 24 June 1947 describes how much the Guardian has said: “There is no mention in the Bahá’í writings of any connection between the Near Eastern and Far Eastern Prophets. There are a very few references made to Buddha, which you have evidently seen. In “God Passes By” you will find that Bahá’u’lláh is the return of the Fifth Buddha, etc., and this is all the information the Guardian has, on this subject of Bahá’u’lláh’s fulfilling Buddhistic prophecies, at present.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the prophecies are in symbols

‘Abdu’l-Bahá speaks about the Buddhist books in a Tablet. The compilation Buddha, Krishna, Zoroaster and Related Subjects gives it as no. 3, from Tablets of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá Abbas, vol. 3, p. 565:

“There are prophecies concerning this Manifestation in the Buddhistic books, but they are in symbols and metaphors, and some spiritual conditions are mentioned therein, but the leaders of religion do not understand. They think these prophecies are material things; yet those signs are foreshadowing spiritual occurrences.”

In a talk in New York on 5 July 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, he says that the Day when “the Promised One of all the Books would appear” and “the banner of the Most Great Peace and conciliation be hoisted” is recorded in “the Torah, or Old Testament, in the Gospel, the Qur’án, the Zend-Avesta, and the books of Buddha.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 80.1)

The Buddha as a Manifestation of God

The Bahá’í texts also state that the Buddha was a Manifestation of God. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Some Answered Questions, answers a question on the two kinds of Prophets: “Buddha also established a new religion and Confucius renewed the ancient conduct and morals, but the original precepts have been entirely changed and their followers no longer adhere to the original pattern of belief and worship. The founder of Buddhism was a precious Being Who established the oneness of God” (Some Answered Questions, SAQ 43.8). In a talk of 7 October 1912, from notes of a talk by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, he names the Buddha among the “Blessed souls—whether Moses, Jesus, Zoroaster, Krishna, Buddha, Confucius or Muḥammad” who “were the cause of the illumination of the world of humanity.” (The Promulgation of Universal Peace, PUP 109.11)

Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi say the same. On 26 December 1941: “The Buddha was a Manifestation of God, like Christ, but His followers do not possess His authentic writings.” Another letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (undated in the compilation) says that Buddha and Zoroaster were “both divinely appointed and fully independent Manifestations of God.” A letter of 4 October 1950 adds: “Buddha is mentioned, and Zoroaster, in our Scriptures — both non-Jewish Prophets or non Semitic Prophets.”

The Bahá’í International Community’s summary

Bahá’u’lláh (1992), a statement of the Bahá’í International Community prepared at the request of the Universal House of Justice, lists the titles that Bahá’u’lláh’s followers give Him in other faiths, and has “to Buddhism, the Maitreya Buddha.” It is a summary, not an interpretation.

3. Those who recognised it

The sources for this section are history, not the Writings.

Burma. Shoghi Effendi writes of the early years of the Faith in the East that the first fruits of itinerant teachers in Persia, India and Burma included “the conversion of a considerable number of the adherents of the Jewish, Zoroastrian and Buddhist Faiths” (God Passes By, GPB XI.30). Later, describing the expansion of Bahá’í activity in India and Burma, he writes of “a steadily growing community, now including among its members representatives of the Zoroastrian, the Islamic, the Hindu and the Buddhist Faiths, as well as members of the Sikh community,” reaching “as far as Mandalay and the village of Daidanaw Kalazoo, in the Hanthawaddy district of Burma, at which latter place no less than eight hundred Bahá’ís resided, possessing a school, a court, and a hospital of their own” (God Passes By, GPB XX.17).

Jamál Effendi and Siyyid Muṣṭafá Rúmí. According to a brief history of the Faith in Myanmar (from thebahaifaith-myanmar.com, unattributed), “The first Bahá’í teacher sent by Bahá’u’lláh to India and Burma was Jamal Effendi. He arrived in India in 1876 and met Siyyid Mustafa Roumi’s family, who were settled in Madras.” Siyyid Muṣṭafá Rúmí travelled with him through India and Burma. After the Faith was established in Rangoon, the two went to Mandalay, then the capital. There, the same history says, they stayed in the house of a silk merchant, Abdu’l-Sattar (U Koo), whose nephew Ko Thwin had heard of them. “U Koo and his nephew became the first believers in Mandalay.” The history does not say what religion U Koo held, and the first believers in Mandalay should not be assumed to be Buddhists.

Sprague in Mandalay. Sydney Sprague visited the communities in A Year With the Bahá’ís of India and Burma (1908). In Mandalay he found meetings held in a private house, “that of a Burmese widow where I was staying,” too small for the number who came twice a week, so that some met out of doors. Women took part in the meetings: “in Mandalay the Buddhist women have always been accustomed to a good deal of liberty and freedom, and now that they have become Bahá’ís they naturally do not abandon that.” He also reports that religious leaders worked to keep their people from the meetings, because “it is only necessary to go to one Bahá’í meeting to become a Bahá’í.” Sprague’s book is a traveller’s account. It does not say what moved each person, nor that Maitreya was the reason.

The saved sources name no individual Buddhist and give no account of why a Buddhist became a Bahá’í because of the Maitreya promise.

4. The tradition’s sacred music and poetry

These notes are descriptions from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0), saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/buddhist/`. They are for choosing an idiom, not for lyrics.

  • Buddhist music in general (Wikipedia, “Buddhist music”): “Most Buddhist music includes chanting or singing, accompanied by instruments.” The texts chanted include sutras, mantras, parittas and verse compositions. Instruments range “from solely relying on the human voice, to many types of classic instruments used in Asian music (such as the ancient Indian veena).”
  • Theravada chant: usually in Pali, “sometimes with vernacular translations interspersed.” Some traditions prefer “a more monotone chant” and others “a more melodic chanting.” A common form is paritta (pirit) chanting, “one of the oldest forms of Buddhist chanting,” including the Metta Sutta on loving-kindness.
  • Paritta (Wikipedia, “Paritta”): verses recited for protection, with an early history in the Pali texts; all-night recitations by monks are believed to bring “safety, peace and well-being to a community.” They are generally recited aloud.
  • Myanmar: the same article describes a modern genre of Buddhist songs, dhamma thachin, composed in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries and sung at monasteries and festivals; some use “traditional Burmese instruments and vocal stylings associated with the Mahāgīta, a genre of Burmese classical music.”
  • Japanese shōmyō (Wikipedia, “Shōmyō”): chant used mainly in the Tendai and Shingon traditions, using “the yo scale, a pentatonic scale,” passed on orally from teacher to student.

The Mandalay history above is Burmese, so the Theravada and Myanmar descriptions are the closest fit to it.

5. What the sources do not settle

How much the Guardian said. The letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi of 24 June 1947 says the God Passes By passage “is all the information the Guardian has” on the fulfilment of Buddhist prophecies. The Bahá’í texts give no verses, no dates and no further identification.

The source of the phrase. God Passes By says the prophecy is “attributed to” the Buddha. The saved Pali translations (DN 26, DN 14, and Warren’s § 102) do not contain the words “the Buddha of universal fellowship” or “boundless glory.” This page found no text, Pali or Bahá’í, that identifies where the Guardian took the wording. The Pali passages above are the closest Buddhist texts that foretell Metteyya.

Whether Bahá’u’lláh named the Buddha. A letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice to an individual, 27 October 1986, says: “Bahá’u’lláh made no mention of Buddha, and if it had not been for ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s” statement, it would not have been possible to state definitely that the Buddha had been a Manifestation of God.

The authenticity of the scriptures. A letter on behalf of Shoghi Effendi of 25 November 1950 says: “We cannot be sure of the authenticity of the scriptures of Buddha and Krishna.” Another (26 December 1941) says the Buddha’s followers “do not possess His authentic writings.” The Pali texts quoted above are therefore the Buddhist community’s own scripture, not texts that a Bahá’í source authenticates word for word. The Bahá’í statement is that the Buddhist books contain prophecies in “symbols and metaphors.”

Scholarship and commentary.

  • Moojan Momen, in Buddhism and the Baha’i Faith (momen.org), notes that “there are no surviving documents by the Bab or Bahá’u’lláh referring directly to Buddhism.”
  • Jamshed Fozdar’s Buddha Maitrya Amithaba Has Appeared (popular Bahá’í commentary) reads the Anāgatavaṃsa’s “Twin-miracle” as two contemporaneous Avatars, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh. Dann May, in a critical post in the “Bahá’í Faith and Buddhism” dialogue (Talisman 1, 1995), disputes Fozdar’s reading of the text and his method. Neither reading is in the Writings.
  • Coleman’s Common Grounds: Buddhism says that Bahá’u’lláh “is referred to as Maitrya Amitabha Buddha, the fifth Buddha after Gautama Buddha.” Momen’s encyclopedia entry says that “Bahá’ís consider” Bahá’u’lláh the fulfilment of the prophecy of the Mettaya (Maitreya) Buddha. These present Bahá’í belief; the identification with Amitabha is not in the Writings.

Buddhist views. This page does not say how Buddhist teachers or institutions understand Metteyya, and does not claim that they accept the Bahá’í reading.

6. For songwriting

Exact phrases from the Bahá’í texts (authorised translations from the Bahá’í Reference Library, © Bahá’í International Community; quote with credit; check the bahai.org terms before a commercial release):

  • “the fifth Buddha” (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.11)
  • “a Buddha named Maitreye, the Buddha of universal fellowship” (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.15)
  • “His boundless glory” (Shoghi Effendi, GPB VI.15)
  • “in symbols and metaphors” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablets vol. 3)
  • “the banner of the Most Great Peace” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, PUP 80.1)

Exact phrases from the Buddhist texts (public domain: Rhys Davids 1910 and 1921, Warren 1896):

  • “even as I am now” (DN 26, para. 25)
  • “an Exalted One named Metteyya” (DN 26, para. 25)
  • “lovely in its origin, lovely in its progress” (DN 26, para. 25)
  • “not by the scourge, not by the sword, but by righteousness” (DN 26, para. 24)
  • “But after me Metteyya comes” (Warren 1896, § 102)
  • “Our cycle is a happy one” (Warren 1896, § 102)

Scholarship (Momen, May, Fozdar, Coleman) is under copyright and is not for lyrics. The Wikipedia descriptions are CC BY-SA and are for description only.

Concrete images from the sources: a royal city, Ketumati, full of people; a king who rules by righteousness and then gives away his palace to beggars and wayfarers; a teacher who says “even as I am now”; a congregation of some thousands; a house in Mandalay too small for those who came, with the overflow meeting outdoors; women attending as they always had.

A possible arc: the Buddha tells Sāriputta that a successor will come “after me”; the long wait, with teaching fading as the texts say; a Teacher’s promise fulfilled in a new place; a first meeting in a house in Mandalay; fellowship, and a closing line of peace that the Buddha’s own promise already holds (a king who conquers “by righteousness”).

A lyric must not:

  • say that Buddhist teachers or institutions accept that Bahá’u’lláh is Maitreya;
  • attribute the phrase “Buddha of universal fellowship” to a Pali text, or give it a Pali source;
  • say that the Writings name Amitabha, give dates for Maitreya, or interpret the “Twin-miracle”;
  • say that Bahá’u’lláh named the Buddha;
  • say that the Pali scriptures are authentic word for word, or that the Bahá’í Writings explain Maitreya’s thousand-year signs;
  • put the Buddhist community’s current teachings in the Bahá’í voice, or call Buddhist practice a corruption (the Writings’ statements on this are not for a song to repeat).
