The Few Who Rise: Dawn Prayer in the Bahá’í Writings and in the Islamic and Sufi Tradition

Last updated 15 October 2026

What this page covers

the-hour-of-dawn sets out the Bahá’í law of dawn (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶115 and Questions and Answers no. 15), the Fast prayer about lovers who “flee from sleep,” the prayers for waking, and how the Báb, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi kept the hour. This page does not repeat that. It asks a narrower question: what do the Writings, and the Islamic and Sufi tradition from which Bahá’u’lláh’s vocabulary comes, say about the person who is awake and praying at dawn when most people are not?

The sources show the same figure in all three traditions. In the Qur’án he is one who “sleeps but little” and asks forgiveness “before dawn.” In Persian poetry he is the saḥar-khíz, “one who rises at dawn.” In Bahá’u’lláh’s Fast prayer he is one of the “ardent lovers” who “forsake their couches.” The sources also share a set of words: saḥar, aṣḥár, ṣabá, nasím, bámdád.

The page has seven parts:

  1. What the texts say about the few. Where the Writings contrast those who rise with those who sleep, and how far they go in saying that the many do not rise.
  2. The Persian and Arabic words for dawn and the dawn breeze, with what the Bahá’í texts do with them.
  3. The text Lessan remembers. Which passage says what about leaving one’s bed to make mention of God.
  4. The Star of the West passage and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Persian wording.
  5. The Islamic background: Qur’án, hadith, the dawn call to prayer.
  6. Ablution in the cold, and what the Kitáb-i-Aqdas says about hardship.
  7. Sufi poetry of dawn (Ḥáfiẓ, Rúmí, Sa‘dí, ‘Aṭṭár) and Bahá’í accounts of believers who kept the hour.

The Bahá’í Writings are the authority for Bahá’í claims here. Islamic and Sufi material is background and comparison, and is labelled as external to the corpus. Persian and Arabic words are transliterated in the Bahá’í system used by the corpus (á, í, ú, ḥ, ẓ). Where I give an English rendering of a Persian or Arabic text that has no authorised English translation in the corpus, it is marked as my own literal rendering.

1. What the texts say about the few

The sleepers, and the visit at dawn

Bahá’u’lláh says plainly that most people are asleep:

“The peoples of the world are fast asleep. Were they to wake from their slumber, they would hasten with eagerness unto God, the All-Knowing, the All-Wise.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶39

In the Hidden Words He places two visits at dawn:

“O BONDSLAVE OF THE WORLD! Many a dawn hath the breeze of My loving-kindness wafted over thee and found thee upon the bed of heedlessness fast asleep. Bewailing then thy plight it returned whence it came.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Hidden Words, Persian no. 30

“O ESSENCE OF DESIRE! At many a dawn have I turned from the realms of the Placeless unto thine abode, and found thee on the bed of ease busied with others than Myself.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Hidden Words, Persian no. 28

The Persian of no. 30 begins dar saḥargáhán nasím-i-‘ináyat-i-man bar tu murúr namúd, “in the dawns the breeze of My bounty passed over thee,” so the English “dawn” and “breeze” are the Persian saḥargáhán and nasím (Persian text: [source]). The Persian of no. 28 has basá saḥargáhán, “many dawns.” Both passages use sleep as a figure for heedlessness. They do not say that the sleeper was physically in bed. They do say that dawn is the hour when God turns toward a person, and that the visit goes unreceived if the person is not attending.

“Save a few”

One Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh, in Persian, puts the dawn and the small number together. It has no English translation in the corpus. My literal rendering of the opening:

در سحرگاهان طلعت رحمن اهل امکان را به مقام اعلی و افق ابهی دعوت نمود … و لکن طالب مشهود نه مگر قلیلی که از بحر عطا آشامیدند و از ما سوی الله فارغ و آزاد گشتند dar saḥargáhán ṭal‘at-i-Raḥmán ahl-i-imkán rá bi-maqám-i-a‘lá va ufuq-i-abhá da‘vat namúd … va lákin ṭálib mashhúd na magar qalílí ki az baḥr-i-‘aṭá áshámídand va az má-siváʼlláh fárigh va ázád gashtand. “At dawn the Countenance of the All-Merciful summoned the people of possibility to the most exalted station and the most glorious horizon … but no seeker was seen, except a few who drank from the ocean of bestowal and became free of all but God.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá, vol. 2 (Persian text: [source]; my literal rendering)

Here “dawn” is the dawn of the Revelation, not a time of day, and the “few” are those who responded to it. I include it because it is Bahá’u’lláh’s own use of saḥargáhán with qalíl, “few.” It is not a statement about prayer at sunrise.

The few in the Qur’án

The Qur’án describes the person who is awake before dawn as one of a few:

“They used to sleep but little of the night, / And in the hours before dawn they would ask forgiveness.” — Qur’án 51:17–18 (Sahih International)

The Arabic is kánú qalílan mina’l-layli má yahja‘ún, wa bi’l-asḥári hum yastaghfirún. Qalílan means “little,” and the verse says they slept little, not that few people did so. The Bahá’í Tablet below uses the same words.

Bahá’u’lláh’s Arabic Tablet that uses the Qur’ánic words

An Arabic Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh (Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá, vol. 1, p. 257, in the corpus’s Arabic text file, [source], an OCR text) reads:

Hádhá kitábun min ladunná ilá’lladhína fí ḥubbí lá yahja‘úna illá qalílan wa bi’l-asḥári hum yaqúmúna ‘an maḍáji‘ihim shawqan li-dhikrí, la-‘amrí innahum mukhliṣún. هذا كتاب من لدنا الى الذين فى حبى لا يهجعون الا قليلاً وبالاسحار هم يقومون عن مضاجعهم شوقا لذكرى لعمرى انهم مخلصون “This is a Book from Us to those who, in love of Me, sleep but little, and at the dawns arise from their couches out of longing for My remembrance. By My life, they are sincere.” — Bahá’u’lláh (my literal rendering; no authorised English found in the corpus)

Set beside the Qur’án, the shared words are visible in the text itself:

Qur’ánBahá’u’lláh’s Tablet
kánú qalílan mina’l-layli má yahja‘ún (51:17)lá yahja‘úna illá qalílan
wa bi’l-asḥári hum yastaghfirún (51:18)wa bi’l-asḥári hum yaqúmúna
tatajáfá junúbuhum ‘ani’l-maḍáji‘i (32:16)yaqúmúna ‘an maḍáji‘ihim

The verbs yahja‘ún (“sleep”), the adverb qalílan, the plural asḥár and the noun maḍáji‘ (“beds, couches”) are all Qur’ánic. What Bahá’u’lláh adds is the reason: shawqan li-dhikrí, “out of longing for My remembrance.” The Qur’án names fear and hope (32:16) and forgiveness (51:18); this Tablet names longing. I can show the shared words. I cannot show what Bahá’u’lláh intended by choosing them, and the Tablet does not cite the verses.

The same link is visible in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas. ¶115 reads in Arabic ṭúbá li-man tawajjaha ilá Mashriqi’l-Adhkári fi’l-asḥári dhákiran mutadhakkiran mustaghfiran, which the authorised English renders “at the hour of dawn, centering his thoughts on God, occupied with His remembrance, and supplicating His forgiveness” ([source]). The word for “dawn” is al-asḥár and the last participle, mustaghfiran, is from the root of yastaghfirún in Qur’án 51:18 and of al-mustaghfirína bi’l-asḥár in 3:17. Bahá’u’lláh also calls the Báb the one “Who extolleth My Name in the night season and at the break of day” (Aqdas ¶20), Arabic fi’l-layálí wa’l-asḥár.

When Bahá’u’lláh was asked what “at dawn” means, He answered with a second word for dawn, al-fajr: remembrance “at the earliest dawn of day, between dawn and sunrise, or even up to two hours after sunrise” is acceptable (Q&A 15; Arabic: ‘inda ṭulú‘i’l-fajr, wa ba‘da ṭulú‘i’l-fajr ilá ṭulú‘i’sh-shams, wa ḥattá ba‘da dhálika bi-sá‘atayn). So the Qur’ánic word asḥár and the plain word fajr are both used, and Bahá’u’lláh treats the first as covering the hour from first light to about two hours after sunrise.

The Fast prayer

The passage in Prayers and Meditations LXXXV is quoted in full in the-hour-of-dawn. The part that bears on this page is its description of the people:

“For Thine ardent lovers Thou hast, according to Thy decree, reserved, at each daybreak, the cup of Thy remembrance, O Thou Who art the Ruler of rulers! These are they who have been so inebriated with the wine of Thy manifold wisdom that they forsake their couches in their longing to celebrate Thy praise and extol Thy virtues, and flee from sleep in their eagerness to approach Thy presence and partake of Thy bounty.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations, LXXXV

I did not find the Arabic of this prayer in a usable text in the corpus (the Arabic prayer file is garbled), so I cannot show which Arabic words stand behind “forsake their couches.” The English “forsake their couches” is close to Pickthall’s “forsake their beds” and Arberry’s “their sides shun their couches” for Qur’án 32:16. Whether the Arabic of the prayer has maḍáji‘ I cannot say from the corpus.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá at night

‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes himself as the one who is awake:

“O YE faithful friends, O ye sincere servants of Bahá’u’lláh! Now, in the midwatches of the night, when eyes are closed in slumber and all have laid their heads upon the couch of rest and deep sleep, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá is wakeful within the precincts of the Hallowed Shrine and, in the ardor of his invocation uttereth this, his prayer:” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 233

This is a night prayer, not a dawn prayer, but it is the clearest picture in the Writings of someone who is awake while everyone else sleeps and what he does with that: he prays for the friends. A Tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá printed in Star of the West names three times for the believers’ remembrance: “raising the tumult of commemoration therein, during the wing of the night, at dawn, and at eventide” (vol. 4, no. 19, 2 March 1914, in a Tablet about building the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár in Chicago). The corpus does not identify the original of that translation. The phrase “the wing of the night” also occurs in his Arabic prayer in Makátíb-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá, vol. 1, no. 2, which begins wa innī ataqarrabu ilayka yá iláhí fí janḥi hádhihi’l-laylati’ẓ-ẓalmá’, “I draw near to Thee, O my God, in the wing of this dark night” (my literal rendering).

In the same prayer he asks that the friends become servants who yunájúka fi’l-asḥár bi-abda‘i’l-adhkár shawqan ilá liqá’ika, in my rendering “commune with Thee at the dawns with the most wondrous remembrances, out of longing for meeting Thee” ([source]). It is a prayer that the believers pray at the dawns.

What the sources say and do not say about rarity

The sources establish:

  • the many sleep (Aqdas ¶39; Hidden Words);
  • God turns toward people at dawn (Hidden Words, Persian 28 and 30);
  • those who rise do so out of love and longing, not duty (Prayers and Meditations LXXXV; the Arabic Tablet above);
  • the Qur’án describes such people as those who sleep “little.”

They do not say that dawn is valuable because few people are awake or because the world is quiet. The sentences about “the whole world being asleep” in this theme come from the heedlessness passages, which are about attention. The hour is rare in the sense that most people are not using it. The texts do not say that this rarity is the reason to rise.

The English that translates these words

Each of these correspondences is shown by the Persian or Arabic text next to the authorised English:

  • Hidden Words, Persian 28, 30: saḥargáhán = “dawn”; nasím-i-‘ináyat = “the breeze of My loving-kindness.” The English noun “breeze” is nasím; “loving-kindness” is the English for ‘ináyat.
  • Hidden Words, Persian 49. The English reads “Tell the rich of the midnight sighing of the poor.” The Persian is aghniyá rá az nála-yi saḥargáhí fuqará akhbár kunid, “inform the rich of the dawn-time lament of the poor.” Steingass gives saḥar as “early morning, dawn of day,” so the English moves the hour earlier, to “midnight.” I found no note explaining the choice. It is consistent with saḥar meaning the end of the night, but the word the English uses is not the word the Persian uses.
  • Tabernacle of Unity, Tablet 5, ¶7: “Whoso hath not breathed the sweet fragrance of the Beloved at this dawntide is indeed accounted among the dead.” The Persian (`reference_library_fa/ai1.md`) is har yak az mardumán-i-jahán ki búy-i-khwush-i-jánán rá dar ín bámdád nayáft az mardigán maḥsúb. “Dawntide” is bámdád.
  • Gleanings XLI: “I was asleep on My couch, when lo, the Breeze of God wafting over Me roused Me.” The Arabic (in Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá vol. 1, and quoted in a Persian work in the corpus, `reference_library_fa/tn.md`) is kuntu ná’iman ‘alá maḍja‘í marrat ‘alayya nafaḥátu Rabbí’r-Raḥmán wa ayqaẓatní min an-nawm. “Couch” is maḍja‘; “Breeze” is nafaḥát, a plural meaning “breaths” or “wafts of fragrance.”
  • Selections from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 115: “Every day at first light, ye gather the Bahá’í children together and teach them the communes and prayers.” The Persian (Makátíb vol. 1, no. 115) is aṭfál-i-bahá’í rá jam‘ numúda dar bámdád ta‘lím-i-munájáṭ midihíd. “At first light” is dar bámdád.
  • Compilation, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, X: “fervently offering unto God prayers and supplications at every dawn.” The Persian (Makátíb vol. 2, no. 133) is dar bámdád bi-namáz va niyáz pardázad va mutaḍarri‘ ilá’lláh gardad. “At every dawn” is dar bámdád, and the preceding sentence begins fayḍ-i-námutanáhí dar har ṣubḥgáhí, “infinite grace, at every early morning,” which the English renders “Each morn.”

I did not find English phrases in the prayers (for example “the gentle winds of Thy grace” in Prayers and Meditations CLXXIX) whose Persian or Arabic I could check in a usable text, so I make no claim about which word stands behind them.

The breeze as messenger

In Persian poetry ṣabá is the east wind and carries messages and the scent of the beloved between lovers. Two lines of Ḥáfiẓ show it (text from ganjoor.net, external source):

ṣabá bi-luṭf bigú án ghazál-i-ra‘ná rá / ki sar bi-kúh va biyábán tú dáda-í má rá “Say gently, East Wind, to that graceful gazelle: you have sent us wandering, heads to mountain and desert.” — Ḥáfiẓ, ghazal 4 (my literal rendering)

bi-búy-i-náfa-í ká-ákhir ṣabá zán ṭurra bigsháyad “For the scent of the musk-pod that the east wind will at last release from that curl.” — Ḥáfiẓ, ghazal 1 (my literal rendering)

Both are from the Ganjoor edition. In ghazal 267 Ḥáfiẓ writes ay ṣabá gar bi-guzarí bar sáḥil-i-rúd-i-Aras / búsa zan bar khák-i-án vádí va mushkín kun nafas, “O east wind, if you pass by the bank of the river Aras, kiss the earth of that valley and make your breath musk-scented.” ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotes this couplet in a Tablet to the friends in Bákú (3 July 1919), translated “O zephyr, shouldst thou pass by the banks of the Araxes, / Implant a kiss on the earth of that valley and make fragrant thy breath,” and connects it to the Báb, who “was banished to Chihríq” in that region (Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks). So ‘Abdu’l-Bahá himself reads Ḥáfiẓ’s east wind as carrying the fragrance of the Báb.

Bahá’u’lláh uses the same figure. In a Persian Tablet in Áthár-i-Qalam-i-A‘lá vol. 1 (`reference_library_fa/ai1.md`, a daily-readings arrangement under 19 Qudrat, 22 November) He addresses the wind as a messenger. My literal rendering:

ای نسیم صبا ، چون قاصدی ملاحظه نمی‌شود ، تو برائحهٔ قمیص بها از رضوان بقا بر مریدین و احبابم مرور نما و بنفثات روح و آیات ظهور جمیع را آگاه کن که شاید بعضی از نفوس … بفردوس اعظم راجع شوند ay nasím-i-ṣabá, chún qáṣidí mulāḥaẓa nimí-shavad, tú bi-ráyiḥa-i-qamíṣ-i-Bahá az Riḍván-i-baqá bar murídín va aḥibbá’am murúr numá … “O east wind, since no messenger is to be seen, pass, with the scent of the shirt of Bahá from the Riḍván of eternity, over My followers and friends, and make all aware of the breathings of the Spirit and the signs of the Manifestation, that perchance some souls … may return to the Most Great Paradise.” — Bahá’u’lláh (my literal rendering)

The “scent of the shirt” is the story in Qur’án 12:94–96, where Jacob smells Joseph’s scent before the caravan arrives and his sight returns when the shirt is laid on his face. The same Tablet tells the breeze to pass over “with complete detachment” (bi-inqiṭá‘-i-tamám). In the same volume a passage says az ín nasím-i-khwush-i-ṣabá-yi-ma‘naví ghāfil nashavíd, “do not be heedless of this sweet breeze of the spiritual ṣabá” (my rendering). And a Tablet that speaks of the people of Írán and addresses the friends says shumá’íd anjum-i-samá’-i-‘irfán va nasá’im-i-saḥargáhán, “you are the stars of the heaven of knowledge and the breezes of the dawns.”

I have not found a Bahá’í text that says the dawn breeze is a reason to rise for prayer. The texts use the dawn breeze as the sign that God has turned toward someone (Hidden Words 30; Gleanings XLI) and as the messenger that carries the Beloved’s fragrance (the Tablet above).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá and the dawn birds

In Makátíb-i-‘Abdu’l-Bahá, vol. 2 (`reference_library_fa/swa2.md`), the dawn images are used for the friends’ service:

  • no. 32: chún nasá’im-i-saḥarí pur mushk va ‘anbar va rúḥ-parvar shavi, “become, like the breezes of dawn, full of musk and ambergris, and soul-nourishing”;
  • no. 59: chunán bi-jush va khurúsh áyí ki khurús-i-saḥarí gardí va bang-i-ḥayya ‘alá’ṣ-ṣaláḥ ḥayya ‘alá’l-falāḥ ḥayya ‘alá’n-najáḥ dar ṣabáḥ-i-Míthák bar-áví, “become so fervent that you are the cock of dawn, and raise in the morning of the Covenant the cry ‘Come to righteousness, come to success, come to triumph’”;
  • no. 80: nála-yi saḥargáhí kun va zárí-yi ṣubḥgáhí, “raise the lament of dawn and the weeping of early morning”;
  • no. 108: pas ay murghán-i-saḥarí, “O birds of the dawn.”

(All four are my literal renderings.) No. 59 echoes the Muslim call to prayer, whose phrases are ḥayya ‘alá’ṣ-ṣalāh, ḥayya ‘alá’l-falāḥ, “come to prayer, come to success.” The text in the corpus reads ṣaláḥ (“righteousness”) where the adhan has ṣalāh (“prayer”), and adds najáḥ (“triumph”). That may be deliberate or an extraction error; I could not check a printed edition. The dawn cock is the figure for the one who wakes others. Section 5 returns to the call.

3. The text Lessan remembers

Lessan recalled this idea: when we open our eyes and wake, we should speed out of bed in our longing to make mention of Him. No single text in the corpus says exactly that. These texts are the nearest, and each says something different:

TextWhat it saysWhat it does not say
Prayers and Meditations LXXXV (Bahá’u’lláh, Fast prayer)The ardent lovers “forsake their couches in their longing to celebrate Thy praise and extol Thy virtues, and flee from sleep in their eagerness to approach Thy presence.”It describes what lovers do. It is not a command, and it is a prayer for the Fast.
Arabic Tablet, Áthár vol. 1, p. 257 (Bahá’u’lláh; my rendering)Those who “at the dawns arise from their couches out of longing for My remembrance.”Not translated in the corpus; OCR text.
Prayers and Meditations CXLIV (Bahá’u’lláh)A prayer to say on waking: “I have risen from my couch at this dawntide when the Daystar of Thy oneness hath shone forth.”It is the person waking, not an instruction to hurry.
‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Tablet of 5 March 1914 (Star of the West 8:4, p. 48)“When a soul rises in the morning from sleep before everything else he must commemorate the name of God.”It does not mention longing or speed. This is the text closest to “the first act on waking.”
‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Compilation X“Each morn God’s infinite grace confirmeth … let every awakened soul obtain, to the extent of its capacity, a portion of this spiritual grace. This can be achieved by fervently offering unto God prayers and supplications at every dawn.”The measure is the person’s capacity.
Gleanings CLIV (Bahá’u’lláh)“Repose not yourselves on your couches, nay bestir yourselves as soon as ye recognize your Lord … and hasten to His assistance.”This is about arising to help the Cause, not about dawn or prayer.
Prayers and Meditations XL (Bahá’u’lláh)Teach so that “the dead may speed out of their sepulchers, and rush forth towards Thee.”The words “speed out” are about the dead. Velasco’s study notes the Arabic verb is yaqúmu, “arise,” rendered by Shoghi Effendi as “speed out” (bahai-library.com article, a scholarly source).

The words “speed” and “beds” do not occur together in any text I found. Lessan’s sentence is a combination of P&M LXXXV (longing; leaving couches), the Arabic Tablet (rising at the dawns from the couch, for remembrance) and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s 1914 Tablet (remembrance before everything else), with the verb “speed” from Shoghi Effendi’s translation elsewhere. It is not a quotation from any of them and I attribute it to none of them.

Another prayer says “I lay asleep on my couch, O my God, when lo, the gentle winds of Thy grace and Thy loving-kindness passed over me, and wakened me … and bade me arise” (Prayers and Meditations CLXXIX). There, God does the waking. The person rises because he has been woken.

4. The Star of the West passage

I searched the volumes of Star of the West in the corpus for a longer ‘Abdu’l-Bahá text on prayer at dawn. The best match to what Lessan remembers is the 1914 Tablet in volume 8, number 4. Its printed text, checked against the page image of the issue:

“Regarding thy question about the morning prayer. Both meanings are included in the word dawn—the natural dawn, and the dawn of the kingdom. When a soul rises in the morning from sleep before everything else he must commemorate the name of God in order that he may obtain spirituality and illumination.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, “Words of Abdul-Baha; from tablet to an American believer; translated by Mirza Ahmad Sohrab, March 5, 1914, Mt. Carmel, Haifa.” Star of the West, vol. 8, no. 4 (17 May 1917), p. 48, in “The Divine Art of Living,” compiled by Mrs. Mary M. Rabb, chapter “Prayer”

It is the last printed page of the issue. It does not say why dawn is the right time. It names two senses of “dawn” and the order of the first act of the day.

Other dawn texts in Star of the West that are not in the-hour-of-dawn:

  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on the Ishqábád Temple: “when the prayers and supplications made at dawn and at sunrise ascend to the throne of the Almighty—then will the effect of the Mashreq ’Ul Azkar be made evident and manifest” (vol. 11, no. 1, 21 March 1920; also vol. 13, no. 10).
  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá to Bahá’ís: “ye may, at dawn, turn unto the Holy Land and may experience spiritual emotions with all intensity” (vol. 12, no. 1). In Persian this is har yak dar saḥar-há tawajjuh bi-arḍ-i-muqaddas kunid, “each of you, at the dawns, turn toward the Holy Land” (Makátíb vol. 1, no. 46).
  • A talk of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in which Bahá’u’lláh, in the early mornings, “would arise and prepare tea for all of us, and while the sun was dawning from the eastern horizon we would gather around Him” (vol. 9, no. 16, 31 December 1918, “Description of a Beautiful Garden”). This is a narrative recollection, recorded by a translator not named in the corpus text.

I found no Star of the West article devoted to prayer at dawn in the sense of a full essay with a long ‘Abdu’l-Bahá quotation. If one exists it is probably in a source outside the corpus.

5. The Islamic background

All material in this section is external to the corpus and is background and comparison. Qur’án translations are from the quran.com / alquran.cloud text and are named by translator; hadith are from the numbering of Sunnah.com as shown on the mirror pages I could open (Sunnah.com itself blocked automated access).

The Qur’án

“The patient, the true, the obedient, those who spend [in the way of Allah], and those who seek forgiveness before dawn.” — Qur’án 3:17 (Sahih International). Arabic wa’l-mustaghfirína bi’l-asḥár. Arberry: “imploring God’s pardon at the daybreak.”

“They used to sleep but little of the night, / And in the hours before dawn they would ask forgiveness.” — Qur’án 51:17–18 (Sahih International). Pickthall: “ere the dawning of each day would seek forgiveness.”

“Their sides shun their couches as they call on their Lord in fear and hope; and they expend of that We have provided them.” — Qur’án 32:16 (Arberry). Pickthall: “Who forsake their beds to cry unto their Lord in fear and hope.” Sahih International: “They arise from [their] beds.”

“Establish prayer at the decline of the sun [from its meridian] until the darkness of the night and [also] the Qur’an of dawn. Indeed, the recitation of dawn is ever witnessed.” — Qur’án 17:78 (Sahih International). Arabic wa qur’ána’l-fajri inna qur’ána’l-fajri kána mashhúdan.

“And from [part of] the night, pray with it as additional [worship] for you; it is expected that your Lord will resurrect you to a praised station.” — Qur’án 17:79 (Sahih International). Arabic wa mina’l-layli fa-tahajjad bihi, the root of tahajjud, night prayer.

“O you who wraps himself [in clothing], Arise [to pray] the night, except for a little …” — Qur’án 73:1–2 (Sahih International). Verse 6: “Indeed, the hours of the night are more effective for concurrence [of heart and tongue] and more suitable for words” (Arabic náshi’ata’l-layli).

One more verse speaks of the dawn itself: “by the dawn when it breathes” (81:18, Sahih International; Arberry “by the dawn sighing”; Arabic wa’ṣ-ṣubḥi idhá tanaffas). It is an oath, not a command to pray. I note it because it describes the dawn as breathing, which fits the Persian pairing of dawn and breeze in section 2. I make no claim of a causal link.

Verses that pair the two ends of the day, as Bahá’u’lláh does in “morn and eventide” (Aqdas ¶149): 24:36 (“exalting Him within them in the morning and the evenings,” Sahih International), 7:205 (Arberry: “Remember thy Lord in thy soul, humbly and fearfully … at morn and eventide. Be not thou among the heedless”), 76:25.

The Writings link to this material directly in two places. The footnote to Bahá’u’lláh’s Lawḥ-i-Sulṭán (Summons of the Lord of Hosts, ¶199), which speaks of “the Book of the Dawntide,” reads “cf. Qur’án 17:78.” And the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh’s phrase fi’l-asḥár (above) is the Qur’án’s.

Hadith

Descent in the last third of the night:

Arabic (Bukhari 1145, Abu Huraira): yanzilu Rabbuná tabáraka wa-ta‘álá kulla laylatin ilá’s-samá’i’d-dunyá ḥína yabqá thuluthu’l-layli’l-ákhir, yaqúlu man yad‘úní fa-astajíba lahu, man yas’alúní fa-u‘ṭiyahu, man yastaghfiruní fa-aghfira lahu. “Our Lord, the Blessed and Exalted, descends every night to the lowest heaven when the last third of the night remains, and says: Who is calling upon Me that I may answer him? Who is asking from Me that I may give him? Who is seeking My forgiveness that I may forgive him?” — Sahih al-Bukhari 1145 (Book 19, hadith 26); Sahih Muslim 758. The Arabic is as printed in the Bukhari text I checked. In the English, the three questions are as quoted on Abu Amina Elias’s website and the rest is my rendering.

The Fajr prayer:

  • “The two rak‘ahs at dawn are better than this world and what it contains.” — Sahih Muslim 725a (A’isha), in-book Book 6, hadith 118.
  • “Angels come to you in succession by night and day and all of them get together at the time of the Fajr and ‘Asr prayers” and report to God, “When we left them they were praying and when we reached them, they were praying.” — Sahih al-Bukhari 555 (Abu Huraira; English as shown on ihadis.com).
  • Praying Fajr in congregation is likened to praying the whole night (Sahih Muslim 656a, ‘Uthmán; I give the sense only).
  • “The most excellent prayer after what is prescribed is prayer during the night.” — Sahih Muslim 1163a (Abu Huraira).

“Prayer is better than sleep”

The Muslim call to prayer for Fajr adds aṣ-ṣalátu khayrun mina’n-nawm, “prayer is better than sleep,” twice. Abu Dawud 501 (Abu Mahdhurah) gives it in the first call of the morning prayer. Ibn Majah 716 (Bilal) reports that Bilal came to call the Prophet to Fajr, was told he was sleeping, and said the phrase, and that it was approved for the Fajr call. Gradings differ: for Ibn Majah 716 and the related Abu Dawud 504, the mirror pages list al-Albání as ṣaḥíḥ and Zubair ‘Alí Za’í as ḍa‘íf. A report in Mālik’s Muwaṭṭa’ says instead that a muezzin found ‘Umar asleep and ‘Umar ordered the phrase added; al-Albání grades the Mishkat version of that report weak. I did not read that text in full. As far as I know the Twelver Shí‘í call to prayer does not include the phrase, but I did not verify this in a Shí‘í source.

For this page, the point is that in Islamic practice the adhan at dawn says aloud what the sibling article’s Hidden Words say: that sleep is what the person is choosing between. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s call to the “dawn cock” in Makátíb vol. 2 no. 59 uses the neighbouring phrase of the same call.

6. Ablution in the cold

The hadith

Lessan remembered poetry about waking, performing ablutions in winter with icy water, and treating that as part of faith. I did not find that poem. The closest real sources are these.

The hadith:

“Abu Huraira reported: The Messenger of Allah said: Should I not suggest to you that by which Allah obliterates the sins and elevates the ranks (of a man)? They said: Yes, Messenger of Allah. He said: Performing the ablution thoroughly despite odds, [taking] more paces towards the mosque, and waiting for the next prayer after observing a prayer.” — Sahih Muslim 251a (in-book Book 2, hadith 54), English as shown on ihadis.com

The Arabic phrase is isbágh al-wuḍú’ ‘alá’l-makárih. The Egyptian Dar al-Iftá explains it (in a fatwa on this phrase, in Arabic) as carefulness in washing the limbs completely despite hardships that can be borne, such as ablution with cold water in harsh winter, and limits the hadith to hardship that is not severe. A fatwa of Ibn Báz cites a variant that says fi’s-sabarát, “in the cold,” and adds that warm water is better for thorough ablution but that one who uses cold water and bears it has great merit. Both are modern fatwas (external sources), not Nawawí’s commentary, which I did not obtain.

A search summary of the Muṣannaf of Ibn Abí Shayba (section on purification) lists early reports that frozen water may be broken and used for ablution. I saw only the summary and cannot quote it.

Poetry and anecdote

I searched Ganjoor’s Persian texts for ablution with ice or in the cold and found nothing about breaking ice for ablution at dawn in the poetry of Ḥáfiẓ, Rúmí, Sa‘dí or ‘Aṭṭár. A search summary of Rúmí’s Masnaví (book 3 near verse 3791, masnavi.net) describes a story in which a dervish returns from absorption and his hands and face are wet from ablution with water that came from heaven. It is about the water, not cold. I could not ground the specific poem Lessan describes. If it exists it is probably in a collection of Sufi hagiography or in verse I did not reach.

The Báb, and Bahá’u’lláh’s law

The Bahá’í account closest to this is narrative. Nabíl records that in the winter at Máh-Kú:

“A winter followed of such exceptional severity that even the copper implements were affected by the intensity of the cold. … The water which the Báb used for His ablutions was of such icy coldness that its drops glistened as they froze upon His face.” — Nabíl, The Dawn-Breakers (narrative, not the Writings), the account of the Báb at Máh-Kú, winter of 1264 AH

Nabíl gives the time as “after the termination of each prayer,” not dawn. I found no other Bahá’í account in the corpus of believers breaking ice for ablution.

Bahá’u’lláh’s law of ablutions is lighter than the Islamic one. In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas ablutions are washing “the hands and then his face” (¶18), and for “him that findeth no water” the believer repeats five times “In the Name of God, the Most Pure, the Most Pure” (¶10). The Questions and Answers address cold and hardship directly:

“Question: With reference to ablutions, it hath been revealed, “Let him that findeth no water for ablution repeat five times the words ‘In the Name of God, the Most Pure, the Most Pure’”: is it permissible to recite this verse in times of bitter cold, or if the hands or face be wounded? Answer: Warm water may be used in times of bitter cold. If there are wounds on the face or hands, or there be other reasons such as aches and pains for which the use of water would be harmful, one may recite the appointed verse in place of the ablution.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, Questions and Answers, no. 51

The Aqdas note on this answer says the substitute verse “is extended to those for whom the use of water would be physically harmful” (note 34).

So the Kitáb-i-Aqdas provides for cold and hardship. It permits warm water. It gives no instruction to treat cold water as a proof of faith, and no text in the corpus says that hardship in ablution has merit. I can set the two beside each other. The Islamic hadith assigns merit to completing ablution despite difficulty. The Aqdas removes the difficulty. Bahá’u’lláh’s wider guidance on devotions says that “were a man to read a single verse with joy and radiance it would be better for him than to read with lassitude all the Holy Books of God” (¶149). The texts do not draw the contrast themselves, and I do not claim they intend it.

7. Sufi poetry of dawn, and Bahá’í accounts

Ḥáfiẓ

Ḥáfiẓ uses saḥar-khízán, “those who rise at dawn,” in three ghazals (165, 183, 194) and the dawn litany (vird-i-saḥarí) in two (159, 216) of the Ganjoor edition (ganjoor.net/hafez/ghazal/sh…; external source). The translations are my own literal renderings.

Ghazal 216, closing couplet:

har ganj-i-sa‘ádat ki Khudá dád bi-Ḥáfiẓ / az yumn-i-du‘á-yi shab va vird-i-saḥarí búd هر گنج سعادت که خدا داد به حافظ / از یمن دعای شب و ورد سحری بود “Every treasure of happiness God gave to Ḥáfiẓ came from the blessing of the night prayer and the dawn litany.”

Immediately before that closing couplet, the same ghazal has khud rá bikush ay bulbul az ín rashk ki gul rá / bá bád-i-ṣabá vaqt-i-saḥar jilva-garí búd, “Kill yourself, nightingale, in this jealousy, for the rose had her display with the east wind at dawn,” which puts ṣabá and saḥar in one line.

Ghazal 183 is a whole poem about a night and dawn of grace:

dúsh vaqt-i-saḥar az ghuṣṣa nijátam dádand / va andar án ẓulmat-i-shab áb-i-ḥayátam dádand دوش وقت سحر از غصه نجاتم دادند / واندر آن ظلمت شب آب حیاتم دادند “Last night at dawn-time they freed me from grief, and in that darkness of night they gave me the water of life.”

himmat-i-Ḥáfiẓ va anfás-i-saḥar-khízán búd / ki zi band-i-gham-i-ayyám nijátam dádand همت حافظ و انفاس سحرخیزان بود / که ز بند غم ایام نجاتم دادند “It was Ḥáfiẓ’s resolve and the breaths of those who rise at dawn that freed me from the bonds of the sorrow of the days.”

Ghazal 194: rukh-i-mihr az saḥar-khízán nagardánand agar dánand, “they would not turn the face of kindness away from those who rise at dawn, if they knew.” Ghazal 165 has a rhetorical question about áh-i-saḥar-khízán, “the sigh of those who rise at dawn,” going up to heaven.

Ḥáfiẓ is not always solemn. Ghazal 159 begins ṣúfí-yi má ki zi vird-i-saḥarí mast shudí / shámgáhash nigarán básh ki sarkhwush báshad, “Our Sufi, who got drunk on the dawn litany: watch him in the evening to see whether he is tipsy.” The poet knows the dawn litany can be a show. His own claim, in ghazal 216, is that it is the source of everything good he received.

Rúmí: “The breeze at dawn”

The English verse by Coleman Barks, from The Essential Rumi, reads:

“The breeze at dawn has secrets to tell you. Don’t go back to sleep. / You must ask for what you really want. Don’t go back to sleep. / People are going back and forth across the doorsill where the two worlds touch. / The door is round and open. Don’t go back to sleep.” — Rúmí, trans. Coleman Barks (as reproduced in a blog quoting The Essential Rumi; I did not check the book’s page)

The Persian is a quatrain, Divan-i Shams, Rubá‘iyyát no. 90 in the Ganjoor edition (ganjoor.net/moulavi/shams/robaeesh/sh90; the Persian text and the identification with Barks’s poem come from the Maulana Rumi Online blog and I confirmed the Persian text on Ganjoor):

in bád-i-saḥar maḥram-i-ráz ast, makhsab / hingám-i-taḍarru‘ va niyáz ast, makhsab / bar khalq-i-du kawn az azal tá bi-abad / in dar ki nabasta ast báz ast, makhsab این باد سحر محرم رازست مخسب / هنگام تضرع و نیاز است مخسب / بر خلق دو کون از ازل تا به ابد / این در که نبسته است باز است مخسب “This dawn wind is the confidant of the secret: do not sleep. / It is the time of supplication and need: do not sleep. / For the creatures of the two worlds, from pre-eternity to post-eternity, / this door, which has not been shut, is open: do not sleep.” (my literal rendering)

Barks’s poems are “versions,” not translations. Dar al-Masnavi (dar-al-masnavi.org) quotes the introduction to Bly and Barks’s Night and Sleep: “Coleman Barks’s versions are the result of collaborating with John Moyne. Literal Persian translations by Moyne provide the base for the versions by Barks.” Compared with the Persian:

  • Close: the dawn wind (bád-i-saḥar); “secrets” for ráz; the refrain “Don’t go back to sleep” for makhsab; the open door.
  • Added: “back” (the Persian says “do not sleep,” not “do not go back to sleep”); “round” (the door in the Persian is “not shut”); “people are going back and forth across the doorsill where the two worlds touch” (the Persian says the door is open “for the creatures of the two worlds, from pre-eternity to post-eternity”).
  • Changed: hingám-i-taḍarru‘ va niyáz, “the time of supplication and need,” becomes “You must ask for what you really want.” That reads niyáz as a personal wish. In Persian devotional use taḍarru‘ va niyáz is humble entreaty before God, and taḍarru‘ is the word of Qur’án 7:205 (above). Barks’s line is an interpretation.
  • Reversed in emphasis: the Persian says the wind is the maḥram, the confidant of the secret. Barks says it “has secrets to tell you.” The first is a role (the one who may be trusted with the secret); the second is a message.

Attribution of individual quatrains in the Divan is sometimes uncertain, and I did not consult the critical edition (Furúzánfar).

Sa‘dí: the bird at dawn

I looked for the story of the bird lamenting at dawn while the narrator slept. It is in the Gulistán, Book 2 (on the morals of dervishes), story 26 in the Ganjoor numbering (ganjoor.net/saadi/golestan/gbab2/sh26), but the sleeper is not quite as remembered. Sa‘dí says that he travelled all night in a caravan and slept at dawn beside a thicket. A distraught (shúrída) fellow-traveller shouted and ran into the desert and would not rest. When day came, Sa‘dí asked what had happened. My literal rendering of the reply and the verses:

“He said: I saw the nightingales begin to lament from the trees, and the partridges from the mountain, and the frogs in the water, and the beasts from the thicket. I thought it would not be manly for everything to be in praise and for me to be asleep in heedlessness.”

dúsh murghí bi-ṣubḥ mí-nálíd / ‘aql va ṣabram bibard va ṭáqat u húsh / yakí az dústán-i-mukhliṣ rá / magar ávāz-i-man rasíd bi-gúsh / guft báwar nadáshtam ki turá / bāng-i-murghí chunín kunad madhúsh / guftam ín sharṭ-i-ádamíyyat níst / murgh tasbíḥ-gúy va man khámúsh دوش مرغی به صبح می نالید / عقل و صبرم ببرد و طاقت و هوش / یکی از دوستان مخلص را / مگر آواز من رسید به گوش / گفت باور نداشتم که تو را / بانگ مرغی چنین کند مدهوش / گفتم این شرط آدمیت نیست / مرغ تسبیح گوی و من خاموش “Last night a bird was lamenting at dawn; it took my reason, patience, strength and wits. One of my sincere friends happened to hear my cry. He said: I would not have believed that a bird’s call could so stun you. I said: it is not the way of a human being, that the bird sings praise and I am silent.”

In the verses Sa‘dí says that a friend heard his cry and said he had not believed that a bird’s call could bewilder him so. The moral is that creation praises at dawn and a person should not be silent. In the prose the sleeper is Sa‘dí, and the one who rose is his companion. Sa‘dí’s verses are the closest classical Persian statement I found of “the world praises at dawn and I should too.”

‘Aṭṭár

In the Tadhkirat al-Awliyá’ (Ganjoor edition), the chapter on Rábi‘a al-‘Adawiyya has two dawn passages. My renderings:

  • When she was ill and was asked the cause, she said: nazartu ilá’l-janna fa-addabaní Rabbí, “I looked toward paradise and my Lord corrected me”; the Persian adds dar saḥargáh dil-i-má bi-súy-i-bihisht nazar kard, dúst bá má ‘itáb kard, ín bímárí az ‘itáb-i-úst, “at dawn our heart looked toward paradise; the Friend reproached us; this illness is from His reproach.”
  • Sufyán reports praying in a corner of the same room through the night until vaqt-i-saḥar, “the hour of dawn,” and then saying, “How shall we give thanks that He enabled us to pray all night?” She replied, “Tomorrow we shall fast.”

These are hagiographic anecdotes from an anthology composed around 1200 and tell what ‘Aṭṭár wanted to record about Rábi‘a, not what is certain of her.

Bahá’í accounts of believers who kept the hour

The sibling article lists the dawn gatherings in Írán, ‘Ishqábád and elsewhere and the messages of the Guardian and the House of Justice. These accounts add to it. They are narrative, not the Writings.

  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Memorials of the Faithful says of a believer in ‘Akká after Bahá’u’lláh’s ascension: “At every dawn he would rise and would sweep the ground about the house of Bahá’u’lláh, his tears pouring down like rain, chanting prayers as he worked” (the section headed “Muḥammad Ḥaná-Sáb”; the sentence names him Áqá Muḥammad). In his tribute to the wife of the King of Martyrs he writes: “her cries were lifted up at dawntide, and the sweet accents of her chanting would be heard in the night season and at the bright noonday.”
  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in 1914 and after: Star of the West vol. 4, no. 19 above; vol. 13, no. 1, “Every morning at dawn I supplicate the Kingdom of God”; and a 1920 pilgrim’s account of him, “up before sunrise, offering his prayers at the threshold of the Almighty” (vol. 11, no. 4, 17 May 1920; a visitor’s description).
  • A summer-school report in Bahá’í News no. 357 (December 1960): “the small groups assembled in the olive grove for dawn prayers each morning, as the sun rose over the hills across the valley to flood the orchards with light.” A reminiscence, and an example of a later practice.

I did not find a Bahá’í account of a believer breaking ice for ablution or of dawn prayer under hardship, other than the Báb’s icy water at Máh-Kú.

What the texts offer to someone deciding whether to get up

Taken together, and stated only as far as the quotations go, the texts offer these reasons to rise:

  • God turns toward a person at dawn. “Many a dawn hath the breeze of My loving-kindness wafted over thee” (Hidden Words, Persian 30). The text says the visit is missed when the person is asleep in heedlessness.
  • A cup is set aside for each daybreak. “For Thine ardent lovers Thou hast … reserved, at each daybreak, the cup of Thy remembrance” (Prayers and Meditations LXXXV).
  • Grace arrives each morning, and a person receives it according to capacity. “Each morn God’s infinite grace confirmeth the ardent and tearful invocations of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. Accordingly, let every awakened soul obtain, to the extent of its capacity, a portion of this spiritual grace” (Compilation X). The measure is the person’s own capacity, so a short prayer counts.
  • Remembrance first. “When a soul rises in the morning from sleep before everything else he must commemorate the name of God in order that he may obtain spirituality and illumination” (Star of the West 8:4, p. 48).
  • The hour is wide and the standard is joy. Dawn extends to two hours after sunrise (Q&A 15), and “were a man to read a single verse with joy and radiance it would be better for him than to read with lassitude all the Holy Books of God” (Aqdas ¶149).
  • Others have done it, and it is described as longing. The Qur’án, the Fast prayer, the Arabic Tablet and the poets all describe the people of the dawn as moved by love, hope or longing. Ḥáfiẓ says that everything good that came to him came from the dawn litany (ghazal 216).

No text threatens the person who sleeps. The Hidden Words say the visit is missed, and they say it with grief: “Bewailing then thy plight it returned whence it came.”

What the sources do not settle

  • Why dawn. The Writings state that dawn is the hour of God’s turning (Hidden Words), of grace each morning (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Compilation X), and of the lovers’ praise (Fast prayer). None gives the quiet of the world or the rarity of those awake as the reason.
  • The Persian words. I could show saḥargáhán = “dawn,” nasím = “breeze,” bámdád = “dawntide / first light / every dawn,” nafaḥát = “Breeze,” and that “midnight” in Hidden Words Persian 49 renders saḥargáhí. I could not check the Arabic or Persian of most of the prayers (the Arabic prayer files are garbled), so I make no other correspondence claim.
  • The Arabic Tablet that echoes the Qur’án (Áthár vol. 1, p. 257) is from an OCR text and has no authorised English. The Persian “few who drank” Tablet and the Persian Tablet to the east wind likewise have no English in the corpus. These renderings are mine and should be checked against the printed edition before being used in anything that will be sung or published as translation.
  • Rúmí’s quatrain is identified by Ganjoor and a blog, not a critical edition. Ḥáfiẓ’s ghazal numbers are those of the Ganjoor edition and differ in other editions.
  • Hadith gradings and numbering differ across editions and I give the references from the mirror pages I could read.
  • The ice-water poem was not found.
  • The “Greatest Name at first waking” sentence and Taherzadeh’s remark that dawn prayers have “a special potency” are discussed in the-hour-of-dawn; I did not find a Bahá’í text behind either.
