Protection Without Command: How the Learned Arm Strengthens Without Directing

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

Roughly ninety Continental Counsellors, about 1,134 Auxiliary Board members, and several thousand of their assistants carry the twin duties of protecting and propagating the Bahá’í Faith across the whole world — and not one of them can tell a Local Spiritual Assembly what to do. They cannot legislate, adjudicate, or administer. They cannot instruct a committee, override a decision, or interpret a text with authority. The Universal House of Justice describes this as “a feature of Bahá’í administration unparalleled in the religions of the past.”

My position on the central question is that the appointed arm does not strengthen the community despite having no command; it strengthens it because it has none. The task assigned to the learned — releasing the community’s own capacity to act — is one that authority over that community would destroy. The 2001 document that governs the institution states the premise without hedging: “The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally is divinely conferred on elected institutions. The power to act, however, resides primarily in the entire body of the believers.” An institution charged with unlocking power in people cannot simultaneously hold power over them, because the two operate by opposite means. Command produces compliance. Only counsel, encouragement, and accompaniment produce initiative — and initiative, not compliance, is what the Bahá’í order runs on.

The design is not accidental and it is not new. It is the resolution of a problem the Faith’s founding texts name directly. Every previous dispensation produced a learned class, and every previous learned class eventually claimed the right to rule, to interpret, or both — with schism the recurring result. Bahá’u’lláh’s answer was neither to abolish the learned nor to enthrone them, but to split the two functions permanently and give each to a different body: binding decisions to elected corporate councils, and spiritual, moral, and intellectual influence to appointed individuals of proven devotion. The Universal House of Justice put the surgery in one sentence in 1972: “Having permanently excluded the evils admittedly inherent in the institutions of the ‘learned’ in past dispensations, Bahá’u’lláh has nevertheless embodied in His Administrative Order the beneficent elements which exist in such institutions.”

Three consequences follow, and they organise everything below. First, rank in this system guarantees a hearing, not obedience — the Counsellors outrank National Assemblies precisely so that their advice cannot be ignored, and “neither directs nor instructs” those same Assemblies. Second, protection is executed as education, not as policing: “Helping the believers to meet high moral standards does not require prying into their private lives. The nature of this responsibility is essentially educative.” Third, influence has to be earned in a way that authority does not — the operative verb in the guidance is that the friends should turn to a Board member “spontaneously.” An Auxiliary Board member whom nobody calls has, functionally, no institution.

The practical judgement I would press hardest on a reader: the characteristic failure in this architecture is not abuse of power by the appointed arm — that is rare and well-guarded against. It is atrophy through disuse and mutual deference. An Assembly that treats the Board member as an inspector to be satisfied, and a Board member who treats the Assembly as a body to be managed, both collapse a collateral relationship into a line of authority that the architecture forbids in either direction. The remedy is almost always the same, and it is unglamorous: convene the consultation that has not yet happened, and invite the person who cannot command you to it.

The Text names two kinds of servant, and blesses them equally

The distinction between the learned and the rulers is not an administrative convenience invented in Haifa in 1968. It is in Bahá’u’lláh’s own Book of the Covenant, and it appears there as a blessing rather than a job description.

“It is incumbent upon everyone to aid those daysprings of authority and sources of command who are adorned with the ornament of equity and justice. Blessed are the rulers and the learned among the people of Bahá. They are My trustees among My servants and the manifestations of My commandments amidst My people.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-‘Ahd, in Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh Revealed after the Kitáb-i-Aqdas

Two categories, one benediction, no ranking between them. Both are called trustees; both are called manifestations of My commandments. Nothing in the verse suggests that one group instructs the other. The same balance recurs in the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat, where Bahá’u’lláh guards each against the other’s contempt in a single clause: “Abase not the station of the learned in Bahá and belittle not the rank of such rulers as administer justice amidst you.” The symmetry of the warning is the point. It anticipates a community in which the learned might be dismissed as having no real power, and the rulers dismissed as mere administrators — and forbids both dismissals in one breath.

What the learned are for is spelled out by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá in the Will and Testament, in a sentence that reads much more like a description of character than a grant of office:

“The obligations of the Hands of the Cause of God are to diffuse the Divine Fragrances, to edify the souls of men, to promote learning, to improve the character of all men and to be, at all times and under all conditions, sanctified and detached from earthly things.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Will and Testament

Diffuse, edify, promote, improve, be detached. Not one of those verbs takes a subordinate as its object. They are all verbs of influence — the kind of work that succeeds only if the person on the receiving end is free to refuse it. And the same document, in the passage that establishes the protective function, is equally verb-specific: the Hands “must be ever watchful” against anyone who begins to oppose the Centre of the Covenant. Watchful, not coercive. The one hard power the Will assigns to the Hands — expulsion of a declared opponent of the Guardian from the congregation — is the extreme boundary case of covenantal integrity, and even that has since been routed, under the present order, through the International Teaching Centre’s investigation and the Universal House of Justice’s confirmation rather than exercised by any appointed individual alone.

Shoghi Effendi supplied the interpretation that maps the two Qur’ánic-sounding categories onto the institutions the community actually has. Writing on 4 November 1931, he identified them:

“In this holy cycle the ‘learned’ are, on the one hand, the Hands of the Cause of God, and, on the other, the teachers and diffusers of His teachings who do not rank as Hands, but who have attained an eminent position in the teaching work. As to the ‘rulers’ they refer to the members of the Local, National and International Houses of Justice.” — Shoghi Effendi, 4 November 1931, translated from the Persian, cited by the Universal House of Justice, 24 April 1972 (muhj-1963-1986)

The House of Justice added, in that same 1972 letter, the structural fact that most cleanly separates the two arms — and it is not the fact of appointment, which is what most friends assume:

“We have noted that the Hands, the Counselors and the Auxiliary Boards are sometimes referred to by the friends as the ‘appointive arm’ of the Administrative Order in contradistinction to the Universal House of Justice and the National and Local Assemblies which constitute the ‘elective arm.’ While there is truth in this description as it applies to the method used in the creation of these institutions, the friends should understand that it is not only the fact of appointment that particularly distinguishes the institutions of the Hands, Counselors and Auxiliary Boards. There are, for instance, many more believers appointed to committees in the ‘elective arm’ than are serving in the so-called ‘appointive arm.’ A more striking distinction is that whereas the ‘rulers’ in the Cause function as corporate bodies, the ‘learned’ operate primarily as individuals.” — The Universal House of Justice, 24 April 1972 (muhj-1963-1986)

This is the load-bearing sentence for everything that follows. A Spiritual Assembly is nine people who become one decision-making entity; its authority exists only when it is in session and consulting, and no member of it carries any authority alone. A Counsellor is one person who remains one person, and whose influence travels wherever she does. The elected arm is corporate and bounded; the learned arm is individual and mobile. Those are not two flavours of the same thing. They are two different physics, and the Faith uses each for what the other cannot do.

How the learned came to have no authority: a deliberate excision

The architecture reads as inevitable only in hindsight. It was assembled over roughly half a century, and at each step the Guardian and the Universal House of Justice were solving a specific problem.

Shoghi Effendi created the Auxiliary Boards in the Riḍván 1954 message, announcing the coming appointment by the fifteen Hands residing outside the Holy Land of five Auxiliary Boards, one on each continent, whose members would act as their deputies and would assist them “in the discharge of their dual and sacred task of safeguarding the Faith and of promoting its teaching activities.” In October 1957, weeks before his passing, he doubled the structure — a second Board on each continent, charged specifically with watching over the security of the Faith. Protection and propagation became two named institutional functions rather than one composite duty, and they have remained two ever since.

Then the problem arrived. Shoghi Effendi died in November 1957 without a successor Guardian, and the Will and Testament had vested the appointment of Hands of the Cause in the Guardian alone. The Universal House of Justice, elected in 1963, concluded that it could not legislate to make further appointments possible. The functions were indispensable; the office that carried them could not be replenished. What the House did next is the crux of the story: it separated the functions from the office, and built a new institution to carry the functions forward. In November 1964 it formally related the Hands to itself, declaring that responsibility for general policy affecting their institution “now devolves upon the Universal House of Justice as the supreme and central institution of the Faith to which all must turn” — and raised Auxiliary Board membership from 72 to 135. In June 1968 the Continental Boards of Counsellors were brought into being, the Auxiliary Boards becoming their auxiliaries. In 1973 the International Teaching Centre was established and Board members were for the first time authorised to name assistants — the tier that put the appointed arm inside the village rather than merely inside the country.

What matters for this article is why the House built the new institution the way it did rather than some other way. The 1972 letter says so directly, and the reasoning reaches back past Bahá’í history into religious history generally. Citing a letter written on the Guardian’s behalf to the Bahá’ís of Istanbul on 14 March 1927, the House recalled that in past dispensations it was individuals who “accounted themselves as superior in knowledge and elevated in position” who caused division — and that Bahá’u’lláh’s remedy was structural:

“the Pen of Glory has done away with the unyielding and dictatorial views of the learned and the wise, dismissed the assertions of individuals as an authoritative criterion, even though they were recognized as the most accomplished and learned among men, and ordained that all matters be referred to authorized centers and specified Assemblies.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 14 March 1927, translated from the Persian, cited 24 April 1972 (muhj-1963-1986)

Then the House’s own summary judgement:

“Having permanently excluded the evils admittedly inherent in the institutions of the ‘learned’ in past dispensations, Bahá’u’lláh has nevertheless embodied in His Administrative Order the beneficent elements which exist in such institutions, elements which are of fundamental value for the progress of the Cause.” — The Universal House of Justice, 24 April 1972 (muhj-1963-1986)

Read those two passages together and the design becomes legible as a solution rather than a compromise. The historic pathology of a learned class is that individual erudition becomes an authoritative criterion — one scholar’s reading binds a community. The historic value of a learned class is that knowledge, vision, and personal spiritual force circulate through a body of believers in a way no committee minute ever has. Bahá’u’lláh keeps the second and eliminates the first by a single move: the learned may say anything and decide nothing. And so the House of Justice could conclude:

“The existence of institutions of such exalted rank, comprising individuals who play such a vital role, who yet have no legislative, administrative or judicial authority, and are entirely devoid of priestly functions or the right to make authoritative interpretations, is a feature of Bahá’í administration unparalleled in the religions of the past. The newness and uniqueness of this concept make it difficult to grasp.” — The Universal House of Justice, 24 April 1972 (muhj-1963-1986)

The newness and uniqueness of this concept make it difficult to grasp. The House said that in 1972 and it is still the sentence a newcomer to the architecture needs most. The difficulty is not intellectual. It is that almost every institution a modern person has experience of — corporation, ministry, university, army, church — bundles expertise with the power to direct. This one does not, on purpose.

Rank that compels attention but confers no jurisdiction

The most confusing feature of the appointed arm is that it has rank. Counsellors are said to outrank National Spiritual Assemblies. If rank does not mean the power to direct, what does it mean? The Universal House of Justice answered exactly that question in a Secretariat letter of 27 March 1978, written after a believer asked for clarification, and then circulated to every National Assembly because the House judged the answer of general interest:

“The statement that the Boards of Counselors outrank the National Institutions of the Faith has a number of implications. A Board of Counselors has the particular responsibility of caring for the protection and propagation of the Faith throughout a continental zone which contains a number of national Bahá’í communities. In performing these tasks it neither directs nor instructs the Spiritual Assemblies or individual believers, but it has the necessary rank to enable it to ensure that it is kept properly informed and that the Spiritual Assemblies give due consideration to its advice and recommendations. However, the essence of the relationships between Bahá’í institutions is loving consultation and a common desire to serve the Cause of God rather than a matter of rank or station.” — Written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 27 March 1978 (muhj-1963-1986)

Rank, in this system, is a guarantee of information flow and of serious consideration. It secures two things and only two: that the Counsellors will be told what is happening, and that when they speak, an Assembly will genuinely weigh what they say rather than filing it. It does not secure agreement, compliance, or a veto. The Assembly still decides. The Institution of the Counsellors repeats the same formulation about the whole appointed arm — the fact that they occupy a higher rank “ensures that they are kept properly informed and that Spiritual Assemblies give due consideration to their advice and recommendations.” Being informed and being heeded are precisely the two failure modes that would otherwise strand an advisory institution, and rank is the engineered fix for both.

Having established the rank, the same 1978 letter spends most of its length dismantling any temptation to enjoy it. It quotes Bahá’u’lláh on the unity of rank and station:

“Indeed, man is noble, inasmuch as each one is a repository of the sign of God. Nevertheless, to regard oneself as superior in knowledge, learning or virtue, or to exalt oneself or seek preference, is a grievous transgression.” — Bahá’u’lláh, cited in the letter of 27 March 1978 (muhj-1963-1986)

And then, lest anyone think the caution runs only in one direction, it turns to the elected side and quotes Shoghi Effendi’s warning to National Assembly members that “They should never be led to suppose that they are the central ornaments of the body of the Cause, intrinsically superior to others in capacity or merit, and sole promoters of its teachings and principles.” Both arms are told the same thing about themselves. The House closes with the sentence that resolves the whole matter: “pride and self-aggrandisement are among the most deadly of sins.”

The etiquette follows from the theology. The Institution of the Counsellors discourages the exaggerated use of titles: there is no objection to calling someone a Counsellor, but “The title should not become inseparable from a Counsellor’s personal name, as would be the case of referring to a member of the Board of Counsellors as, for example, ‘Counsellor Jones’.” Nor is “Former Counsellor” a title anyone carries. These look like small points of manners. They are load-bearing. A system whose entire mechanism is influence rather than authority is exceptionally vulnerable to the formation of a de facto clergy, and the smallest linguistic habits — the honorific that sticks, the rank that outlives the term — are how such a class begins to form.

Why having no authority is the source of the power, not a limit on it

Three properties of the appointed arm are direct dividends of its powerlessness. None of them would survive if Counsellors could give orders.

First: freedom from administration buys vantage. The 2001 document names this as “a crucial feature”:

“A crucial feature of the work of the Counsellors that enables them to offer valuable advice to the National Spiritual Assemblies is their aloofness from administrative details, a freedom that allows them to concentrate on the vital issues of the Cause. Care must be exercised, however, that this aloofness does not give rise to extreme situations.” — The Universal House of Justice, The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

An institution that had to run the national teaching programme would be too busy to see it clearly, and too implicated in it to say so. Because the Counsellors administer nothing, they can look at everything. And the document immediately guards against the obvious over-correction: aloofness must not become detachment. Counsellors “should not become inhibited from expressing their views to the National Spiritual Assemblies on administrative matters,” and Assemblies “should not feel restricted” in consulting them on such matters. Freedom from administration is a vantage point, not a recusal.

Second: having nothing to defend makes candour cheap and therefore obligatory. This is the passage in the whole corpus that most sharply distinguishes advice from politeness:

“The Counsellors have not only the right but the obligation to deliberate with, advise, and offer suggestions to the National Spiritual Assemblies in the discharge of their specific functions. They alert National Assemblies to any problems or tendencies in the Bahá’í community which they feel require attention. Their concern in this respect extends to the very functioning of the National Assemblies. If the Counsellors notice serious departures from administrative or other principles in the work of a National Spiritual Assembly or its agencies, they are to consult with the Assembly about the matter and suggest corrective action. This they must do irrespective of any apprehension that such a step might give rise to tension between the two institutions.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

That final clause converts counsel from an option into a duty. The Counsellors cannot correct an Assembly — but they are obliged to tell it, plainly, that it is departing from principle, and forbidden from softening the message to preserve the relationship. This is the exact inversion of how advisory bodies usually behave. An adviser who wants influence normally trims to keep access. Here, access is already secured by rank, so the incentive to trim disappears, and the guidance closes the remaining gap by making candour mandatory. Because they cannot enforce, they can afford to be entirely honest; because they cannot be dismissed, they are required to be.

Third, and decisively: the object of the work is the release of other people’s power. The 2001 document states the premise in the section titled “The Individual and the Community,” and it is the load-bearing claim of this article:

“The authority to direct the affairs of the Faith locally, nationally and internationally is divinely conferred on elected institutions. The power to act, however, resides primarily in the entire body of the believers. This power is unlocked at the level of individual initiative and at the level of collective volition. If the Cause is to realize Bahá’u’lláh’s purpose for humankind, every institution of the Faith must be concerned with the release of power in both these expressions, as they are with ensuring the wise administration of the community’s affairs. The institution of the Counsellors is particularly charged with this vital task and is endowed with the capacity to accomplish it.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Authority and power are here two different substances. Authority is conferred, located, and bounded — it sits with elected bodies. Power is diffuse, latent, and resident in the believers themselves. The institution specifically charged with unlocking the second is the one that holds none of the first, and the pairing is not coincidence. Initiative cannot be ordered into existence. A community can be told to hold a devotional meeting; it cannot be told to want to. The document says as much about the teaching obligation, which is individual and non-transferable: “Bahá’u’lláh’s command to each believer to teach His Faith confers an inescapable responsibility which cannot be transferred to, or assumed by, any institution of the Cause.” No administrative order can discharge that on a believer’s behalf. What an institution can do is described three paragraphs later, and every verb in it is a verb of influence:

“Stimulating individual initiative is one of the paramount duties of the Auxiliary Board members… It involves constant encouragement of the friends, evoking the valour of the heroes of the Faith and bringing to their attention the importance of exemplifying in their lives the glory of the Teachings… It requires building confidence and changing fear and hesitation into courage and perseverance. It asks of the Board members and those they serve alike to forget their own weaknesses and fix their reliance on the power of divine confirmations. Further, it implies accompanying the friends in their endeavours as they develop the capabilities of effective service.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Encouraging, evoking, building confidence, converting fear into courage, accompanying. There is no instruction in the list because there is no instruction that would work. The document itself recognises how far this departs from ordinary organisational thinking: “This challenging conception of the work of the Auxiliary Board members calls for a fundamental departure from limited assumptions about social order which, in the world today, determine administrative theory and practice.”

What protection actually protects

“Protection” is the word most likely to be misread, and the misreading is predictable: a Board for Protection sounds like an internal security function, and an Auxiliary Board member for Protection sounds like someone who investigates believers. The guidance says something markedly different, and says it in the most direct sentence in the 2001 document.

The full statement of what protection consists of is worth reading whole, because its content is almost entirely constructive:

“In discharging their obligations related to protection, the Counsellors and Auxiliary Board members endeavour to nourish the roots of certitude, fan the flame of an all-embracing love in the hearts of the friends, combat the age-old habit of conflict and contention and fortify bonds of friendship and unity, promote adherence to principle and the ethical standards enshrined in the Teachings, raise the believers’ sight above the limitations of self-centredness that they may dedicate their energies to the welfare of the human race, and strengthen their allegiance to the Order of Bahá’u’lláh. Helping the believers to meet high moral standards does not require prying into their private lives. The nature of this responsibility is essentially educative.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Six clauses of construction, then the disclaimer. Protection is the strengthening of what makes a community resilient — certitude, love, unity, principle, selflessness, allegiance — rather than the detection of what makes it vulnerable. The vector is entirely inward-to-outward: fortify the thing, and it needs less defending. And the pastoral caveat is unusually blunt for a document of this kind, because the counterfeit it guards against is so easy to reach. A believer who takes “protection” to mean moral surveillance will produce exactly the ecclesiastical apparatus the Faith abolished.

Three specific boundaries make the educative reading concrete.

Sanctions belong to Assemblies, not to the learned. The same paragraph notes that the educational process “includes the imposition of sanctions by the Spiritual Assemblies, whenever it becomes absolutely necessary” — and that in such cases the Counsellors’ and Board members’ advice to the Assembly can be of particular value. The appointed arm advises on the sanction; the elected arm imposes it. The person who counsels is structurally not the person who punishes, which is what keeps counselling safe to receive.

Personal problems are ordinarily not the Board member’s work. “In general, it is not the task of Auxiliary Board members, but rather that of the Spiritual Assemblies, to deal with the personal problems of individuals and conflicts between them and with disciplinary matters.” The document immediately balances this — Board members and assistants do counsel believers, and if someone comes to them with a personal matter “it is for the Board member or assistant to decide whether to give advice or ask the believer to turn to the Spiritual Assembly.” Discretion, not a rule of referral. But the default location of pastoral casework is the Assembly.

Covenant-breaking is handled at the top, and rarely. The gravest protective function — determining that someone has broken the Covenant — is not exercised by a Counsellor or a Board member at all. The International Teaching Centre “must investigate all cases of incipient Covenant-breaking—employing, as necessary, the services of the Continental Counsellors and their auxiliaries and evaluating their reports—and decide whether the offender should be expelled from the Cause, submitting the decision to the Universal House of Justice for its consideration.” Two institutions above the field must concur. And the temper in which such matters are approached is on record. When a coordinated campaign of internal opposition emerged on the early Internet in 1996, the House of Justice reported in a letter of 7 April 1999 that it had “intervened in the current situation only to the extent that has been unavoidable, trusting to the good sense and the goodwill of the believers involved to awaken to the spiritual dangers to which they are exposing themselves” — and noted the outcome of the appointed arm’s characteristic method: “Some of the people responsible resigned from the Faith when Counselors pointed out to them the direction their activities were taking” (issues-related-study-bahai-faith). The instrument in that episode was a conversation. What it produced was self-selection, not expulsion.

What protection does mean, concretely and in the present, was set out at length by the House of Justice in its message of 30 December 2021 to the Conference of the Continental Boards of Counsellors. The passage is worth reading as an inventory, because it is the closest thing the corpus offers to a job description for the Boards for Protection in the current era:

“As the spiritual energies released by earnest pursuit of the Plan surge, they meet resistance from the countervailing forces that hold humanity back from attaining full maturity. In the face of such forces, the vitality of the various lines of action being followed at the local level needs to be preserved and fortified. This critical responsibility is of special relevance to the members of the two Auxiliary Boards, whose numerous, demanding duties keep them closely connected to conditions at the grassroots and alert to anything that might affect the spirit of a community.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 December 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

And then the list of what they must help the friends face: to help previously antagonistic groups find unity through pursuit of a common goal; to put aside inherited customs and attitudes belonging to humanity’s adolescence and overcome prejudices of all kinds; “to guard against any tendency to view matters with cynicism or an eye for faults, and instead sustain an eager and constructive outlook”; to put the equality of women and men into practice; “to cast off inertia and apathy through the exercise of individual initiative”; to put support for collective plans before personal preference; “to harness the power of modern technologies without succumbing to their potentially enervating effects”; “to reject the opiate of consumerism”; and to turn away from materialist ideologies.

Cynicism. Inertia. Prejudice. Screens. Consumerism. That is the threat model. Not infiltrators — habits. Every item on that list is a disposition that erodes a community from inside, gradually, without anyone deciding to harm it, and not one of them can be addressed by an order. You cannot instruct a community out of cynicism; you can only outlast it with a better example. Which is exactly how the House of Justice closes the passage:

“Through the power of their good example and the clarity of their good counsel, may they help the friends to grow in faith, assurance, and commitment to a life of service, and accompany them as they build communities that are havens of peace, places where a harried and conflict-scarred humanity may find shelter.” — The Universal House of Justice, 30 December 2021 (muhj-2001-2022)

Good example and good counsel. Those are the two instruments, named in the same sentence, and they are the only two the institution has.

Protection and propagation are one motion, not two departments

Every locality in the Bahá’í world is served by two Auxiliary Board members — “the Bahá’ís of any one locality are to have both a Protection Board member and a Propagation Board member to whom they can refer.” The functional split is real. The unity underneath it is more important, and the 2001 document insists on it:

“Members of the Auxiliary Board for Protection, as experience shows, also assist in propagating the Cause, but focus much of their energies on deepening the friends’ knowledge of the Covenant and fostering a spirit of love and unity. Their efforts contribute significantly to the growth of the Bahá’í community, for the protection of the Faith is intimately linked to its propagation.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

The link is causal in both directions, and it explains why one institution carries both duties rather than two institutions carrying one each. A community that is not firm in the Covenant, not united, and not clear about what it believes cannot grow — it will fracture under the strain of growth, and it will have nothing distinctive to offer the people it invites. Conversely, a community that has stopped growing turns inward, and inward-turned communities are where cynicism and faction breed. Protection makes growth survivable; growth makes protection unnecessary in most of the ways it would otherwise be needed.

The guidance goes so far as to decline to regulate which Board a believer should approach: “The question as to which of the two Boards—Propagation or Protection—a Local Spiritual Assembly or individual believer should turn to on a particular issue is not a matter to be regulated, but can be gradually clarified as experience is gained at the local level. If an Auxiliary Board member feels that a matter would have been better referred to his or her colleague, this could easily be arranged.” A system genuinely divided into two jurisdictions would need a referral rule. This one does not, because the division is of emphasis rather than of authority — and because both Board members are, in the end, doing the same job: strengthening people.

The historical anchor for the pairing is Shoghi Effendi’s cable of 4 June 1957, quoted in the 2001 document’s introduction, sent five months before his passing and addressed to the relationship between the Hands and the National Assemblies. It reads, in the telegraphic capitals of the original, as a single unbroken dependency: “SECURITY PRECIOUS FAITH PRESERVATION SPIRITUAL HEALTH BAHÁ’Í COMMUNITIES VITALITY FAITH ITS INDIVIDUAL MEMBERS PROPER FUNCTIONING ITS LABORIOUSLY ERECTED INSTITUTIONS FRUITION ITS WORLDWIDE ENTERPRISES FULFILMENT ITS ULTIMATE DESTINY ALL DIRECTLY DEPENDENT BEFITTING DISCHARGE WEIGHTY RESPONSIBILITIES NOW RESTING MEMBERS THESE TWO INSTITUTIONS.” Security, spiritual health, individual vitality, institutional functioning, worldwide enterprise, ultimate destiny — six things, one chain, and the Guardian makes all six depend on the two arms discharging their responsibilities together.

The instruments: encouragement, accompaniment, and the circulation of learning

If the appointed arm cannot direct, what does it actually do on a Tuesday? Three things, in ascending order of institutional distinctiveness.

It encourages. This is the oldest and least glamorous of the functions, and the Guardian’s own list of tasks for Auxiliary Board members is dominated by it: to stimulate and strengthen the teaching work in cooperation with existing administrative bodies; to help activate weak centres, groups and Assemblies through their visits; to keep in touch with pioneers, “helping them to persevere and pointing out to them the sacredness of their responsibility”; “to encourage individuals and Assemblies through correspondence and through visits”; to impress upon the believers that the foundation of all Bahá’í activity is unity; to encourage free contribution to the Funds; and to bring to their attention the importance of individual effort and initiative. Visits and letters. That is the technology. The 2001 document explains why it is not trivial: the administrative processes of the Faith “are not only concerned with judicial matters, laws and regulations, and programmes that direct action, but also embrace those measures that elicit from the friends wholehearted response and channel their energies.” Eliciting response is an administrative function in this system, and it is the one assigned to people who cannot compel it.

It accompanies. This word did not arrive by decree; the House of Justice noticed it arriving on its own. In the Riḍván 2010 message it named the shift as an advance at the level of culture:

“This evolution in collective consciousness is discernable in the growing frequency with which the word ‘accompany’ appears in conversations among the friends, a word that is being endowed with new meaning as it is integrated into the common vocabulary of the Bahá’í community. It signals the significant strengthening of a culture in which learning is the mode of operation, a mode that fosters the informed participation of more and more people in a united effort to apply Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings to the construction of a divine civilization… Such an approach offers a striking contrast to the spiritually bankrupt and moribund ways of an old social order that so often seeks to harness human energy through domination, through greed, through guilt or through manipulation.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

That sentence names the four alternatives to accompaniment, and it is the sharpest statement in the corpus of what the appointed arm exists instead of. Domination is the coercive option; greed the transactional one; guilt the religious one; manipulation the political one. All four work, in the short run, at harnessing human energy — that is why every social order reaches for them. The Bahá’í order forbids the appointed arm all four by the simple expedient of giving it nothing to dominate with, nothing to pay with, no confessional standing to shame with, and a rule of candour that makes manipulation pointless. What is left is walking alongside someone while they learn to do something.

The same message adds the posture accompaniment requires — “a posture of humility, a condition in which one becomes forgetful of self” — and describes the result: souls who “labour together ceaselessly, delighting not so much in their own accomplishments but in the progress and services of others.” This is not decorative. It is the operating condition of an institution whose entire output is other people’s capacity.

It circulates learning. This is the function least visible from inside a single cluster and most consequential at world scale. Because Counsellors and Board members move constantly, see many communities, and administer none of them, they constitute the Bahá’í world’s sensing organ. The 2001 document describes what the International Teaching Centre makes of that:

“A resource made available to the Counsellors by the International Teaching Centre and through them to the community at large is an accumulating store of wisdom born of experience—the experience of a highly diverse community dedicated to the creation of a new civilization. Through the network of Counsellors, Auxiliary Board members and assistants, the Teaching Centre can observe the workings of individual and collective endeavours, analysing their methods and approaches, and introducing the conclusions it draws into the processes of the systematic growth of the Faith. Thus in the institution of the Counsellors we have a system through which the lessons learned in the remotest spots on the globe can be shared with the entire body of the believers, enriching consultation, stimulating experimentation and inspiring confidence.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Note what the appointed arm does not do with what it observes: it does not turn it into policy. It turns it into a shared store that enriches consultation elsewhere. Learning travels laterally through the appointed arm and is decided upon vertically by the elected one. That division is why the Faith’s global learning does not calcify into a mandated method — and it is why the House of Justice’s most recent messages describe the appointed arm’s task in terms of tending rather than running. In its message of 31 December 2025 to the Counsellors’ Conference the House writes that “it is the institutions of the Faith that are ultimately charged with tending to this entire ecosystem of learning,” that all involved must be “connected by loving relationships imbued with a spirit of humility and magnanimity,” and asks of the Counsellors and their auxiliaries something quite precise:

“we look to you and your auxiliaries to be alert to when existing arrangements at any level of the community need to evolve to meet the requirements of growth, and then, in your interactions with relevant institutions, to encourage the emergence of new arrangements in a suitable form.” — The Universal House of Justice, 31 December 2025 (20251231-001)

Encourage the emergence. Not design, not mandate, not install. The appointed arm’s job when a community has outgrown its structures is to notice first and then to persuade the bodies that can actually change them.

What this looks like on the ground is documented in the Reflections on Growth accounts the International Teaching Centre prepares for the Counsellors, and the striking thing about them is how small the appointed arm’s visible contribution usually is. In Cobly, a locality of the Matéri cluster in north-western Benin where activities had become sporadic, the turning point after the 2013 youth conferences was a series of reflection meetings held “with the help of an Auxiliary Board member who had recently moved to the community,” at which the friends studied messages of the House of Justice and then made their own plans; the Local Assembly subsequently consulted on how to support the activities and joined the cluster agencies to serve “shoulder to shoulder” (rog-35-reinvigorating-programs-of-growth). In Alotau, Papua New Guinea, a cluster that had plateaued at some fifty activities, the Local Spiritual Assembly convened an institutional meeting in September 2014 to which the institute coordinators, the Area Teaching Committee, and the Auxiliary Board members were invited, with a National Assembly member and a Continental Counsellor also present; the cluster was divided into four sectors, the Assembly and cluster agencies agreed to meet jointly on a regular basis, and activity rose from 54 to 103 in a single cycle. In Hamilton, Canada, where the cluster agencies had stopped functioning altogether, a small group began visiting families in a diverse east-end neighbourhood — “We were accompanied and encouraged by an Auxiliary Board member, and within a few weeks after the first visits to families, a small junior youth group was formed.”

In each account, the Board member is present at the hinge and absent from the decision. Nobody was told to divide Alotau into sectors; the institutions there decided it, in a room a Counsellor happened to be in. That is the mechanism working exactly as designed, and it is why it is nearly invisible in the record.

The rules of engagement, and the two ways the relationship fails

Because the relationship between the arms is collateral rather than hierarchical, it needs more procedural care than a chain of command would. The Institution of the Counsellors devotes its longest technical section to this, and the rules only make sense once you see what they are protecting against.

The core prohibitions run in both directions. “National and Local Spiritual Assemblies cannot give any directions to Auxiliary Board members, much as they depend upon their vital services.” If a National Assembly wants a Board member to undertake a service as a Board member, it must ask the Counsellors. In the other direction: “Auxiliary Board members do not direct Local Spiritual Assemblies or individual believers in their activities, but are entirely free to make suggestions and recommendations they judge wise and necessary.” And a Board member’s recommendation that would require action by a National Assembly does not go to that Assembly directly — it “need[s] to be referred to the Counsellors, who may share them in their entirety with the National Assembly, or modify or reject them.” Reports containing news and information flow freely; recommendations requiring action are routed and filtered.

Then there are the boundaries designed to prevent the appointed arm from acquiring a shadow administration. Board members are not appointed to committees, as voting or non-voting members; Counsellors do not normally communicate directly with national committees; and within a national community, conferences and seminars are called by the National Assembly or its committees, not by the Counsellors — explicitly “to avoid an impression of two parallel series of conferences in the same country.” Counsellors are ineligible for election to administrative bodies, and an Auxiliary Board member elected to such a post must choose one role or the other. Assistants, by contrast, may serve on Assemblies, Councils, and committees simultaneously — but “do not function as assistants in the context of that membership.”

The document is unusually explicit about what all this is for, and names the two symmetric failure modes:

“These few limitations on the mode of interaction enhance collaboration between the institution of the Counsellors and Spiritual Assemblies. They ensure that the energies and time of the Auxiliary Board members are not diffused as a result of their engagement in the administration of teaching. Thus, the dangers of two extreme situations are avoided: one in which the Auxiliary Board member gradually takes over the direction of a national committee, and the other whereby he or she is sent hither and thither at the direction of the committee or the Assembly as a mere travelling teacher.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Capture and errand-running: the same relationship collapsing into a line of authority, in opposite directions. Both are common enough to be named in the foundational document, and neither announces itself. Capture happens because the Board member is competent, present, and asked; errand-running happens because the Board member is willing and the Assembly is busy. The tell for the first is a committee that stops deciding; the tell for the second is a Board member whose diary is filled by other people’s plans.

Against the risk that all this procedure hardens into legalism, the document ends the section with a corrective that should be read alongside every rule above:

“In the relationship between Auxiliary Board members and Local Spiritual Assemblies, to overstress distinctions is not only unnecessary but detrimental to the spirit of loving collaboration and encouragement which is essential to the progress of the Faith in every locality. The differentials of rank, functions or procedures between agencies of the Bahá’í administration are meant to canalize, not obstruct, the work of the Cause.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

Canalise, not obstruct. A canal exists to make water go somewhere it would not otherwise reach. The distinctions between the arms are for directing effort, not for adjudicating precedence — and the document had already said, of the attitude between the two institutions, that it “is not motivated by a legalistic application of the rules of their functional relationship.”

There is one further rule that shows how the whole design turns on relationship rather than office, and it is the sentence I would put at the centre of any training for the appointed arm:

“It falls on the Auxiliary Board members to build up a warm and loving relationship with Local Spiritual Assemblies and believers so that they will spontaneously turn to them for advice and assistance.” — The Institution of the Counsellors (1 January 2001)

The burden is on the Board member, not on the community. And the success criterion is spontaneity — that people call because they want to, not because a procedure requires it. This is the sense in which an institution without authority must earn its existence continuously. An Assembly that never meets still holds jurisdiction. An Auxiliary Board member nobody calls holds nothing at all.

The standard admits no compromise; the person is treated with forbearance

There is an obvious tension in asking one institution both to hold up an uncompromising standard and to encourage people who fall short of it. The House of Justice addressed it directly in its message of 28 December 2010, in a passage written specifically about what Auxiliary Board members and their assistants must recognise:

“On the one hand, the high standard of conduct inculcated by Bahá’u’lláh’s Revelation can admit no compromise; it can, in no wise, be lowered, and all must fix their gaze on its lofty heights. On the other, it must be acknowledged that, as human beings, we are far from perfect; what is expected of everyone is sincere daily effort. Self-righteousness is to be eschewed.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

Two precepts, described as “fundamental, interlocking” — which is to say neither survives alone. Hold the standard without the forbearance and you produce a community of the anxious and the excluded; hold the forbearance without the standard and there is nothing left to protect. The resolution is that the standard applies to conduct and the forbearance applies to persons, and the appointed arm’s characteristic error would be to reverse them.

The same balance shapes the environment the two arms are meant jointly to create. Where an Auxiliary Board member and a strong Local Assembly work well together, the 2001 document says, they “create an atmosphere of learning and disciplined behaviour, characterized by patience and forbearance toward mistakes,” and they “build and maintain unity of thought and action in an environment free of excessive criticism, of backbiting, of conflict and contention, which at the same time welcomes the expression of concern on the part of every believer.”

That last clause does a lot of work. A community can suppress criticism and call the silence unity; the guidance forecloses that reading in the same sentence in which it forbids backbiting. Concerns are welcome; they are simply to be expressed to those who can act on them rather than about them to those who cannot. It is precisely here that the appointed arm earns its keep, because a Counsellor or Board member is a place a believer can take a concern that is not the body being complained about — and one who has no power to act on it administratively, which is what makes the conversation safe for everyone.

What follows, for a reader deciding how to act

If you serve on a Spiritual Assembly. Consult the Auxiliary Board member early and informally rather than reporting to them late and formally. Nothing obliges a Local Assembly to share its minutes with a Board member, and the guidance leaves the question to the Assembly’s discretion — but the whole architecture presumes an ongoing relationship rather than periodic disclosure. Two procedural points are worth memorising because Assemblies get them wrong: you cannot give a Board member an instruction, and if you want one to perform a specific service as a Board member the request goes to the Counsellors. And if you have a serious difficulty with an Auxiliary Board member’s conduct, the referral is to the Counsellors, not a direct approach — except where the matter is purely personal, in which case a quiet conversation first is preferable.

If you are an individual believer. You may turn to either Board member in your area — Protection or Propagation — without permission, without going through your Assembly, and without needing to know which is the “right” one; the guidance explicitly declines to regulate that. If your concern is about the integrity of the Faith, the instruction is to contact whichever institution you feel moved to turn to, and it becomes that institution’s duty to route it correctly. If your concern is a personal difficulty or a conflict with another believer, expect to be pointed toward your Assembly, and understand that this is not a brush-off but the architecture working.

If you serve as an assistant. Your appointment is personal, area-specific, and made by a named Board member — the correct designation is “assistants to the Auxiliary Board members”, not to the Board. It does not disqualify you from any elected service, and it does not require you to resign anything. It also does not travel: you are an assistant in your assigned area and nowhere else, and when you sit on an Assembly you sit as a member, not as an assistant. In clusters where the Local Assembly is young, the guidance places unusual weight on you — promoting study groups, devotional meetings, children’s classes, and the Nineteen Day Feast — because the Board member cannot be everywhere and the assistant lives there.

If your community is already functioning well and you wonder what the appointed arm adds. It adds the thing you cannot see from inside: what is being learned in the next cluster, the next country, the next continent. No local body can generate that for itself, and the appointed arm is the only structure in the Faith whose members routinely traverse all three scales. If your community’s plans have not changed in response to anything learned elsewhere in the last year, the appointed arm is under-used.

If you build software or work with data. There is a genuine and specific contribution here, and an equally specific trap. The contribution is that the appointed arm’s distinctive value — circulating what is learned — is bottlenecked on knowledge moving between people who meet rarely and write reports that are read once. Tools that help a Board member find the community that already solved the problem in front of them, that make the Reflections on Growth corpus and the House of Justice’s messages searchable against a real situation, or that let an institute’s learning reach the next institute without a conference, all press directly on that bottleneck. The trap is that the obvious product — a dashboard of clusters, activities, and participation, with each Board member’s territory rendered as a set of metrics — quietly builds the reporting line the architecture forbids. A system that lets a Counsellor monitor performance is a system that has converted accompaniment into supervision, and it will be used that way whatever the design intent. Three constraints follow, and they are unusual enough to be worth stating as requirements: information about a community should flow to that community first; recommendations requiring action must route through the channels the 2001 document specifies rather than around them — a Board member’s recommendation to a National Assembly goes via the Counsellors, who may modify or reject it, and no tool should make it easier to bypass that; and the files belong to the institution, not the individual — the guidance says so explicitly, which means retention, handover at end of term, and confidentiality are design requirements rather than afterthoughts. Build for circulation, not for oversight.

And the general judgement. When something in a Bahá’í community is not working and the instinctive question is who is in charge of this, the architecture’s answer is usually that nobody is, and that the question is the wrong one. The productive question is which consultation has not yet happened, and who should be in the room. Very often the answer includes the one person present who cannot tell anybody what to do.

Open questions / honest limits

  • The numbers are approximate and dated. The figure of 1,134 Auxiliary Board members is exact but comes from the House of Justice’s message of 25 November 2016, which announced an increase of 144, equally divided between the Boards for Protection and Propagation; I have not located a corpus source for a later revision. The figure of roughly ninety Continental Counsellors appointed every five years comes from bahai.org’s descriptive page on the institution, not from an authoritative message, and should be treated as approximate. The number of assistants worldwide is not published anywhere I can find, and the 2001 document’s provision that Board members may name assistants for terms of varying length, or for a single project, means the number is genuinely fluid rather than merely unreported.
  • This page describes the architecture; it cannot report how it feels in practice. The corpus states what Counsellors and Board members are to do. It does not describe the ordinary texture of the relationship — how often a typical Local Assembly actually consults its Board member, how requests are handled in practice, or how the two failure modes named in 2001 present themselves in a given national community. The field accounts drawn on here (Benin, Papua New Guinea, Canada) are selected and published by the International Teaching Centre precisely because they went well.
  • The 1972 and 2001 wordings differ slightly and I have not resolved the difference. The 24 April 1972 letter says the appointed institutions have “no legislative, administrative or judicial authority”; The Institution of the Counsellors (2001) says “no legislative, executive or judicial authority” — and the 2001 document elsewhere states plainly that Counsellors do carry administrative duties relating to their offices, funds, and Board members, and may be given temporarily administrative functions by the House of Justice. The later formulation is the more precise one and the one this page follows; whether the 1972 wording is a variant translation or a genuine refinement is not something the sources settle.
  • The boundary between “advice” and “direction” is not defined, and probably cannot be. A Counsellor’s counsel carries the weight of rank and of the House of Justice’s appointment. Whether a given Assembly experienced a given recommendation as advice or as instruction is a matter of relationship and temperament, not of rule. The guidance addresses this by cultivating the right spirit rather than by drawing the line, which is honest but leaves the practical question open.
  • This page does not treat consultation itself, which is the method by which the elected arm actually decides and the medium in which most of the interaction described here takes place; see consultation. Nor does it treat the Hands of the Cause as an institution in their own right, the mechanics of Covenant-breaking and reinstatement, the internal workings of the International Teaching Centre, or the relationship between Counsellors and Regional Bahá’í Councils and National Growth Committees beyond what bears on the central question.
  • The 27 March 1978 letter is a Department of the Secretariat letter, written on behalf of the House of Justice rather than signed by it. On the precedence rules used in this knowledge base, such letters carry the same authenticity as signed letters; readers who weight them differently should note where the argument rests on it — principally the account of what “rank” means.
