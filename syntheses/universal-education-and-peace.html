Universal Education and the Peace of the World: The Sixth Issue in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

In October 1985 the Universal House of Justice addressed a statement “To the Peoples of the World.” Part II of The Promise of World Peace reviews the declarations and conventions adopted through the United Nations and then singles out “a few such issues” that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven: racism, the disparity between rich and poor, unbridled nationalism, religious strife, the emancipation of women, universal education, and an international auxiliary language. Universal education is the sixth, and its paragraph has six sentences:

“The cause of universal education, which has already enlisted in its service an army of dedicated people from every faith and nation, deserves the utmost support that the governments of the world can lend it. For ignorance is indisputably the principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples and the perpetuation of prejudice. No nation can achieve success unless education is accorded all its citizens. Lack of resources limits the ability of many nations to fulfill this necessity, imposing a certain ordering of priorities. The decision-making agencies involved would do well to consider giving first priority to the education of women and girls, since it is through educated mothers that the benefits of knowledge can be most effectively and rapidly diffused throughout society. In keeping with the requirements of the times, consideration should also be given to teaching the concept of world citizenship as part of the standard education of every child.” — The Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, October 1985, Part II

This page reads the paragraph closely, places it in the argument of the whole statement, and follows its theme outward: to the book ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá wrote in 1875 from which two of its sentences descend, to the laws and counsels of Bahá’u’lláh, to ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s Tablets and talks, to Shoghi Effendi, and to the messages of the Universal House of Justice from 1989 to 2018. It ends with a section prepared for a songwriter.

The knowledge base has no earlier synthesis on education. Several pages touch parts of the subject and are not repeated here: the equality of women and men and the companion article on the fifth issue treat the priority of daughters’ education as part of the equality principle; the parenting guide treats the education of children at home; the institute process and the core activities treat the Bahá’í community’s own educational programmes; and how the Bahá’í community learns treats the generation of knowledge in the community’s work. This page asks a narrower question: why the House of Justice treats education as a condition of peace, and what the sources say about the kind of education that serves it.

The paragraph, sentence by sentence

“An army of dedicated people from every faith and nation.” The paragraph opens with a tribute before it makes a request. The cause already exists and already has its workers, and they are not Bahá’ís only; they come “from every faith and nation.” The statement asks governments to support a movement that is under way. The same word returns in Part IV, where the House of Justice describes “the army of men and women, drawn from virtually every culture, race and nation on earth, who serve the multifarious agencies of the United Nations” as a sign of the drive towards world unity.

“The utmost support that the governments of the world can lend it.” The request is addressed to governments. This matters because the Bahá’í Writings place the duty to educate first on parents, and then, where parents cannot meet it, on the community. The statement carries the same duty to the largest scale: the governments of the world.

“Ignorance is indisputably the principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples and the perpetuation of prejudice.” This sentence gives two reasons in one. The first is civilisational: peoples decline and fall through ignorance. The second is moral and social: prejudice is kept alive through ignorance. The second reason is what ties education to peace. The first issue on the statement’s list is racism, and the third section of the statement says that world order requires “abandonment of prejudice—prejudice of every kind.” If ignorance perpetuates prejudice, education is one of the means by which the earlier remedies take hold.

“No nation can achieve success unless education is accorded all its citizens.” The word that carries the sentence is all. A nation that educates a class, a sex, a religious majority or a city, and leaves the rest, has not met the condition.

“A certain ordering of priorities … first priority to the education of women and girls.” The statement is practical about scarcity. It does not say that every nation can educate everyone at once. It says that where means are short, girls and women should come first, and it gives the reason: “it is through educated mothers that the benefits of knowledge can be most effectively and rapidly diffused throughout society.” The argument is about how knowledge spreads. A mother is a child’s first teacher; what she knows reaches the next generation earliest.

“Teaching the concept of world citizenship as part of the standard education of every child.” The last sentence asks for a content as well as a quantity. Education that spreads literacy and skill but teaches children that their nation, race or religion stands above others would not serve peace. The phrase “in keeping with the requirements of the times” links this to the preceding paragraph on nationalism, which says that “the concept of world citizenship is a direct result of the contraction of the world into a single neighborhood through scientific advances and of the indisputable interdependence of nations,” and that “love of all the world’s peoples does not exclude love of one’s country.”

Where two of the sentences come from

In 1875 ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá wrote an open letter to the people and rulers of Persia, published without His name and later known as The Secret of Divine Civilization. In it He wrote:

“The primary, the most urgent requirement is the promotion of education. It is inconceivable that any nation should achieve prosperity and success unless this paramount, this fundamental concern is carried forward. The principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples is ignorance. Today the mass of the people are uninformed even as to ordinary affairs, how much less do they grasp the core of the important problems and complex needs of the time.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Secret of Divine Civilization

Set beside the 1985 paragraph, the correspondence is close:

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, 1875The Universal House of Justice, 1985
“The principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples is ignorance.”“Ignorance is indisputably the principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples and the perpetuation of prejudice.”
“It is inconceivable that any nation should achieve prosperity and success unless this paramount, this fundamental concern is carried forward.”“No nation can achieve success unless education is accorded all its citizens.”
“Most of the population lack even the vocabulary to explain what they want.”Part II: the paralysis of will “is also traceable to the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order.”

The House of Justice does not cite the source, and nothing in the statement says the wording was borrowed deliberately. The match is a textual observation. What it shows is that the 1985 paragraph restates, for the whole world, a diagnosis ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá had made for one country 110 years earlier. The 1985 version adds the word “prejudice.”

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s book gave a concrete case. In 1875 China had “something over four hundred million inhabitants” and yet, “because of its lack of education in cultural and material civilization,” it was “the feeblest and the most helpless of all weak nations”; Japan, a fraction of its size, had adopted education and the sciences and had advanced. He drew the conclusion: “Observe carefully how education and the arts of civilization bring honor, prosperity, independence and freedom to a government and its people.”

The third line of the table is the most important for the argument of the statement. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá described ordinary Persians who could not obtain justice because they had no words to state their case:

“At present, however, because of their inadequate schooling, most of the population lack even the vocabulary to explain what they want.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, The Secret of Divine Civilization

The House of Justice returned to this book in a letter of 26 November 2003 to the Bahá’ís of Iran, “The Challenge of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s The Secret of Divine Civilization for Iran Today.” It listed the features of modernity that the book had identified, among them “universal education,” and summarised its central argument: “it is readily observable that human development depends on education.”

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The paralysis of will. Part II opens by saying that bans on particular weapons “will not remove the root causes of war,” and that the obstacle is “a paralysis of will.” The statement gives the paralysis three roots: “a deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind,” an “unwillingness to face courageously the far-reaching implications of establishing a united world authority,” and “the incapacity of largely ignorant and subjugated masses to articulate their desire for a new order in which they can live in peace, harmony and prosperity with all humanity.” Of the seven issues, universal education is the one that answers the third root directly. A people who cannot read, and who lack the words to say what they want, cannot add their voice to a demand for peace. Education gives the majority of humanity the means to ask for it.

Raising the context to the level of principle. After the seven issues, the statement makes “two points” that apply to all of them: the abolition of war “is not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols,” and the “primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism.” Education is the most practical of the seven issues — budgets, schools, priorities — and the statement still asks that it be governed by principle: girls first, world citizenship taught to every child.

Part I: the human spirit and its Educators. The first section of the statement is an argument about human nature. It denies that human beings are “incorrigibly selfish and aggressive” and says that such conduct “represents a distortion of the human spirit.” It names the mind as the spirit’s “essential quality,” and it describes the Founders of the religions as “the Educators of its collective childhood”: “Had humanity seen the Educators of its collective childhood in their true character, as agents of one civilizing process, it would no doubt have reaped incalculably greater benefits.” The whole statement is built on the idea that humanity is educable, passing through “stages analogous to the stages of infancy and childhood” towards “its long-awaited coming of age.” The sixth issue applies to each child what Part I says of the race.

Part III: oneness taught in schools. The third section says the oneness of mankind “should therefore be universally proclaimed, taught in schools, and constantly asserted in every nation as preparation for the organic change in the structure of society which it implies.” It then quotes Shoghi Effendi’s description of a future world community in which “the fury of a capricious and militant nationalism will have been transmuted into an abiding consciousness of world citizenship.” The education paragraph supplies the means: world citizenship as “part of the standard education of every child.” Part III also says that the will to act for peace depends on “the reality of man, namely, his thought,” actualised “through candid, dispassionate and cordial consultation.”

Part IV: the gemlike reality of man. The closing section gives thanks to the Creator who has “exalted the gemlike reality of man; honored it with intellect and wisdom, nobility and immortality.” The image is Bahá’u’lláh’s, discussed below: man as “a mine rich in gems of inestimable value” whose treasures only education can reveal.

The neighbours on the list. The paragraph before is on the emancipation of women; the girls-first sentence connects the two. The paragraph after is on an international auxiliary language, and Bahá’u’lláh pictured that language being “taught to children in schools throughout the world” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Bishárát, the third Glad-Tidings). The seven issues are not separate campaigns. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá had said the same to the peace organisation at The Hague in 1919: Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings “were not limited to the establishment of universal peace. They embraced many teachings which supplemented and supported that of universal peace,” and these “must be added to the matter of universal peace and combined with it, so that results may accrue.” Education is one of the teachings He listed.

The Writings of Bahá’u’lláh: a law, and a mine of gems

Bahá’u’lláh’s account of education begins with what a human being is.

“Man is the supreme Talisman. Lack of a proper education hath, however, deprived him of that which he doth inherently possess. … The Great Being saith: Regard man as a mine rich in gems of inestimable value. Education can, alone, cause it to reveal its treasures, and enable mankind to benefit therefrom.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, CXXII

The passage makes two claims. The capacities are already present: the gems are in the mine before anyone digs. And without education they stay buried. A person deprived of education is deprived of “that which he doth inherently possess” — something that belongs to him. The same passage, read on, ties this to peace: if “the learned and worldly-wise men of this age were to allow mankind to inhale the fragrance of fellowship and love,” every understanding heart would “discover the secret of undisturbed peace.”

Bahá’u’lláh made education a law. In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas He wrote:

“Unto every father hath been enjoined the instruction of his son and daughter in the art of reading and writing and in all that hath been laid down in the Holy Tablet. He that putteth away that which is commanded unto him, the Trustees are then to take from him that which is required for their instruction if he be wealthy and, if not, the matter devolveth upon the House of Justice. Verily have We made it a shelter for the poor and needy. He that bringeth up his son or the son of another, it is as though he hath brought up a son of Mine; upon him rest My glory, My loving-kindness, My mercy, that have compassed the world.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶48

The law names both son and daughter. It sets up a chain of responsibility: the father; if he neglects it and is wealthy, the Trustees take the cost from him; if he is poor, the House of Justice bears it. No child is to go uneducated because a family is poor. The last sentence extends the blessing beyond one’s own children to “the son of another.” In the Ishráqát, Bahá’u’lláh quotes this verse again, opening with “The Pen of Glory counseleth everyone regarding the instruction and education of children” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, the seventh Ishráq).

He also said what kind of knowledge He meant:

“Knowledge is as wings to man’s life, and a ladder for his ascent. Its acquisition is incumbent upon everyone. The knowledge of such sciences, however, should be acquired as can profit the peoples of the earth, and not those which begin with words and end with words.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Tajallíyát, the third Tajallí

Knowledge here is useful knowledge, measured by whether it profits “the peoples of the earth.” And on religious instruction He set a limit in the same breath as the duty:

“Schools must first train the children in the principles of religion … but this in such a measure that it may not injure the children by resulting in ignorant fanaticism and bigotry.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Kalimát-i-Firdawsíyyih (Words of Paradise), the eighth leaf

An education that produces “ignorant fanaticism and bigotry” fails the test, whatever its religious content. That limit connects education to the fourth issue on the statement’s list, religious strife.

Bahá’u’lláh tied the education of peoples to the end of division in so many words:

“Bend your minds and wills to the education of the peoples and kindreds of the earth, that haply the dissensions that divide it may, through the power of the Most Great Name, be blotted out from its face, and all mankind become the upholders of one Order, and the inhabitants of one City.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, CLVI

The purpose clause — “that haply the dissensions that divide it may … be blotted out” — is the 1985 paragraph’s argument in Bahá’u’lláh’s own words. Education is directed at unity.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: a universal law, and the light and darkness of every child

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá stated the principle as a law for the whole world. In Montreal in 1912 He said:

“Bahá’u’lláh has announced that inasmuch as ignorance and lack of education are barriers of separation among mankind, all must receive training and instruction. Through this provision the lack of mutual understanding will be remedied and the unity of mankind furthered and advanced. Universal education is a universal law. It is, therefore, incumbent upon every father to teach and instruct his children according to his possibilities. If he is unable to educate them, the body politic, the representative of the people, must provide the means for their education.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Church of the Messiah, Montreal, 1 September 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

The reason He gives is the same one the House of Justice gives in 1985: ignorance separates people. The remedy is also the same: where the family cannot pay, the public must. In His 1919 Tablet to the Central Organization for a Durable Peace at The Hague, He listed education among the principles that support peace:

“And among the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh is the promotion of education. Every child must be instructed in sciences as much as is necessary. If the parents are able to provide the expenses of this education, it is well, otherwise the community must provide the means for the teaching of that child.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, First Tablet to The Hague, 17 December 1919

Read in order, the sources widen the circle of responsibility step by step: the father and the House of Justice in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, the community and “the body politic” in ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s Tablet and talk, and “the governments of the world” in 1985.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá also said that education is no longer optional:

“Were there no educator, all souls would remain savage, and were it not for the teacher, the children would be ignorant creatures. It is for this reason that, in this new cycle, education and training are recorded in the Book of God as obligatory and not voluntary.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, no. 98

And He set its reach at the last child in the last village:

“In this new and wondrous Cause, the advancement of all branches of knowledge is a fixed and vital principle, and the friends, one and all, are obligated to make every effort toward this end, so that the Cause of the Manifest Light may be spread abroad, and that every child, according to his need, will receive his share of the sciences and arts—until not even a single peasant’s child will be found who is completely devoid of schooling.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, from a Tablet, in Social Action (Research Department compilation, 2020), no. 193

Three kinds of education

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá distinguished kinds of education. In Some Answered Questions He explained that “education is of three kinds: material, human, and spiritual.” Material education concerns the body and is “common to both man and animal.” Human education “consists in civilization and progress, that is, sound governance, social order, human welfare, commerce and industry, arts and sciences, momentous discoveries, and great undertakings.” Of the third He said:

“As to divine education, it is the education of the Kingdom and consists in acquiring divine perfections. This is indeed true education, for by its virtue man becomes the focal centre of divine blessings and the embodiment of the verse ‘Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness.’” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Some Answered Questions, ch. 3, “The Need for an Educator”

He gave the purpose of the Prophets in the language of education: “to educate humanity, that this lump of coal may become a diamond and this barren tree may be grafted and yield fruit of the utmost sweetness and delicacy” (Some Answered Questions, ch. 64). This is the background of the statement’s phrase “the Educators of its collective childhood.”

Character before learning

The Writings do not treat literacy and skill as sufficient. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá wrote:

“Training in morals and good conduct is far more important than book learning. A child that is cleanly, agreeable, of good character, well-behaved—even though he be ignorant—is preferable to a child that is rude, unwashed, ill-natured, and yet becoming deeply versed in all the sciences and arts. … If, however, the child be trained to be both learned and good, the result is light upon light.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Selections, no. 110

This qualifies the statement’s paragraph without contradicting it. The 1985 paragraph speaks to governments, about access; ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá speaks to teachers, about content. A world in which every child could read, and in which schools taught contempt for other peoples, would have universal education in one sense and not in the sense that serves peace. That is why the statement adds world citizenship to the curriculum.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s account of why a child’s education matters so much rests on the double possibility in human nature:

“For the inner reality of man is a demarcation line between the shadow and the light, a place where the two seas meet; it is the lowest point on the arc of descent, and therefore is it capable of gaining all the grades above. With education it can achieve all excellence; devoid of education it will stay on, at the lowest point of imperfection. Every child is potentially the light of the world—and at the same time its darkness; wherefore must the question of education be accounted as of primary importance.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Selections, no. 103

In the same Tablet He asks that the teacher be “a doctor to the character of the child,” and He describes what such education could change: “The very demons will change to angels then, and wolves to shepherds of the flock.” In another He names ignorance as the root of wrongdoing and calls the world a school: “The root cause of wrongdoing is ignorance, and we must therefore hold fast to the tools of perception and knowledge. Good character must be taught. Light must be spread afar, so that, in the school of humanity, all may acquire the heavenly characteristics of the spirit” (Selections, no. 111). And: “All humankind are as children in a school, and the Dawning-Points of Light, the Sources of divine revelation, are the teachers, wondrous and without peer” (Selections, no. 102).

Girls first

The 1985 priority for women and girls rests on ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s explicit instruction:

“The question of training the children and looking after the orphans is extremely important, but most important of all is the education of girl children, for these girls will one day be mothers, and the mother is the first teacher of the child. In whatever way she reareth the child, so will the child become, and the results of that first training will remain with the individual throughout his entire life, and it would be most difficult to alter them. And how can a mother, herself ignorant and untrained, educate her child? It is therefore clear that the education of girls is of far greater consequence than that of boys.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, from a Tablet, in Social Action, no. 192

The same Tablet continues: “I hope, therefore, that the friends will make strenuous efforts to educate their children, sons and daughters alike.” Priority under scarcity is not the neglect of boys. To a school for girls ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá wrote: “The school for girls taketh precedence over the school for boys, for it is incumbent upon the girls of this glorious era to be fully versed in the various branches of knowledge, in sciences and the arts and all the wonders of this pre-eminent time” (Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks).

When the authorised English translation of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas was published in 1992, note 76 on the education law summarised this guidance. It quotes ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá that the “training and culture of daughters is more necessary than that of sons,” and concludes that if a family cannot educate all its children, “preference is to be accorded to daughters since, through educated mothers, the benefits of knowledge can be most effectively and rapidly diffused throughout society.” The last clause is, nearly word for word, the reason the 1985 statement gives to governments.

In Philadelphia in 1912 ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá connected equal education to the end of war:

“He promulgated the adoption of the same course of education for man and woman. Daughters and sons must follow the same curriculum of study, thereby promoting unity of the sexes. When all mankind shall receive the same opportunity of education and the equality of men and women be realized, the foundations of war will be utterly destroyed.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Unitarian Church, Philadelphia, 9 June 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

Shoghi Effendi and the schools of Iran

Shoghi Effendi listed “the introduction of compulsory education” among the “essential elements of that Divine polity” that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá proclaimed in the West in 1911–1913 (God Passes By). In a letter of 30 January 1926 to the Local Spiritual Assemblies of the East he set out their duties as the Texts had laid them down:

“These bid them to work towards the improvement of morals and the spread of learning; to strive to eradicate ignorance and unenlightenment, eliminate prejudice, and reinforce the foundation of true faith in people’s hearts and minds … to promote the emancipation and advancement of women and support the compulsory education of both sexes.” — Shoghi Effendi, letter of 30 January 1926 (translated from the Persian), in Social Action, no. 131

The pairing in the first sentence — “eradicate ignorance … eliminate prejudice” — is the pairing of the 1985 paragraph.

The Bahá’ís of Iran put this guidance into practice. Century of Light, written under the supervision of the House of Justice, records that “the emphasis in the teachings on education provided the impulse for the establishment of Bahá’í schools—including the Tarbíyat school for girls, which gained national renown—in the capital, as well as in provincial centres.” Its note adds: “The school was closed in 1934, by order of Reza Shah, because it had observed Bahá’í Holy Days as religious holidays. The closing of all other Bahá’í schools in Iran followed.” Shoghi Effendi recorded the same event in God Passes By: “the closing of all schools belonging to the Bahá’í community in that country, as a direct consequence of the refusal of the representatives of that community to permit official Bahá’í institutions … to transgress the clearly revealed law requiring the suspension of work on Bahá’í Holy Days.” He also wrote that the community’s “immediate closing of its schools in Persia” was proof of its obedience to government.

Western believers served in those schools. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá wrote of a school for girls in Tehran, “Erelong will women in every field keep pace with the men,” and sent greetings to “the teacher in the school, Miss Lillian Kappes” (Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks). In God Passes By Shoghi Effendi named “Susan Moody and Lillian Kappes and their valiant associates who lie buried in Ṭihrán” among those whose sacrifice had brought honour to the American Bahá’í community.

The Universal House of Justice after 1985

Literacy as a right (1989). Four years after the statement, the House of Justice asked every Assembly to work towards eliminating illiteracy from the Bahá’í community:

“The most immediate access to the dynamic influence of the sacred Word is through reading. The ability to read is therefore a fundamental right and privilege of every human being. Bahá’u’lláh promotes this right in His command to parents to ensure the instruction of their sons and daughters in the ‘art of reading and writing.’” — The Universal House of Justice, 10 July 1989, to all National Spiritual Assemblies

The letter commended to the Bahá’í world the United Nations’ International Literacy Year, 1990, and pointed to Iran as the example: “In the earliest years of this century, when no systematic, overall plan of education existed in Iran, the Bahá’ís seized their chance and organized a widespread program of education.” It also asked that adults who want to learn to read “be encouraged and assisted to achieve, with dignity, their heart’s desire.”

The record of Iran. In a letter of 20 June 2008 to the Bahá’ís of Iran, the House of Justice recalled that “in 1911, nearly a century ago, you founded the Tarbíyat School for Girls in Ṭihrán, which provided girls of all backgrounds with an opportunity for education,” and that “long ago you succeeded in eliminating in your community illiteracy among women under the age of forty.” On 9 November 2018 it described the early Iranian community again: “A network of schools that provided moral and academic education, including for girls, flourished. Illiteracy was virtually eliminated in the Bahá’í community.” A letter of 1 August 2014 recalled the same effort in ‘Ishqábád, where the believers ensured “that all Bahá’í children and youth were literate in a society with rampant illiteracy, especially among girls.”

Classrooms in homes. The same community was later barred from the schools it had helped to build. The 26 November 2003 letter says:

“When your children were expelled from schools because of their Faith, you created classrooms in your homes. Graduates of the institution you founded to meet the needs of university students, who are similarly denied education, are today distinguishing themselves in prestigious universities in other countries where their credentials have been gladly accepted.” — The Universal House of Justice, 26 November 2003, to the Bahá’ís of Iran

The institution is the Bahá’í Institute for Higher Education. A Bahá’í International Community open letter of 26 August 2011, which the House of Justice sent to all National Assemblies, explains how it began: after the revolution Bahá’í students were expelled from the universities and Bahá’í professors dismissed, and “the community made informal arrangements to use the volunteer services of dismissed professors to teach Bahá’í youth.” On 21 May 2011 agents of the Ministry of Intelligence raided the homes of people involved in the Institute in several cities; at least thirty households were searched and sixteen Bahá’ís were arrested (letter written on behalf of the House of Justice, 25 May 2011). For the wider history of the persecution, see SituationOfBahaisInIran.

Ignorance as oppression (2010). The House of Justice’s fullest development of the 1985 sentence on ignorance came in its Riḍván 2010 message, in a passage on social action:

“One is the centrality of knowledge to social existence. The perpetuation of ignorance is a most grievous form of oppression; it reinforces the many walls of prejudice that stand as barriers to the realization of the oneness of humankind, at once the goal and operating principle of Bahá’u’lláh’s Revelation. Access to knowledge is the right of every human being, and participation in its generation, application and diffusion a responsibility that all must shoulder in the great enterprise of building a prosperous world civilization—each individual according to his or her talents and abilities. Justice demands universal participation.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010

Two things have changed since 1985. Ignorance is now named as a form of oppression — something done to people, not only a lack in them. And the goal is no longer only that knowledge reach everyone, but that everyone take part in generating and applying it. The 1985 image of knowledge “diffused throughout society” from educated mothers becomes, in 2010, a world in which every person is both a learner and a contributor. A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 27 December 2017, on religious prejudice, quotes this passage and says that “a great share of the Bahá’í community’s efforts has been directed at addressing the root cause of religious prejudice—ignorance.”

A sign of integration (2006). In its Riḍván 2006 message the House of Justice listed “the systematic pursuit of universal education” among the developments in the wider world through which “the spirit of world solidarity” that Shoghi Effendi described has spread, alongside “the rejection of deeply ingrained racial prejudices” and “the dawning consciousness of world citizenship.” The “army of dedicated people” of 1985 is, in this reading, part of the same process.

The Bahá’í community’s own educational work since the 1990s — children’s classes, junior youth groups, and the study circles of the training institute — is treated in CoreActivities and InstituteProcess, and the community’s approach to social and economic development in SocialAction.

Institutional elaboration: the Bahá’í International Community

The Bahá’í International Community, the Faith’s representative body at the United Nations, carried the paragraph into international forums. Its statements are institutional and do not carry the authority of the House of Justice, but they show how the paragraph was read at the time.

  • In July 1986, in Barcelona, it asked what kind of people would live in a world where nations had agreed to stop fighting: “the same people who today, with their moral and spiritual nature barely developed, harbor prejudices and hatreds that keep the flame of discrimination intensely alive.” It concluded that turning awareness of the oneness of humanity into practice “is the responsibility then of universal education” (Education for Peace and Unity, 7 July 1986).
  • In June 1993 it recommended “that world citizenship be taught in every school” and that “the principle of world citizenship” become “part of the standard education of every child” (World Citizenship: A Global Ethic for Sustainable Development).
  • In March 1995, on the education of girls and women, it observed that “the continuing failure to ensure the education of girls suggests a lack of will” (Educating Girls and Women) — the same diagnosis the Promise makes of peace as a whole.

The present field (external sources — description only, current as of October 2026)

The following describes the state of education in the world. It is not authority for any Bahá’í principle, and the figures will change.

  • UNESCO reported on 13 June 2025 that “the number of out-of-school children worldwide stands at 272 million,” an estimate for 2023 published with its SDG 4 Scorecard. The figure was 21 million higher than the previous estimate, mainly because of revised United Nations population estimates and an increase in out-of-school children in Afghanistan.
  • UNESCO’s factsheet for International Literacy Day 2025 states: “In 2024, 739 million adults still lacked basic literacy skills,” and “women made up to nearly two-thirds of the 739 million illiterate adults (466 million).”
  • On 11 August 2026, five years after the Taliban takeover, UNESCO reported that about 2.4 million girls in Afghanistan were excluded from secondary education, and its programme coordinator said that “Afghanistan remains the only country in the world where girls and women are formally banned from secondary and higher education” (as reported by the Associated Press).

In 1985 the statement said that lack of resources forces “a certain ordering of priorities” and asked that girls and women come first. Forty years later, women are still close to two-thirds of the adults who cannot read, and one government bars girls from secondary school by law.

Open questions and limits

  • The source of the 1985 wording. The correspondence between the paragraph and The Secret of Divine Civilization is shown above from the texts. The House of Justice does not cite the book in the statement, and this research found no statement that the wording was taken from it deliberately.
  • Talks and Tablets. The Montreal and Philadelphia passages are from The Promulgation of Universal Peace, which records talks from stenographic or other notes. They agree with the Tablets quoted here (the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, the Tablet to The Hague, the Tablets on girls’ schools), which are the firmer ground.
  • Older translations. The Research Department’s 1976 compilation Bahá’í Education (revised 1990), which is in the corpus in a non-official copy, renders several of these passages differently from the current bahai.org texts — for example, “the light of the world and at the same time its triple darkness” where the current text of Selections no. 103 reads “the light of the world—and at the same time its darkness.” This page quotes the current texts.
  • What “world citizenship” in the curriculum means. The statement says “consideration should also be given” — it is a recommendation, and it does not describe a curriculum. The 1993 Bahá’í International Community proposal gives more detail, at institutional level only.
  • The education law in practice. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas assigns the cost of a poor child’s education to the House of Justice. How that provision will operate is not addressed in the sources used here.
  • Iran after 2011. The corpus messages consulted here describe the Bahá’í Institute for Higher Education up to 2011. This research did not establish the present state of Bahá’í access to higher education in Iran.
  • Web figures are from UNESCO and the Associated Press as of the dates given, and will age.

For the song

The heart of it, in one sentence. The House of Justice asks the governments of the world to give universal education their utmost support, because ignorance is the main reason peoples decline and prejudice survives; where means are short, girls and women should be taught first, because educated mothers spread knowledge fastest; and every child should be taught to be a citizen of the world.

Concrete images, stories, people, places and moments

  • The mine and its gems — every human being a mine of treasures that only education brings out (Bahá’u’lláh); the statement’s closing thanks for “the gemlike reality of man.”
  • Wings and a ladder — knowledge as what lifts a person.
  • Coal into diamond; a barren tree grafted — the work of the Prophets as Educators.
  • The two seas — the inner reality of a child where shadow and light meet; each child “the light of the world—and at the same time its darkness.”
  • The teacher as doctor — healing faults of character while giving learning.
  • A green branch — “Children are even as a branch that is fresh and green; they will grow up in whatever way ye train them” (Selections, no. 110).
  • A father’s duty and a shelter for the poor — the Kitáb-i-Aqdas: if the father is poor, the House of Justice pays; the child of another raised as “a son of Mine.”
  • The peasant’s child — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s measure: not one village child left without schooling.
  • No words to say what they want — the Persians of 1875 who could not state their own case; the “ignorant and subjugated masses” of 1985 who cannot ask for peace.
  • China and Japan, 1875 — the most populous nation, without education, defeated; a small one, with education, advancing.
  • The Tarbíyat School for Girls, Tehran, 1911 — founded by the Bahá’ís; closed in 1934 by order of Reza Shah because it kept the Bahá’í Holy Days; all other Bahá’í schools in Iran closed after it.
  • Susan Moody and Lillian Kappes — American Bahá’ís who taught and served in Iran and are buried in Tehran.
  • Women under forty — the Iranian Bahá’í community that eliminated illiteracy among them long ago.
  • ‘Ishqábád — a community that made all its children literate where girls were rarely taught.
  • Classrooms in homes; professors without posts — Bahá’í children expelled from school, taught in living rooms; dismissed professors teaching young people who were barred from university; homes raided on 21 May 2011.
  • An army from every faith and nation — the teachers and workers of the world’s education, already enlisted in 1985.
  • The world as one school — all humankind as children in a school, with the Prophets as teachers.

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • Not schooling alone. The sources ask for knowledge that profits the peoples of the earth, joined to character. “Training in morals and good conduct is far more important than book learning,” and learning with good character is “light upon light.” A song that praises diplomas and test scores as the road to peace says less than the texts.
  • Not contempt for the unschooled. The statement condemns ignorance, not ignorant people. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá preferred a well-behaved child “even though he be ignorant” to a learned child of bad character, and the 1989 letter asks that adults learning to read be helped “with dignity.” A song that mocks those without education turns the principle against the people it is meant to serve.
  • Girls first does not mean boys last, and women are not valued only as mothers. The priority applies where resources are short. The same Tablet asks for “sons and daughters alike,” and ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá asked for “the same curriculum” for both. The mother-as-first-educator argument explains why the priority spreads knowledge fastest; it is not the whole case for educating women, which the fifth issue on the list makes on grounds of equality.
  • Not a Bahá’í project only. The “army” of 1985 is drawn “from every faith and nation,” and the request is to governments. A song that presents the Bahá’ís as the only people working for education misstates the paragraph.
  • Religious education without fanaticism. Bahá’u’lláh asks for religious training “in such a measure that it may not injure the children by resulting in ignorant fanaticism and bigotry.” A song should not suggest that peace comes from teaching children one creed against others.
  • World citizenship does not cancel love of country. The statement says “love of all the world’s peoples does not exclude love of one’s country.”
  • Not a song of grievance about Iran. The House of Justice describes the Iranian Bahá’ís’ response to being shut out of schools as resourcefulness — “you created classrooms in your homes” — and the 2003 letter speaks of their “abiding love for the land in which you have suffered so greatly.” The story is about what they built.
  • Attribute accurately. “The principal reason for the decline and fall of peoples is ignorance” is ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s, from 1875; the version with “and the perpetuation of prejudice” is the House of Justice’s, from 1985. “Universal education is a universal law” is from a talk in Montreal, reported in The Promulgation of Universal Peace, not from a Tablet. “Light of the world … and its triple darkness” is an older rendering; the current text says “its darkness.” “Educators,” with a capital, in the statement means the Founders of the religions.
