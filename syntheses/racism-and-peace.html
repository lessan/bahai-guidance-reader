Racism and the Peace of the World: The First Issue in The Promise of World Peace

Last updated 8 October 2026

In October 1985 the Universal House of Justice addressed a statement “To the Peoples of the World.” Part II of The Promise of World Peace reviews the international declarations and conventions of the preceding decades, and then singles out a few issues that, “because of their immediate relevance to establishing world peace, deserve additional comment.” There are seven. Racism comes first, and its paragraph has four sentences:

“Racism, one of the most baneful and persistent evils, is a major barrier to peace. Its practice perpetrates too outrageous a violation of the dignity of human beings to be countenanced under any pretext. Racism retards the unfoldment of the boundless potentialities of its victims, corrupts its perpetrators, and blights human progress. Recognition of the oneness of mankind, implemented by appropriate legal measures, must be universally upheld if this problem is to be overcome.” — The Universal House of Justice, The Promise of World Peace, October 1985, Part II

This page reads that paragraph closely, places it in the argument of the whole statement, and follows its theme outward: through the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, the talks and actions of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, the letters of Shoghi Effendi, and the messages of the Universal House of Justice from 1985 to 2020. It ends with a section prepared for a songwriter.

The knowledge base already has a long article on unity in diversity, which covers the garden image, freedom from prejudice as a daily discipline, and the procedural rules (the minority tie-break, the absence of nominations) by which Bahá’í communities protect difference. This page does not repeat that material. Its subject is narrower: why the House of Justice treats racism as a barrier to peace, what harm it names, what remedy it prescribes, and how its guidance on the subject has developed since 1985.

The paragraph, sentence by sentence

“One of the most baneful and persistent evils.” Two adjectives carry two different claims. Baneful is a moral judgement: racism is poisonous, a bane, and not merely an error of opinion. Persistent is a historical observation: it survives the laws, campaigns and declarations raised against it. The second word explains why the statement includes the issue at all. The declarations it has just praised had already condemned racial discrimination on paper; the evil had outlasted them.

“A major barrier to peace.” The statement does not treat racism as a domestic social problem that sits beside the question of war. It places it on the road to peace, as an obstacle.

“Too outrageous a violation of the dignity of human beings to be countenanced under any pretext.” This sentence rules out excuses in advance. Racism has historically justified itself with pretexts — economic, scientific, religious, cultural, the claimed need for order. The statement does not weigh them. It says none of them suffices.

“Retards … corrupts … blights.” The third sentence names three injuries, each with a different object:

  • the victims, whose “boundless potentialities” are held back from unfolding;
  • the perpetrators, who are corrupted by what they practise;
  • human progress as a whole, which is blighted.

The structure is significant. Racism is not presented as a transfer of advantage from one group to another, with losers on one side and winners on the other. Everyone involved is damaged. The victims lose the most visibly; the perpetrators lose morally; and humanity loses what both would have contributed.

“Recognition of the oneness of mankind, implemented by appropriate legal measures, must be universally upheld.” The remedy has three parts. Recognition is a change in understanding: the acceptance of a truth about what human beings are. Implementation by appropriate legal measures is a change in law. Universally rules out exceptions and matches the earlier “under any pretext”: no place and no group is exempt. The statement does not set the inward change against the outward one. It requires both.

Where the paragraph sits in the statement

The paralysis of will

Part II opens by arguing that banning particular weapons or settling particular disputes will not remove “the root causes of war.” The world’s leaders do not lack information. “There is, however, a paralysis of will.” That paralysis, the statement says, is “rooted, as we have stated, in a deep-seated conviction of the inevitable quarrelsomeness of mankind.”

The phrase “as we have stated” points back to the introduction, where the House of Justice described “a paralyzing contradiction”: people everywhere long for peace, yet give “uncritical assent” to “the proposition that human beings are incorrigibly selfish and aggressive.” The statement’s reply is that such conduct, “far from expressing man’s true self, represents a distortion of the human spirit.”

Racism belongs inside this argument. It is a belief about human beings — that some kinds of people are by nature lesser, dangerous or unfit — and like the belief in inevitable quarrelsomeness, it makes conflict look natural. A world that accepts racial hierarchy as a fact of nature has already accepted that some groups will always contend with others. The racism paragraph therefore addresses one of the specific assumptions that keep the will to peace paralysed.

The conventions, and the “additional comment”

Immediately before the seven issues, the statement lists the measures it counts as “hopeful” signs: “The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, and the similar measures concerned with eliminating all forms of discrimination based on race, sex or religious belief.” All such measures, “if courageously enforced and expanded, will advance the day when the specter of war will have lost its power to dominate international relations.”

So the racism paragraph comments on existing international law; it does not dismiss it. The condition “if courageously enforced and expanded” returns in the paragraph’s final sentence as “appropriate legal measures.”

The two points that follow the seven

After the seventh issue (an auxiliary language), the statement adds: “Two points bear emphasizing in all these issues.” The first is that “the abolition of war is not simply a matter of signing treaties and protocols; it is a complex task requiring a new level of commitment to resolving issues not customarily associated with the pursuit of peace.” Racism is such an issue: peace conferences seldom treat it as their business. The second point is that “the primary challenge in dealing with issues of peace is to raise the context to the level of principle, as distinct from pure pragmatism. For, in essence, peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude.”

Read with these two points, the racism paragraph has a definite logic. Its legal half is practical; its first half — “recognition of the oneness of mankind” — is the principle to which the context must be raised.

Parts I, III and IV

The theme is present across the whole statement.

  • Part I criticises the ideologies that took religion’s place in the twentieth century, many of which tended “to subordinate the rest of mankind to one nation, race or class.” It asks: “Where are the breakthroughs into new realms of cultural achievement produced by the aggrandizement of this race, of that nation or of a particular class?” Racial supremacy appears here as one of the failed substitute faiths of the century.
  • Part III names the foundation of world order: “an unshakable consciousness of the oneness of mankind, a spiritual truth which all the human sciences confirm.” It continues: “Recognition of this truth requires abandonment of prejudice—prejudice of every kind—race, class, color, creed, nation, sex, degree of material civilization, everything which enables people to consider themselves superior to others.” Race heads the list. The same part quotes Shoghi Effendi’s 1931 description of the future world community as one “in which the flame of racial animosity will have been finally extinguished.”
  • Part IV closes with the conviction that the Creator “out of His infinite love has created all humanity from the same stock,” and with the belief that the “potentialities inherent in the station of man, the full measure of his destiny on earth, the innate excellence of his reality, must all be manifested in this promised Day of God.” The racism paragraph’s charge was that racism “retards the unfoldment of the boundless potentialities of its victims.” Part IV’s hope is that those potentialities will be manifested. Racism works directly against the outcome that the statement’s final pages describe.

The House of Justice had prepared for the statement’s release with a letter of 23 January 1985 about the International Year of Peace that the United Nations had proclaimed for 1986. It proposed that Bahá’í communities sponsor activities on “various topics relevant to peace, such as: the role of women, the elimination of racism, the eradication of prejudice,” and others. The elimination of racism was named as a peace topic from the start of that campaign.

The Writings of Bahá’u’lláh: one origin, no grounds for superiority

The statement’s remedy, “recognition of the oneness of mankind,” rests on the Writings. Bahá’u’lláh addresses humanity in The Hidden Words:

“O CHILDREN OF MEN! Know ye not why We created you all from the same dust? That no one should exalt himself over the other. Ponder at all times in your hearts how ye were created.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Arabic no. 68

The verse gives a reason for the common origin, and the reason is moral: “that no one should exalt himself over the other.” Racism is a form of exactly that exaltation. In this verse it is not only unkind; it contradicts the purpose for which human beings were made from one substance.

A short passage, quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The Advent of Divine Justice and included in the compilation Bahá’í Sacred Writings, gives the instruction directly:

“O YE discerning ones! Verily, the words which have descended from the heaven of the Will of God are the source of unity and harmony for the world. Close your eyes to racial differences, and welcome all with the light of oneness.” — Bahá’u’lláh, quoted in The Advent of Divine Justice; Bahá’í Sacred Writings

The two clauses depend on each other. Close your eyes withdraws attention from race as a basis for judgement; welcome all directs attention toward every person. The command is to refuse racial distinction any weight in how one receives a person. It is not a command to ignore what racism has done.

Two further passages from Gleanings connect oneness to peace. One reads: “Ye are the fruits of one tree, and the leaves of one branch. Deal ye one with another with the utmost love and harmony, with friendliness and fellowship… So powerful is the light of unity that it can illuminate the whole earth” (Gleanings, CXXXII). The other is the sentence the House of Justice quoted in Part IV of the Promise: “The well-being of mankind, its peace and security, are unattainable unless and until its unity is firmly established” (Gleanings, CXXXI). If peace depends on unity, and racism is a refusal of unity, then racism is a barrier to peace by definition.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá: prejudice as a cause of war

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá made the link between prejudice and war explicit, many times, and in terms that the 1985 statement follows closely.

In His first Tablet to The Hague, sent on 17 December 1919 to the Central Organisation for a Durable Peace in the year after the First World War ended, He listed among the teachings of Bahá’u’lláh “that religious, racial, political, economic and patriotic prejudices destroy the edifice of humanity. As long as these prejudices prevail, the world of humanity will not have rest.” He then made a historical claim: “In every period war has been waged in one country or another and that war was due to either religious prejudice, racial prejudice, political prejudice or patriotic prejudice” (Tablets to The Hague).

In another Tablet, written while a war was being fought, He described cities levelled and wrote: “Mothers have wept away their hearts over dead children.” He then named the source: “And the breeding ground of all these tragedies is prejudice: prejudice of race and nation, of religion, of political opinion; and the root cause of prejudice is blind imitation of the past” (Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, no. 202). This gives the 1985 word “persistent” a cause. Racism persists because it is inherited and imitated, not reasoned.

In Paris in November 1911, speaking on the abolition of prejudices, He called racial prejudice false at its root:

“Concerning the prejudice of race: it is an illusion, a superstition pure and simple! For God created us all of one race. There were no differences in the beginning, for we are all descendants of Adam… In the sight of God there is no difference between the various races. Why should man invent such a prejudice? How can we uphold war caused by an illusion?” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Paris Talks, “The Fifth Principle—The Abolition of Prejudices”

The final question states the peace argument in a single sentence. Wars fought over race are wars fought over something that does not exist.

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá in America, 1912: words and acts

‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s journey through North America in 1912 gave the Bahá’í teaching on race its most concrete expressions. Century of Light, prepared under the supervision of the Universal House of Justice, notes that His audiences on that continent included many devoted to “peace, women’s rights, racial equality, social reform and moral development.”

Three talks in two cities

On 23 April 1912 He spoke at Howard University in Washington, D.C. In the current translation in The Promulgation of Universal Peace, He began:

“Today I am most happy, for I see here a gathering of the servants of God. I see white and black sitting together. There are no whites and blacks before God. All colors are one, and that is the color of servitude to God. Scent and color are not important. The heart is important.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Howard University, 23 April 1912, The Promulgation of Universal Peace

He then turned to the birds: “Doves are of many colors; nevertheless, they live in utmost harmony. They never look at color; instead, they look at the species. How often white doves fly with black ones.” He drew the conclusion: “Animals, despite the fact that they lack reason and understanding, do not make colors the cause of conflict. Why should man, who has reason, create conflict?” And He closed with a hope: “I pray that you attain to such a degree of good character and behavior that the names of black and white shall vanish. All shall be called human, just as the name for a flight of doves is dove.”

The same evening, at the Bethel Literary Society in the Metropolitan African Methodist Episcopal Church, He described what the gifts of intellect are for: “By directing our efforts toward the unification of the human race… appreciate these virtues by accomplishing the unity of whites and blacks… Then will mankind be as one nation, one race and kind—as waves of one ocean.”

On 30 April 1912 in Chicago He spoke twice on the subject. At Hull House He set out an argument from points of agreement. In the United States, He observed, the two races share citizenship, language, civilisation and religion, “whereas the one point of distinction is that of color. Shall this, the least of all distinctions, be allowed to separate you as races and individuals?” He added that argument alone would not be enough: “But there is need of a superior power to overcome human prejudices… That irresistible power is the love of God.” At the Fourth Annual Conference of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, He argued from the human being as the image of God: “color is not the standard and estimate of judgment and that it is of no importance, for color is accidental in nature,” and the station of the image of God “is not to be sacrificed by the mere accident of color.”

“An assurance of the world’s peace”

In The Advent of Divine Justice Shoghi Effendi gathered many of ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s sayings on race. One of them connects racial unity directly to world peace:

“Strive earnestly … and put forth your greatest endeavor toward the accomplishment of this fellowship and the cementing of this bond of brotherhood between you. Such an attainment is not possible without will and effort on the part of each; from one, expressions of gratitude and appreciation; from the other, kindliness and recognition of equality. Each one should endeavor to develop and assist the other toward mutual advancement.… Love and unity will be fostered between you, thereby bringing about the oneness of mankind. For the accomplishment of unity between the colored and white will be an assurance of the world’s peace.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The Advent of Divine Justice, 25 December 1938

This passage comes from an earlier English rendering of the Howard University talk; the version now published in The Promulgation of Universal Peace is a fresh translation and words the talk differently. The sentence about the world’s peace is cited here as the Guardian quoted it.

Shoghi Effendi set beside it two warnings from ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá. “This question of the union of the white and the black is very important,” ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá warned, “for if it is not realized, erelong great difficulties will arise, and harmful results will follow.” And again: “If this matter remaineth without change,” runs the second warning, “enmity will be increased day by day, and the final result will be hardship and may end in bloodshed.” The 1985 statement’s claim that racism is a barrier to peace is, in ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s words to America, a prediction of violence if the barrier remains.

He also gave the positive form. “In the world of being,” He said, “the meeting is blessed when the white and colored races meet together with infinite spiritual love and heavenly harmony,” and of such gatherings, “the angels of the Kingdom praise them, and the Beauty of Bahá’u’lláh addresseth them, ‘Blessed are ye! Blessed are ye!’”

Acts

Century of Light observes that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá taught by action as well as speech, and gives two examples: “His unprovocative but clear demonstration of the meaning of Bahá’u’lláh’s teachings on racial oneness by welcoming black as well as white guests at His own dinner table and the tables of His prominent hostesses,” and “His gentle but dramatic act in encouraging the marriage of Louis Gregory and Louise Mathew—the one black, the other white. The initiative set a standard for the American Bahá’í community as to the real meaning of racial integration, however timid and slow its members were in responding to the core implications of the challenge” (Century of Light).

One dinner table is remembered in detail. Historical accounts — the In Memoriam article for Louis Gregory in The Bahá’í World, vol. 12, and later biographies — describe a luncheon in ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s honour given in Washington on 23 April 1912, the day of the Howard talk, by Ali‑Kuli Khan, chargé d’affaires of the Persian Legation, and his wife Florence. About fifteen guests from official and social Washington had been invited. Louis Gregory, an African American Bahá’í of Washington who had trained as a lawyer, had come for a meeting with ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá beforehand; when the luncheon was announced he did not follow the others into the dining room and was waiting for a chance to leave. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá stood, asked where Mr. Gregory was, had him brought in, rearranged the places, and seated him at His right, in the seat of honour; then, in the account’s words, “in the most natural way as if nothing unusual had happened, proceeded to give a talk on the oneness of mankind.” The details come from these historical accounts, not from the authoritative texts.

Louis Gregory spent the rest of his life on the work of race unity. When he died in 1951, Shoghi Effendi cabled: “Profoundly deplore grievous loss of dearly beloved, noble-minded, golden-hearted Louis Gregory, pride and example to the Negro adherents of the Faith… Deserves rank of first Hand of the Cause of his race. Rising Bahá’í generation in African continent will glory in his memory and emulate his example” (Citadel of Faith, 6 August 1951).

An earlier believer is remembered in a Tablet. God Passes By records that Robert Turner, butler to Phoebe Hearst, was “the first member of his race to embrace the Cause of Bahá’u’lláh in the West,” and that he was among the first Western pilgrims to visit ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá (God Passes By). When news of his death reached ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, He wrote:

“Gracious God! What a shining candle was lighted within that black-coloured lamp… O All-Knowing God! While that innocent soul was black in colour, he was, like unto the black pupil of the eye, a source of radiant light.” — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, Tablet concerning Robert Turner, in Additional Tablets, Extracts and Talks

The image of the pupil has an older source. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá reported, in a saying quoted in The Advent of Divine Justice, that “Bahá’u’lláh … once compared the colored people to the black pupil of the eye surrounded by the white. In this black pupil is seen the reflection of that which is before it, and through it the light of the spirit shineth forth.” The pupil is the dark part of the eye, and it is the part through which light enters.

Shoghi Effendi: the “most vital and challenging issue”

The Advent of Divine Justice (1938)

Shoghi Effendi’s fullest treatment of race is addressed to the Bahá’ís of North America in The Advent of Divine Justice, dated 25 December 1938. He describes their nation as “a prey to one of the most virulent and long-standing forms of racial prejudice,” and names three spiritual prerequisites for their work, the third being “a fraternity freed from that cancerous growth of racial prejudice, which is eating into the vitals of an already debilitated society.” He then writes:

“As to racial prejudice, the corrosion of which, for well-nigh a century, has bitten into the fiber, and attacked the whole social structure of American society, it should be regarded as constituting the most vital and challenging issue confronting the Bahá’í community at the present stage of its evolution.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice, 25 December 1938

He does not claim the Bahá’ís had solved it. The problem is one “which the American believers are still far from having satisfactorily resolved,” and “A long and thorny road, beset with pitfalls, still remains untraveled.”

The remedy he prescribes is reciprocal. He asks each group for a different effort, and both efforts are demanding:

“Let the white make a supreme effort in their resolve to contribute their share to the solution of this problem, to abandon once for all their usually inherent and at times subconscious sense of superiority, to correct their tendency towards revealing a patronizing attitude towards the members of the other race, to persuade them through their intimate, spontaneous and informal association with them of the genuineness of their friendship and the sincerity of their intentions, and to master their impatience of any lack of responsiveness on the part of a people who have received, for so long a period, such grievous and slow-healing wounds.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Advent of Divine Justice

He then asks the Black believers to show “the warmth of their response, their readiness to forget the past, and their ability to wipe out every trace of suspicion that may still linger in their hearts and minds,” and closes off every way of avoiding the task: “Let neither think that the solution of so vast a problem is a matter that exclusively concerns the other. Let neither think that such a problem can either easily or immediately be resolved… Let neither think that anything short of genuine love, extreme patience, true humility, consummate tact, sound initiative, mature wisdom, and deliberate, persistent, and prayerful effort, can succeed in blotting out the stain which this patent evil has left on the fair name of their common country.”

The passage names a “subconscious sense of superiority” and “grievous and slow-healing wounds.” Shoghi Effendi treated racism as something that persists below conscious intention and leaves injuries that take a long time to heal. The unity-in-diversity article treats the practical instructions in the same letter — freedom from prejudice as a “watchword,” cultivated “through the various and everyday opportunities, no matter how insignificant,” and the rule giving priority to minorities.

Racialism among the “triple gods” (1941)

In The Promised Day Is Come (1941), written while the Second World War was being fought, Shoghi Effendi placed racism among the false religions of the age:

“The chief idols in the desecrated temple of mankind are none other than the triple gods of Nationalism, Racialism and Communism, at whose altars governments and peoples, whether democratic or totalitarian, at peace or at war, of the East or of the West, Christian or Islamic, are, in various forms and in different degrees, now worshiping. Their high priests are the politicians and the worldly-wise, the so-called sages of the age; their sacrifice, the flesh and blood of the slaughtered multitudes.” — Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come

This passage stands behind Part I of the 1985 statement, which describes the twentieth century’s ideologies as “substitute faiths” and asks what “the aggrandizement of this race” had produced. Century of Light draws the same line from the Guardian’s “triple gods” to the racial theories of Adolf Hitler, and to the liberation of the Nazi death camps, which “exposed for all to see the horrific consequences of racism.” In Shoghi Effendi’s account, racialism is not only a private prejudice but one of the forces that produced the world wars.

The future he described

Shoghi Effendi also described the condition that would replace racism. In The Goal of a New World Order (28 November 1931), quoted in Part III of the Promise, the future world community is one “in which the flame of racial animosity will have been finally extinguished.” In The Unfoldment of World Civilization (11 March 1936) he wrote that in the unified world “racial animosity and prejudice will be replaced by racial amity, understanding and coöperation” (The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh). The word amity had a history. In the early 1920s Agnes Parsons and Louis Gregory organised the “Race Amity” initiatives that, Century of Light says, were “inspired by ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá”; historical accounts date the first Race Amity Convention to Washington, D.C., in May 1921.

The Universal House of Justice after 1985

The House of Justice has returned to racism repeatedly since 1985. Its later guidance keeps the 1985 diagnosis and adds detail about method.

Apartheid, 1986: the force of example

In 1986 the House of Justice approved a statement, Apartheid: A Bahá’í View, prepared by the Office of Public Information and sent to all National Spiritual Assemblies with a letter of 12 October 1986 from the Department of the Secretariat. It records what South African Bahá’ís did under racial law:

“When the apartheid laws made it illegal for certain local groups, including elected Bahá’í Spiritual Assemblies, to contain members from different racial backgrounds, it was the white Bahá’ís who surrendered the privilege of elected membership so that the principle of giving priority to disadvantaged segments of society could continue to prevail.” — Apartheid: A Bahá’í View, 1986, in Messages of the Universal House of Justice 1986–2001

The statement calls apartheid a system “violating as it does the very honor of humanity,” and draws a lesson about method: “solutions to situations such as that which prevails in South Africa must draw on the force of example.” It also explains why Bahá’ís did not join the partisan political struggle against apartheid: “political action, in the absence of conditions for genuine consultation, in which partisan interests are subordinated to the interests of the whole,” will not by itself bring an enduring resolution.

The Bahá’í International Community, 1989: the symptom and the disease

In February 1989 the Bahá’í International Community addressed the United Nations Commission on Human Rights in Geneva on the same theme. Its statement is an institutional elaboration of the 1985 paragraph and does not carry the authority of the House of Justice, but it shows how the paragraph was carried into international discourse:

“Just as a fever is a symptom of disease in the body, racism is a symptom of disease in society. Suppressing the symptom does not cure the disease, but curing the disease eliminates the symptom.” — Bahá’í International Community, Eliminating Racism, Geneva, 8 February 1989

The statement names the disease as “failure to recognize the principle of the oneness of humanity,” and restates the 1985 triad: racism “cripples its victims, corrupts its perpetrators, and blights human progress.”

2002: “something of the character of a spiritual disease”

In its April 2002 letter To the World’s Religious Leaders, the House of Justice assessed how far the world had moved:

“Racism is now tainted by its association with the horrors of the twentieth century to the degree that it has taken on something of the character of a spiritual disease. While surviving as a social attitude in many parts of the world—and as a blight on the lives of a significant segment of humankind—racial prejudice has become so universally condemned in principle that no body of people can any longer safely allow themselves to be identified with it.” — The Universal House of Justice, April 2002, in Messages of the Universal House of Justice 2001–2022

The letter does not claim victory. “Vast numbers of people continue to endure the effects of ingrained prejudices of ethnicity, gender, nation, caste and class.” Its claim is that “a threshold has been crossed from which there is no credible possibility of return,” because the principles “are becoming progressively incarnated in institutions capable of imposing them on public behaviour.” This is the 1985 remedy partly carried out: principle recognised in public, and being written into law and institutions.

2008 and 2011: seven prejudices, and racism in changed forms

The Riḍván 2008 message set the standard for Bahá’í conduct: “So free must be your thoughts and actions of any trace of prejudice—racial, religious, economic, national, tribal, class, or cultural—that even the stranger sees in you loving friends.”

A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice to an individual on 10 April 2011 answered a question many American Bahá’ís had asked: does the Guardian’s 1938 description of racial prejudice as “the most vital and challenging issue” still apply? The letter called that way of asking “not productive,” because it creates “an implicit and false dichotomy” — either the statement no longer matters, or it matters so much it must be addressed apart from everything else. It then described how the problem had changed:

“The American nation is much more diverse than in 1938, and the friends cannot be concerned only with relations between black and white, essential as they are. The expressions of racial prejudice have transmuted into forms that are multifaceted, less blatant and more intricate, and thus more intractable.” — Letter written on behalf of the Universal House of Justice, 10 April 2011

The letter also observed that earlier efforts had followed “a cyclical pattern, with fits and starts,” and it located the work within the systematic pattern of community building, social action and participation in the discourses of society. In that work “issues of prejudice of race, class, and color will inevitably arise as the friends reach out to diverse populations,” and the process is “not a process that some carry out on behalf of others who are passive recipients.” The aim, the letter says, remains the one ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá set: “to eliminate racial prejudice from the face of the earth.”

2019: the Promise revisited

On 18 January 2019, a hundred years after the Paris Peace Conference opened, the House of Justice wrote to the Bahá’ís of the world about “Humanity’s Progress Towards World Peace.” It described a retreat in the new century: the will to international collective action “has been cowed, assailed by resurgent forces of racism, nationalism, and factionalism.” It described the fragmentation of identity: “Seemingly countless permutations of ‘us’ and ‘them’ define group identities ever more narrowly and in contrast to one another.”

The letter then looked back to 1985. Since The Promise of World Peace, Bahá’ís had been “learning how to nurture communities that embody those prerequisites of peace we identified in 1985.” The first example it gave concerned racism: “They cultivate environments in which children can be raised untainted by any form of racial, national, or religious prejudice.”

2020: racism and true morality

On 22 July 2020, as protests against racial injustice spread across the United States, the House of Justice wrote to the Bahá’ís of that country. It noted that “any significant progress toward racial equality has invariably been met by countervailing processes, overt or covert, that served to undermine the advances achieved and to reconstitute the forces of oppression by other means,” and recalled the Guardian’s “long and thorny road.” Its central paragraph restates the 1985 diagnosis thirty-five years later:

“Racism is a profound deviation from the standard of true morality. It deprives a portion of humanity of the opportunity to cultivate and express the full range of their capability and to live a meaningful and flourishing life, while blighting the progress of the rest of humankind. It cannot be rooted out by contest and conflict. It must be supplanted by the establishment of just relationships among individuals, communities, and institutions of society that will uplift all and will not designate anyone as ‘other’. The change required is not merely social and economic, but above all moral and spiritual.” — The Universal House of Justice, 22 July 2020, to the Bahá’ís of the United States

Set beside the 1985 text, the correspondence is close. “Retards the unfoldment of the boundless potentialities of its victims” becomes “deprives a portion of humanity of the opportunity to cultivate and express the full range of their capability.” “Blights human progress” becomes “blighting the progress of the rest of humankind.” The 2020 letter adds a statement about method that the 1985 paragraph did not contain: racism “cannot be rooted out by contest and conflict.” The remedy is not the defeat of one group by another but “just relationships” that “will not designate anyone as ‘other’.”

The letter ends with love as the transforming force: “love originating from the relationship with the divine, love ablaze among members of a community, love extended without restriction to every human being.” It gives the Bahá’ís a role: “You are channels for this divine love; let it flow through you to all who cross your path.” And it describes ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s example in a list of qualities, among them “indiscriminating in fellowship, unfailing in sympathy for the downtrodden,” and “unwavering in His keen sense of justice.”

Law and the heart: the two halves of the remedy

The 1985 remedy joins “recognition of the oneness of mankind” to “appropriate legal measures.” The guidance that follows keeps both halves and explains how they relate.

Law is necessary. The Promise counts the anti‑discrimination conventions among the hopeful signs of its time, on condition that they are “courageously enforced and expanded.” The 2002 letter treats the embedding of principle “in institutions capable of imposing them on public behaviour” as the reason the change cannot be reversed. In The Advent of Divine Justice, Shoghi Effendi laid down a rule for the Bahá’í community itself: when an election ties or qualifications are equal, priority goes to the minority.

Law is not sufficient. ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá told the audience at Hull House that “there is need of a superior power to overcome human prejudices,” and named it as the love of God. The 1986 apartheid statement said political action alone “will not itself bring an enduring resolution.” The Bahá’í International Community’s Brussels Office, in a February 2023 statement on European anti‑racism action plans, wrote that “deep-seated prejudices cannot be unrooted from the hearts and minds of individuals—much less from the structures that underpin society and the institutions that serve it—through anti-racism legislation alone.”

The Promise’s own “two points” explain why both halves are needed. Treaties and laws regulate conduct; but “peace stems from an inner state supported by a spiritual or moral attitude.” A law against discrimination can forbid an act. It cannot produce the “recognition” the paragraph calls for, and it cannot produce the friendship across racial lines that ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá and Shoghi Effendi asked for. The 2020 letter adds a warning about the opposite error: the Bahá’í contribution cannot be made “merely by adopting the perspectives, practices, concepts, criticisms, and language of contemporary society.”

The present field (external sources — description only, current as of October 2026)

The following describes the state of international law and policy. It is not authority for any Bahá’í principle, and the figures will change.

  • The International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, adopted by the UN General Assembly on 21 December 1965 and in force since 4 January 1969, is the main instrument among the “measures concerned with eliminating all forms of discrimination based on race” that the Promise mentions. The UN Treaty Collection lists 182 parties and 88 signatories as of 7 October 2026.
  • On 17 December 2024 the General Assembly proclaimed a Second International Decade for People of African Descent, running from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2034, with the theme “People of African Descent: Recognition, Justice, and Development” (resolution A/RES/79/193). It continues the first decade (2015–2024).
  • The Universal House of Justice’s 2019 description of “resurgent forces of racism, nationalism, and factionalism” is the most recent authoritative assessment in this corpus of the direction of world affairs on this question.

The convention has existed for six decades and is nearly universal in ratification, and a second UN decade has followed the first. The 1985 word “persistent” still describes the situation.

Open questions and limits

  • Historical vocabulary. The Advent of Divine Justice (1938) and the 1912 talks use the racial vocabulary of their time (“Negro,” “colored”). The words are quoted here as written. They are not current usage, and using them in new writing would misrepresent the present guidance, which speaks of “racial prejudice,” “race unity” and “people of African descent.”
  • The Howard University talk. The talk exists in two English renderings: the older one quoted by Shoghi Effendi in The Advent of Divine Justice and the fresh translation by Amin Banani now in The Promulgation of Universal Peace. The newer version contains a passage on gratitude for emancipation, framed by the history of the time, which should not be quoted alone. Shoghi Effendi’s own 1938 formulation sets out obligations on both sides and names “grievous and slow-healing wounds.”
  • Light and dark imagery. Some passages from 1912 use the moral imagery of their period — a heart “pure and white” within, or “dark and sinful.” The point of those passages is that skin colour is no measure of the heart. The imagery itself does not translate well into present-day writing.
  • “Differences of race be annulled.” Bahá’u’lláh’s words to E. G. Browne in 1890, quoted in The Advent of Divine Justice, include the wish “that diversity of religion should cease, and differences of race be annulled.” The same body of Writings treats racial diversity as the colours of one garden. The unity-in-diversity article notes this tension; nothing quoted on this page settles exactly how the two relate.
  • Scope of the Guardian’s diagnosis. The Advent of Divine Justice addresses one national community at one moment. Its principles are general; its analysis of American history is specific. The 10 April 2011 letter itself says the landscape has changed.
  • Historical details from secondary sources. The account of the 23 April 1912 luncheon, the dates of Robert Turner’s life and pilgrimage, the date of the Gregory–Mathew marriage (September 1912), and the date of the first Race Amity Convention (May 1921) come from historical accounts (The Bahá’í World, Bahaipedia and similar), not from the authoritative texts. Century of Light confirms the marriage and the Race Amity initiatives without giving dates.
  • The Tablet “Close your eyes to racial differences.” The corpus gives the passage, attributed to Bahá’u’lláh, in The Advent of Divine Justice and in Bahá’í Sacred Writings, but this research did not identify the Tablet it comes from.

For the song

The heart of it, in one sentence. The House of Justice names racism as one of the most baneful and persistent evils and a major barrier to peace, because it violates human dignity beyond any excuse, holds back its victims, corrupts those who practise it and blights the progress of everyone, and it can be overcome only when the oneness of humanity is recognised in every heart and upheld in every law.

Concrete images, stories, people, places and moments

  • The same dust — all human beings made from one substance, so that none may exalt himself over another.
  • The pupil of the eye — the dark centre of the eye, through which light enters and in which the world is reflected; Bahá’u’lláh’s comparison as ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá reported it, and ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s words for Robert Turner.
  • A shining candle in a black-coloured lamp — ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá on Robert Turner, the Hearst family’s butler, who listened to the new teachings while serving tea and became the first person of African descent in the West to accept the Faith.
  • White doves flying with black ones — the Howard University talk, Washington, 23 April 1912, before an audience of black and white listeners together.
  • The seat at His right hand — the same day, at a diplomatic luncheon in Washington, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá stood, asked for Louis Gregory, rearranged the table and seated him in the place of honour, then spoke on the oneness of mankind as though nothing unusual had happened.
  • A marriage — Louis Gregory and Louise Mathew, one black and one white, encouraged by ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá, married in 1912 when such marriages were illegal or unrecognised in many American states.
  • The waves of one ocean; the flowers of one garden — the Bethel Literary Society talk, the same evening.
  • The least of all distinctions — Hull House, Chicago: two peoples sharing citizenship, language, civilisation and faith, divided by one point only.
  • The table and the ballot in South Africa — under apartheid law, white Bahá’ís gave up their seats on Spiritual Assemblies so that the principle of priority for the disadvantaged could stand.
  • The fever and the disease — racism as the symptom, the failure to recognise oneness as the illness (Bahá’í International Community, Geneva, 1989).
  • The triple gods — Nationalism, Racialism and Communism, with their high priests and their sacrifice of “the flesh and blood of the slaughtered multitudes” (Shoghi Effendi, 1941).
  • The long and thorny road — the Guardian’s 1938 image, taken up again by the House of Justice in 2011 and in 2020.
  • A flame extinguished — the future world in which “the flame of racial animosity” has gone out.
  • Children raised untainted — the 2019 picture of communities where children grow up without racial, national or religious prejudice.
  • 1985 and 2020 — two statements thirty-five years apart naming the same three injuries: to the victims, to the perpetrators, to human progress.

Misreadings a song must avoid

  • “Close your eyes” is not denial. The instruction is to give race no weight in how one receives a person. It is not a call to overlook injustice. Shoghi Effendi named “grievous and slow-healing wounds,” and the House of Justice in 2020 named the “countervailing processes” that have undone past progress. A song that says “I don’t see colour, so there is no problem” says something the texts do not.
  • Not an American problem only, and not only black and white. The Promise addresses the peoples of the world; the apartheid statement concerns South Africa; the 2011 letter says the friends “cannot be concerned only with relations between black and white, essential as they are”; and Riḍván 2008 lists seven kinds of prejudice.
  • Not an us-against-them song. The Promise says racism “corrupts its perpetrators” — they are harmed as well as harmful — and the 2020 letter says racism “cannot be rooted out by contest and conflict.” A song that turns one group into the enemy works against the remedy the texts prescribe. This does not soften the judgement: the practice is “too outrageous a violation … to be countenanced under any pretext.”
  • Not a claim that Bahá’ís have solved it. Shoghi Effendi said the American believers were “still far from having satisfactorily resolved” it; the 2011 letter describes a “cyclical pattern, with fits and starts.” The Promise offers the Bahá’í community “as a model for study,” not as a finished example.
  • Not sentiment alone, and not law alone. The remedy is recognition of oneness and legal measures, upheld universally. A song about feelings only, or about legislation only, drops half of it.
  • Unity is not sameness. The diversity of races is the colours of one garden and the waves of one ocean. The hope that “the names of black and white shall vanish” concerns names used to divide, not the people or their heritage.
  • Do not borrow the period vocabulary or the light/dark moral imagery. “Colored,” “Negro,” and the image of a heart “white” within were the language of 1912 and 1938; they should not appear in a new lyric as if they were current.
  • Attribute accurately. The pupil-of-the-eye comparison is Bahá’u’lláh’s as reported by ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá; the “assurance of the world’s peace” sentence is ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá’s as quoted by Shoghi Effendi; the fever-and-symptom image is the Bahá’í International Community’s, not the House of Justice’s.
