The Tablet of the Bell (Lawḥ-i-Náqús)

Last updated 17 October 2026

The Tablet of the Bell is a Tablet of Bahá’u’lláh, in Arabic, revealed in Constantinople in 1863 on the anniversary of the Declaration of the Báb. After every call or petition it repeats one refrain, which the sources transliterate as Subḥánika-yá-Hú (“Praised be Thou, O He!”). It was written to be chanted by one voice with the others joining the refrain. This page gathers what the sources say about the Tablet, quotes the authorised English text, and lists what a song based on it can and cannot say. It belongs to the series on poetry: see Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

The poem

Name. The Tablet is called Lawḥ-i-Náqús (Tablet of the Bell) from the word náqús in its first call, and is also called after its refrain (Taherzadeh, The Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh, vol. 2, p. 19). Stephen Lambden gives the refrain title as Lawḥ-i Subḥánika yá-Hú (“Tablet of Praised be Thou, O He!”). The transliteration of the refrain differs between sources (Subḥánika, Subhánáka); no Arabic text of the Tablet was available for this page, so the vowelling of the manuscripts is not known here. The authorised translation in Days of Remembrance gives the title as “Lawḥ-i-Náqús (Tablet of the Bell)” and is item 26 in that volume, in the section for the Declaration of the Báb.

When and where. Adib Taherzadeh writes that “a beautiful tablet in Arabic and in Bahá’u’lláh’s own hand was revealed in Constantinople on the eve of the 5th of Jamadiyu’l-Avval 1280 A.H. (19 October 1863), the anniversary of the Declaration of the Báb” (Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh 2, p. 18). He adds that the Declaration itself was on the evening of 5 Jamádí I 1260 (22 May 1844) and that in the East the anniversary is observed by the lunar calendar (p. 19, note 1). Lambden gives the same Gregorian date and says Bahá’u’lláh wrote the Tablet in His own hand. Denis MacEoin’s abstract says only that it was written in Istanbul in the autumn of 1863.

For whom. Taherzadeh says it was revealed after a request made through ʻAbdu’l-Bahá by one of Bahá’u’lláh’s companions, Áqá Muḥammad-ʻAlí-i-Tambáku-Furúsh-i-Iṣfahání. Lambden says the same and cites a letter of Shoghi Effendi to Mírzá Badíʻu’lláh Ághá-Badíʻí (cited by Lambden as Ganj, 71) as a source for it; that letter was not seen for this page.

Form. The authorised translation has an opening paragraph (DOR 26.1), an invocation (26.2), twenty-one calls (26.3–26.23), each ending with the refrain, and seven paragraphs of petition (26.24–26.30), each also ending with the refrain. The refrain occurs twenty-eight times in all. The last paragraph quotes the Qur’án (28:5). The preface to Days of Remembrance says the volume’s translations “endeavour to afford a glimpse of the poetic tone” of the Tablets, and that some are “expressed in a celebratory and uplifting tone, occasionally with repeated refrains” (preface). The preface does not name this Tablet or say that it is in verse; the verse-like structure is visible from the text.

How it was used. Taherzadeh says the Tablet is “composed in a style that lends itself to collective chanting” and “creates an atmosphere of ecstasy and joy when chanted by the believers”. In a note he adds that tablets in the original language are chanted by an individual and that “occasionally, when there is a refrain in a Tablet, it has been customary for others to join in the refrain if it is suitable to do so”, which he distinguishes from congregational prayer (p. 19, note 1). R. Jackson Armstrong-Ingram, writing in the Wilmette Institute notes (1999), says the sections were cantillated by one person and everyone joined the refrain, that a melody for the refrain was notated around 1906 from the singing of Ameen Fareed and printed in his Music, Devotions, and Mashriqu’l-Adhkár (p. 345), and that two Bahá’ís who grew up in Tehran in the 1930s and 1940s told him it was the refrain melody of their childhood. He also says there is evidence that a Bahá’í performance of the Tablet was mistaken for Sufi devotions.

Taherzadeh gives one account of its use in prison. Ḥájí Mírzá Ḥaydar-ʻAlí, imprisoned in Egypt, “taught his companions to chant the Lawh-i-Naqus”, and the guards who heard the prisoners chanting “aloud and in unison” came to see that they were “men of God and not criminals”, took off their chains and gave them a light (vol. 2, p. 441).

The word náqús. Lambden says náqús is a non-Qur’ánic Arabic loan-word from Aramaic-Syriac, meaning a pierced wooden board struck with a stick to call Eastern Christians to worship, and suggests Bahá’u’lláh may have heard it near the churches of Istanbul. He notes that Bahá’u’lláh calls Himself the “Bell” in other Writings, and quotes Shoghi Effendi’s translation of the second Tablet to Napoleon III (Proclamation of Bahá’u’lláh, p. 29): “The Most Mighty Bell … hath appeared in the form of Him Who is the Most Great Name”. That quotation is Lambden’s, not checked here against the book.

The text in English

Authorised translation (Days of Remembrance, 2017) — ok-with-credit

This is the translation approved by the Bahá’í World Centre. The licence note: © Bahá’í International Community; check the bahai.org terms before a commercial release. The refrain closes every numbered paragraph from 3 to 30; it is given in full once and then marked.

He is the All-Glorious. (DOR 26, opening line)

1. This is the garden of Paradise, wherein arise the anthems of God, the Help in Peril, the Self-Subsisting; wherein ascend the soul-entrancing melodies warbled by the Nightingale of Eternity upon the twigs of the Divine Lote-Tree; wherein abide the Maids of Heaven whom none hath touched save God, the All-Glorious, the Most Holy; and wherein lieth enshrined that which draweth the needy to the shores of the ocean of true wealth and guideth the people to the Word of God. And this, verily, is naught but the manifest truth. (DOR 26.1)

2. By Thy name “He”! Verily Thou art “He”, O Thou Who art “He”! (DOR 26.2; note 12)

The first call, with the refrain:

3. O Monk of the Divine Unity! Ring out the bell, for the Day of the Lord is come and the Beauty of the All-Glorious hath ascended His blessed and resplendent throne. Praise be to Thee, O Thou Who art “He”, O Thou besides Whom there is none but “He”! (DOR 26.3)

The calls that follow, without the refrain (each of them ends with it in the text):

4. O Húd, Prophet of the Divine Decree! Sound the clarion in the name of God, the All-Glorious, the Most Bountiful, for the Temple of holiness hath been established upon the seat of supernal glory. (DOR 26.4)

5. O Countenance of immortality! Pluck with the fingers of the spirit the sacred and wondrous strings, for the Beauty of the Divine Essence hath appeared, arrayed in a silken vesture of light. (DOR 26.5)

6. O Angel of light! Sound a blast upon the trumpet at the advent of this Revelation, for the letter Há’ hath been joined to the letter of ancient glory. (DOR 26.6; note 13)

7. O Nightingale of heaven! Warble upon the boughs of this celestial garden in the name of the Beloved, for the beauty of the Rose hath appeared from behind an impenetrable veil. (DOR 26.7)

8. O Songster of Paradise! Trill out upon the twigs in these wondrous days, for God hath cast His effulgent rays upon all created things. (DOR 26.8)

9. O Bird of eternity! Soar aloft in these heights, for the Bird of faithfulness hath soared in the atmosphere of divine nearness. (DOR 26.9)

10. O denizens of Paradise! Sing out and chant in the sweetest of tones, for the melody of God hath been raised within the Tabernacle of matchless sanctity. (DOR 26.10)

11. O inmates of the Kingdom! Intone the name of the Beloved, for the beauty of His Cause hath shone forth from behind the veils, adorned with a luminous spirit. (DOR 26.11)

12. O dwellers of the kingdom of names! Bedeck the furthest reaches of heaven, for the Most Great Name is come, riding upon the clouds of transcendent majesty. (DOR 26.12)

13. O inhabitants of the Dominion of divine attributes in the Realm of Glory! Make ready to enter the presence of God, for the soft breezes of holiness have wafted from the sanctuary of the Divine Essence, and this, verily, is a conspicuous bounty. (DOR 26.13)

14. O paradise of the Divine Unity! Rejoice within thyself, for the paradise of God, the Most Exalted, the All-Powerful, the All-Knowing, hath appeared. (DOR 26.14)

15. O heaven of grandeur! Render thanks unto God within thine inmost being, for the heaven of holiness hath been upraised in the firmament of a heart of stainless purity. (DOR 26.15)

16. O sun of worldly dominion! Eclipse thy face, for above the horizon of a resplendent morn there have shone the rays of the Day-Star of eternity. (DOR 26.16)

17. O earth of knowledge! Swallow up thy learning, for the Earth of true knowledge hath been outspread through Him Who is the Self of God, the All-Glorious, the All-Bountiful, the Most High. (DOR 26.17)

18. O lamp of earthly sovereignty! Put out thy light, for the Lamp of God hath been lit within the niche of eternity and hath illumined all that are in heaven and all that are on earth. (DOR 26.18)

19. O seas of the world! Still the pounding of your waves, for a most wondrous Cause hath made to surge the Crimson Sea. (DOR 26.19)

20. O Peacock of the Divine Unity! Utter thy plaintive cry amidst the thickets of the celestial world, for the melody of God hath sounded near on every side. (DOR 26.20)

21. O Cockerel of eternity! Sound thy call in the forests of the empyrean heaven, for the Summoner of God hath cried out from every lofty height. (DOR 26.21)

22. O concourse of ardent lovers! Rejoice in your souls, for the day of separation hath ended, and the Covenant hath been fulfilled, and the Beloved hath appeared arrayed in sublime and majestic beauty. (DOR 26.22)

23. O assemblage of mystic knowers! Let your hearts be filled with joy, for the time of remoteness hath passed, and the spirit of certitude hath appeared, and the countenance of the celestial Youth hath beamed forth, adorned with the ornament of holiness in the paradise of His name, the Almighty. (DOR 26.23)

The petition (paragraphs 24–30), again without the refrain:

24. Glorified art Thou, O Lord, my God! I beseech Thee by Thy Day through which Thou didst bring forth all other days, and in a single moment whereof Thou didst reckon up the appointed time of all that have been and all that shall be— (DOR 26.24)

25. And by Thy Name which Thou hast made the sovereign of the kingdom of names and the ruler of all who are in heaven and all who are on earth— (DOR 26.25)

26. To graciously enable Thy servants to dispense with all but Thee, to draw nigh unto Thee, and to become detached from aught else save Thee. Thou, verily, art the God of power, of might and mercy. (DOR 26.26)

27. Enable them then, O my God, to bear witness to Thy unity and to testify to Thy oneness in such wise that they may behold naught save Thee and shut their eyes to all else. Thou, in truth, art powerful to do what pleaseth Thee. (DOR 26.27)

28. Kindle, then, within their breasts, O my Beloved, the fire of Thy love, that it may burn away the mention of aught else, and that they may testify within themselves that from everlasting Thou hast dwelt within the inaccessible heights of Thine eternity, that Thou wert alone with none beside Thee, and that Thou wilt continue unto everlasting to be what Thou hast ever been. No God is there but Thee, the Lord of might and bounty. (DOR 26.28)

29. For were Thy servants who long to scale the heights of Thy unity to set their hearts upon aught except Thee, they could not be reckoned among such as have truly believed, nor would the sign of Thy singleness be found within them. (DOR 26.29)

30. Glorified art Thou, O Lord my God! Such being the case, I implore Thee to send down from the clouds of Thy mercy that which shall purify the hearts of Thine ardent lovers and sanctify the souls of those who adore Thee. Raise them up, then, through Thy transcendent power, and render them victorious over all who dwell on earth. This, indeed, is that which Thou hast promised Thy loved ones through Thy word of truth: “And We desire to show favour to those who were brought low in the land, and to make them spiritual leaders among men, and to make of them Our heirs.” (DOR 26.30)

The refrain, as it ends each of paragraphs 3–30:

Praise be to Thee, O Thou Who art “He”, O Thou besides Whom there is none but “He”!

The notes in Days of Remembrance say: note 12, ”In the Tafsír-i-Hú Bahá’u’lláh explains that the name “He” (or Huva, consisting of the letters Há’ and Váv) is God’s Most Great Name, for it is a mirror in which all of God’s names and attributes are reflected together”; note 13, “That is, the letter “B” in the name “Bahá”.” Note 14 gives the Qur’án reference, 28:5.

Provisional translation by Denis MacEoin (1994) — permission-needed

MacEoin’s translation was published in Rituals in Babism and Bahá’ísm (London: British Academic Press, 1994, pp. 169–172) and is mirrored at bahai-library.com with the note “Permission: translator”. It is a provisional translation, not the text of the Writings, and may not be printed in a lyric without the translator’s permission. Two short extracts show how it differs from the authorised text. Its first call reads:

O monk of divine unity, toll the bell, for the Day of God hath been made manifest and the Beauty of the All-Glorious hath been established upon the Throne of manifest holiness.

and its refrain:

Praised be Thou, O He; O He that is He; O He that there is none other than He.

The two translations agree on the structure and the sense. They differ in wording: “toll the bell” against “Ring out the bell”, and a refrain that repeats “O He” three times against the authorised “O Thou Who art “He”, O Thou besides Whom there is none but “He””.

Provisional translation by Stephen Lambden (1999) — permission-needed

Lambden’s “tentative provisional translation”, with notes by him and by R. Jackson Armstrong-Ingram, was prepared for the Wilmette Institute (1999). The translation text is not in the extracted copy held in the corpus; only the introduction and notes are. Lambden says he consulted the Arabic printed in Adʻiyyih-i-Ḥaḍrat-i-Maḥbúb (Cairo, 1920–21) and Risálih-yi Ayyám-i-Tisʻih, unpublished manuscripts, and the earlier translations by ʻAlí-Qulí Khán with Marzieh Gail (unpublished) and MacEoin, and that his own is “not … in any way superior to those just mentioned”. Lambden also published a translation and introduction in Lights of ʻIrfán 4 (2003); it was not read for this page.

Themes and images

A garden where the Day is announced. The opening paragraph calls the Tablet itself “the garden of Paradise”, with anthems, melodies and the “Nightingale of Eternity”. The calls then address, in turn, a monk, a prophet, an angel, birds, “denizens of Paradise”, “dwellers of the kingdom of names”, and finally “ardent lovers” and “mystic knowers”. The same message is given to each: the Day has come, and each is to do what is proper to it (ring, sound, pluck, warble, rejoice).

The Day of God has come. Taherzadeh summarises the Tablet as announcing “the rising of the Orb of His Revelation” and that the One who was hidden “is now made manifest”, and that it declares the Day of God has come. The text itself says: “the Day of the Lord is come” (26.3), “the Most Great Name is come” (26.12), “the Beloved hath appeared” (26.22).

He. The refrain and the invocation turn on the name “He”, which the note in Days of Remembrance (note 12), citing the Tafsír-i-Hú, calls God’s Most Great Name. The Tablet opens “By Thy name “He”!” (26.2) and each paragraph ends by praising God as “He”.

The Báb and the Beloved. The Tablet was revealed for the anniversary of the Báb’s Declaration. Paragraph 6 speaks of “the letter Há’ … joined to the letter of ancient glory”, which note 13 reads as the letter “B” of “Bahá”. Lambden suggests that paragraphs 4 and 5 and others allude to the Báb’s Súrat al-Ḥúríyyih in the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’, where a veiled houri in silk appears; this is his reading and the sources do not confirm it elsewhere. He also reads the Tablet as celebrating Bahá’u’lláh’s station, declared in the Riḍván of 1863.

The powers of the world are told to yield. Paragraphs 16–19 address the sun of worldly dominion, the earth of knowledge, the lamp of earthly sovereignty and the seas of the world, and tell each to give way: “Eclipse thy face”, “Swallow up thy learning”, “Put out thy light”, “Still the pounding of your waves”.

Separation ended. “The day of separation hath ended, and the Covenant hath been fulfilled” (26.22); “the time of remoteness hath passed” (26.23).

The petition: detachment, then victory. The prayer asks that God’s servants be enabled “to dispense with all but Thee” (26.26), that He “kindle … the fire of Thy love, that it may burn away the mention of aught else” (26.28), and that they be made “victorious over all who dwell on earth” (26.30). Taherzadeh reads the last request as already shown in the history of the Faith (vol. 2, p. 20); the Tablet’s own closing text grounds it in the Qur’ánic promise to “those who were brought low in the land”.

What is not settled

  • Exact date. Taherzadeh writes “on the eve of the 5th of Jamadiyu’l-Avval 1280 A.H. (19 October 1863)”; Lambden writes “on the evening of the (lunar) celebration” of the Declaration, 5 Jumádí I 1280 = 19 October 1863. Whether “eve” and “evening” mean the same hours is not stated, and the sources do not say which evening of the Gregorian calendar is meant beyond 19 October.
  • Title and refrain spelling. The sources use Subḥánika, Subhánáka and Subḥánaka. The Arabic was not checked. The meaning given in all of them is “Praised be Thou, O He”.
  • Language. Taherzadeh and Lambden both call the Tablet Arabic. Lambden calls it “wholly Arabic”.
  • Verse. No source examined says the Tablet is “poetry” in a technical sense; the evidence is its structure of calls and refrain and the statement that it was chanted.
  • The Húd address. Paragraph 4 addresses “Húd, Prophet of the Divine Decree”. The authorised notes do not comment on it. No source was found that says whom Bahá’u’lláh means by it, so a lyric should not explain it.
  • The speaker. The Tablet opens with a statement in the third person (26.1) and then addresses a monk and others. The sources do not identify the “Monk of the Divine Unity”. Lambden reads it as Bahá’u’lláh Himself, “the secreted ‘Monk of the Divine Unicity’”; this is his interpretation.
  • Page references for Taherzadeh. The pages (18–21, 441) are from the print page markers in the text copy at [source].

For songwriting

These are notes, not lyrics.

Shortest exact phrases (authorised translation, DOR 26; ok-with-credit):

  • “Praise be to Thee, O Thou Who art “He”” (26.3 and every call; the natural refrain)
  • “Ring out the bell, for the Day of the Lord is come” (26.3)
  • “Sing out and chant in the sweetest of tones” (26.10)
  • “Intone the name of the Beloved” (26.11)
  • “Rejoice in your souls, for the day of separation hath ended” (26.22)
  • “Let your hearts be filled with joy, for the time of remoteness hath passed” (26.23)
  • “Kindle, then, within their breasts … the fire of Thy love” (26.28; the omission is marked)

Images that are in the text: the bell, the clarion and trumpet, strings plucked with “the fingers of the spirit”, the nightingale and the rose, the garden of Paradise, birds on twigs and boughs, the peacock and the cockerel, the sun eclipsed, the lamp put out, the sea stilled, a silken vesture of light, the fire of love.

A possible shape. The Tablet already works as a song: one voice sings the call, a group answers with the refrain. A song can keep that: verses of calls, each answered by the same line. The order in the text runs from the garden and the bell (a summons) through the birds and the heavens (everything called to sing), to the lovers (the end of separation), and then to a quiet petition for detachment and for the believers’ victory. A lyric could follow that order: summons, joy, then asking.

Historical anchors, if a lyric wants them: the evening of the anniversary of the Báb’s Declaration, 1863, Constantinople; the refrain chanted by a group; prisoners in Egypt chanting it in a dark cell (Taherzadeh, vol. 2, p. 441). The melody noted around 1906 (Armstrong-Ingram, Music, Devotions, and Mashriqu’l-Adhkár, p. 345) is a possible model for a sung refrain; whether it may be reused is a matter for the copyright holder of that book.

What a lyric must not claim:

  • That the Tablet’s words are a song text by Bahá’u’lláh in a fixed meter, or that it is “poetry” in a stated form. The sources say it was chanted, with a refrain.
  • That “He” in the refrain means any person other than God. The authorised note calls “He” God’s Most Great Name. The refrain addresses God (“Praise be to Thee”).
  • That “Monk of the Divine Unity” or “Húd” has a settled identity (see above).
  • That MacEoin’s or Lambden’s wording is Scripture. Use the authorised text for the Writings’ words; a provisional translation must be labelled “provisional translation” in any publication, and the Universal House of Justice’s policy asks that such translations used in books or pamphlets be cleared through the Bahá’í World Centre (4 July 1999 policy on authorised and provisional translations).
  • That the 1863 date or the request by Áqá Muḥammad-ʻAlí rests on the Writings alone. It rests on Taherzadeh and Lambden, both scholarship.
  • Any tune as “the” tune of the Tablet. The sources record one melody for the refrain used in the early 1900s and in Tehran in the 1930s–40s, not a prescribed one.
