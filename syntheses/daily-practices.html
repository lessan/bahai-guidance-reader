What Daily Practices Should a Bahá’í Have?

Last updated 8 August 2026

Executive summary

The daily obligations Bahá’u’lláh actually places on an adult Bahá’í are four, and they are shorter than most people assume: one obligatory prayer of your choosing, ninety-five repetitions of “Alláh-u-Abhá” after ablutions, reading from the revealed Word morning and evening, and — for nineteen days each year — the Fast. Said in their simplest permitted form, the first three fit inside about fifteen minutes. Everything else customarily filed under “Bahá’í daily practice” — meditation, memorisation, family devotions, self-examination, teaching conversations, acts of service — is enjoined, consequential, and deliberately left without prescribed form. Shoghi Effendi’s secretary put the design principle bluntly in a letter of 24 June 1949: “Bahá’u’lláh has reduced all ritual and form to an absolute minimum in His Faith.”

My position on the central question: the characteristic failure among practising Bahá’ís is not laxity but conflation. Friends either inflate the optional into obligation — building an hour-long devotional programme, missing it for a week, and concluding they have failed — or they dissolve the obligatory into whatever currently feels meaningful, and quietly stop saying an obligatory prayer at all. The Writings legislate against both, and against over-practice as explicitly as against neglect: “Read ye the sacred verses in such measure that ye be not overcome by languor and despondency.” The discipline the texts teach is to keep the small binding core exactly and unfailingly, and to let everything above it be free, variable, and joyful.

And the practical judgement I would press hardest: for most believers today the thing competing for the morning is not sloth. It is the feed. The single highest-leverage change is not more devotional time but placing the daily reading before the phone — a decision about sequence, not duration.

The obligations are few, fixed, and small

Bahá’u’lláh legislates daily worship in three verses of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas, and each one is notably economical. The first establishes obligatory prayer and immediately reduces its burden:

“We have enjoined obligatory prayer upon you, with nine rak‘ahs, to be offered at noon and in the morning and the evening unto God, the Revealer of Verses. We have relieved you of a greater number, as a command in the Book of God.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶6

The relief is the point. What survived from the Bábí Dispensation into this one is a shorter obligation, not a longer one, and Bahá’u’lláh then made it shorter still by revealing three alternative prayers of which the believer says only one. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi on 10 January 1936 sets out the choice with unusual precision: the short prayer “consists of a single verse which has to be recited once every twenty-four hours and at midday”; the medium is said “three times a day, in the morning, at noon and in the evening” with prescribed gestures; the long “has to be recited only once in every twenty-four hours, and at any time one feels inclined to do so.” And then the operative sentence: “The believer is entirely free to choose any one of these three prayers, but is under the obligation of reciting one of them.”

Choosing the short prayer is therefore not a lesser observance. Writing on the Guardian’s behalf in 1949 to a believer troubled by the genuflections, his secretary advised using “only the short midday Obligatory Prayer,” explaining that turning towards ‘Akká “is a physical symbol of an inner reality, just as the plant stretches out to the sunlight.” The forms attached to the two longer prayers “are only symbols of the inner attitude. There is a wisdom in them, and a great blessing, but we cannot force ourselves to understand or feel these things, that is why He gave us also the very short and simple prayer.”

The second daily verse adds a practice widely under-observed in the West until quite recently:

“It hath been ordained that every believer in God, the Lord of Judgment, shall, each day, having washed his hands and then his face, seat himself and, turning unto God, repeat ‘Alláh-u-Abhá’ ninety-five times.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶18

Ablutions precede it, but not twice: Questions and Answers 77 settles that ablutions performed for the obligatory prayer suffice for the ninety-five repetitions. Said at an unhurried pace this takes two or three minutes. The Universal House of Justice called it “this simple act of worshipful meditation.”

The third is the reading of the Word, and it is the verse that governs the temper of all the others:

“Recite ye the verses of God every morn and eventide… Pride not yourselves on much reading of the verses or on a multitude of pious acts by night and day; for were a man to read a single verse with joy and radiance it would be better for him than to read with lassitude all the Holy Books of God… Read ye the sacred verses in such measure that ye be not overcome by languor and despondency.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶149

Asked directly what this verse required, Bahá’u’lláh answered in Questions and Answers 68 that “the intention is all that hath been sent down from the Heaven of Divine Utterance. The prime requisite is the eagerness and love of sanctified souls to read the Word of God. To read one verse, or even one word, in a spirit of joy and radiance, is preferable to the perusal of many Books.” The notes to the Aqdas add a precision worth knowing: Shoghi Effendi clarified that “verses of God” means the revealed Word — it does not include the writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, nor his own. A morning spent with The Advent of Divine Justice is study; it is not the discharge of this exhortation. A single passage of Gleanings or the Hidden Words is.

Fourth, in its season, comes the Fast: “Abstain from food and drink from sunrise to sundown” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶17) through the month of ‘Alá’. Bahá’u’lláh pairs it with prayer so consistently that the two function as a single instrument: “Fasting and obligatory prayer are as two wings to man’s life.” Elsewhere He is more vivid still — “the religion of God is like unto heaven; fasting is its sun, and obligatory prayer is its moon.” The practical detail is treated in the-fast-practical-guide.

Two structural facts complete the picture. Obligatory prayer is solitary: “It hath been ordained that obligatory prayer is to be performed by each of you individually. Save in the Prayer for the Dead, the practice of congregational prayer hath been annulled” (¶12). And the obligations begin at fifteen and are lifted freely — Bahá’u’lláh exempts “those who are weak from illness or age” (¶10), defines that age as seventy, and states without qualification that “in time of ill health it is not permissible to observe these obligations.” Exemption is not concession; it is instruction. Believers who fast through illness because it feels more devout are, on the text’s own terms, disobeying.

1999: the year the whole law fell on the whole community

For most of the twentieth century, Western Bahá’ís lived under a partial application of these laws. That ended with a letter of 28 December 1999 to the Bahá’ís of the World, written after the Universal House of Justice reviewed, over the course of the Four Year Plan, which unapplied laws of the Aqdas it was timely to implement. The reasoning is worth reading before the ruling:

“In every land we see a growing thirst for spiritual life and moral clarity. There is recognition of the ineffectiveness of plans and programs for human betterment which are not rooted in lives of spiritual awareness and ethical virtue. Who should be better equipped to satisfy this longing than those who are already inspired by the Teachings of Bahá’u’lláh and aided by His Power?” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 1999 (muhj-1986-2001)

The determination that followed named three laws — “the laws which pertain to obligatory prayer, fasting and recitation of the Greatest Name ninety-five times a day” — and closed the remaining gaps in the first two: “a number of aspects of the law, such as those concerning ablutions, traveling and the compensation for prayers missed, remained to be made universally applicable. This step is now taken. Thus all elements of the laws dealing with obligatory prayer and fasting are, without any exception, now applicable.”

Three practical consequences follow, and they are the ones friends most often miss. Ablutions are part of the law, not a devotional flourish. Travel provisions are part of the law — a single prostration in place of each obligatory prayer missed for want of a safe place, with the designated verse and eighteen repetitions afterwards (¶14). And the Text itself anticipates missed prayers and provides for them (Questions and Answers 58–60). This last point is pastorally decisive: a legal system that legislates compensation is a system that expects lapses. The believer who missed a week is not in a special state of failure; the believer is in the ordinary state the Book was written for.

The same letter carried the recitation of the Greatest Name from a practice of the Eastern friends to a practice of the whole community, and then immediately turned the individual outward:

“The spiritual growth generated by individual devotions is reinforced by loving association among the friends in every locality, by worship as a community and by service to the Faith and to one’s fellow human beings.” — The Universal House of Justice, 28 December 1999 (muhj-1986-2001)

An accompanying letter answered the residual question of scope: all the exhortations listed in the Synopsis — reciting the holy verses at morn and eventide among them — “are applicable universally at the present time insofar as it is possible for the friends to implement them.” Reciting the Word morning and evening is codified among the Book’s exhortations rather than among its penal or personal-status laws. Bahá’u’lláh’s own language around it is nevertheless as grave as anything in the Aqdas: whoever fails to recite them “hath not been faithful to the Covenant of God and His Testament.”

What is enjoined but never prescribed

Beyond those four obligations lies a second layer the Writings press hard and specify almost not at all. The distinction is not accidental; it is the design.

Meditation is the clearest case. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives it an extraordinary valuation in Paris Talks: “Meditation is the key for opening the doors of mysteries. In that state man abstracts himself: in that state man withdraws himself from all outside objects.” He describes the faculty as a mirror — “if you turn the mirror of your spirits heavenwards, the heavenly constellations and the rays of the Sun of Reality will be reflected in your hearts” — and warns that “some thoughts are useless to man; they are like waves moving in the sea without result.” And yet, writing on the Guardian’s behalf on 25 January 1943:

“There are no set forms of meditation prescribed in the teachings, no plan, as such, for inner development. The friends are urged—nay enjoined—to pray, and they also should meditate, but the manner of doing the latter is left entirely to the individual.” — On behalf of Shoghi Effendi, 25 January 1943 (prayer-devotional-life)

Enjoined, then left entirely to the individual. A believer who reads the Word slowly, pauses, and thinks about it has meditated. The guidance adds only two cautions: prefer “the Meditations given by Bahá’u’lláh” to techniques imported from elsewhere, and “guard against superstitious or foolish ideas creeping into it.”

The same architecture protects non-obligatory prayer. Responding in 1934 to a question about how the friends should recite prayers together, the Guardian’s secretary drew the line precisely: obligatory prayer “is obligatory and must… be recited privately,” while free prayer “is neither compulsory nor is there any prescribed way for its recital.” The friends are “left free to follow their own inclination” — with one caution, that any manner they practise “should not acquire too rigid a character, and thus develop into an institution.” A Faith with no clergy guards jealously against the spontaneous re-growth of ritual.

Memorisation is commended without quota. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá writes simply: “We should memorize the Hidden Words, follow the exhortations of the Incomparable Lord, and conduct ourselves in a manner which befitteth our servitude at the threshold of the one true God.” The Guardian advised a young believer in 1939 “to endeavour to commit to memory certain passages from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh, and in particular, some of His prayers,” noting that this “would also serve to considerably deepen and enrich your own spiritual life at present.” Memorised text is the only text available in the hospital corridor, on the road, in the hour when the book is not to hand.

Self-examination is the one daily practice named as daily outside the devotional laws, and it is named by Bahá’u’lláh Himself:

“O SON OF BEING! Bring thyself to account each day ere thou art summoned to a reckoning; for death, unheralded, shall come upon thee and thou shalt be called to give account for thy deeds.” — Bahá’u’lláh, The Hidden Words, Arabic 31

No form is given — no examen, no journal, no checklist. But the object of the accounting is specified elsewhere in the same book, and it is not others: “How couldst thou forget thine own faults and busy thyself with the faults of others?” (Arabic 26). Shoghi Effendi extends the same discipline into conduct in The Advent of Divine Justice, where the chaste and holy life “demands daily vigilance in the control of one’s carnal desires and corrupt inclinations.” Vigilance is a daily practice with no clock attached to it.

Teaching, finally, is folded into the ordinary day rather than scheduled beside it. The Guardian asks that every believer “make of the mandate of teaching, so vitally binding upon all, the all-pervading concern of his life” — and then locates it exactly where a person actually is: “In his daily activities and contacts, in all his journeys, whether for business or otherwise, on his holidays and outings.” Teaching, on this account, is not an activity added to the day. It is a quality of attention carried through it. The distinctions between conversation, deepening, and direct teaching are worked out in teaching-vs-direct-vs-meaningful-conversation.

The rhythm is not only daily

An individual devotional life that never becomes communal is, in the Bahá’í conception, incomplete — and the guidance since 1996 has been consistent on this. The Riḍván 1996 message stated that “it is essential to the spiritual life of the community that the friends hold regular devotional meetings in local Bahá’í centres, where available, or elsewhere, including the homes of believers.” By Riḍván 2008 the House of Justice could describe a community “shaping a pattern of life distinguished for its devotional character.” And in a letter of 29 December 2015 it drew the portrait fully:

“Devotional meetings are occasions where any soul may enter, inhale the heavenly fragrances, experience the sweetness of prayer, meditate upon the Creative Word, be transported on the wings of the spirit, and commune with the one Beloved.” — The Universal House of Justice, 29 December 2015 (muhj-2001-2022)

The same letter makes the claim that matters for a page about daily practice: “By convening a gathering for worship at which adults and children of any background are welcome, the spirit of the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár is evoked in any locality.” The House of Worship is not a distant building one visits; its spirit is available in a living room on a Tuesday (mashriqul-adhkar-unique-role).

For households with children, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes the practice concretely, and the detail is arresting — it is a morning practice:

“Every day at first light, ye gather the Bahá’í children together and teach them the communes and prayers. This is a most praiseworthy act… These children are even as young plants, and teaching them the prayers is as letting the rain pour down upon them, that they may wax tender and fresh.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 115

The image is agricultural and it sets the expectation correctly: rain on a seedling is frequent and light, not occasional and torrential. Two minutes of prayer with a seven-year-old every morning does more than a monthly forty-minute devotional the child endures. The family application is developed in parenting and childrens-spiritual-education-mixed-faith-home.

Outward from the day, the calendar supplies the longer beats. The Nineteen Day Feast recurs monthly, and Bahá’u’lláh sets its threshold deliberately low — “It hath been enjoined upon you to offer a feast, once in every month, though only water be served; for God hath purposed to bind hearts together” (¶67). The three-month cycle of expansion, consolidation and reflection organises the community’s collective effort (three-month-cycle-tempo). The year turns through Ayyám-i-Há, the Fast, Naw-Rúz and the Holy Days.

“Prayer and meditation, and study, without action, are of no value”

The guidance is repetitive on one point in a way that suggests it was repeatedly needed. Writing to the Hands of the Cause in Asia on 30 May 1956, the Guardian’s secretary put it flatly: “The friends… should turn to God for guidance in prayer and meditation, should study the Teachings of the Faith and then arise and act. Prayer and meditation, and study, without action, are of no value, and action must be reinforced by these other things, which strengthen the soul and nourish the mind.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá states the positive form, and it is the sentence I would put above any devotional plan:

“Therefore strive that your actions day by day may be beautiful prayers. Turn towards God, and seek always to do that which is right and noble. Enrich the poor, raise the fallen, comfort the sorrowful, bring healing to the sick, reassure the fearful, rescue the oppressed, bring hope to the hopeless, shelter the destitute!” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Paris Talks, no. 26

That is a list of daily practices. None of them appears on a devotional schedule. It also settles a question friends raise about how much of an ordinary life counts. Writing on behalf of the House of Justice on 22 September 2002, the Secretariat enumerated what service to Bahá’u’lláh includes: “the love and respect one has for one’s parents; the pursuit of one’s education; the nurturing of good health; the acquisition of a trade or profession; one’s behaviour toward others and the upholding of a high moral standard; one’s marriage and the rearing of one’s children; one’s activities in teaching the Faith… and, of course, one’s daily prayer and study of the Writings.” Daily prayer arrives last in that sentence, after the job and the marriage and the health — not because it ranks lowest but because the list is one thing, not two. Work itself is raised to worship in the Aqdas, a point developed in work-as-worship.

The counterweight against over-correction comes from the Guardian’s own household correspondence. Writing in 1947 to a believer who had been ill and was pushing too hard, his secretary said: “You should certainly safeguard your nerves, and force yourself to take time, and not only for prayer and meditation, but for real rest and relaxation. We don’t have to pray and meditate for hours in order to be spiritual.”

The competitor is not sloth

The corpus supplies the obligations. It cannot describe the specific conditions under which a believer in 2026 tries to keep them, and here external evidence is worth weighing — strictly as description of the field, never as authority for a Bahá’í principle.

The relevant fact is the attention budget. DataReportal’s Digital 2026 Global Overview Report, drawing on GWI’s Q2 2025 survey wave, puts the average internet user at roughly 18 hours 36 minutes per week on social and video feeds, and about 33.5 hours per week across all online media. Whatever the precise figure for any individual, the ratio is the point: a daily devotional core of fifteen minutes is competing against something that consumes two to three hours a day and is engineered to.

The Universal House of Justice described the mechanism sixteen years ago, in a passage about education that reads more sharply now than when it was written:

“Passivity is bred by the forces of society today. A desire to be entertained is nurtured from childhood, with increasing efficiency, cultivating generations willing to be led by whoever proves skilful at appealing to superficial emotions.” — The Universal House of Justice, Riḍván 2010 (muhj-2001-2022)

The same message names the practice that stands against it: “the habit of daily reading will remain an integral part of Bahá’í identity.” Habit is the operative word, and it is where the empirical literature is genuinely useful. Phillippa Lally and colleagues, tracking 96 people forming new daily behaviours over twelve weeks (European Journal of Social Psychology, 2010), found a median of 66 days to reach the plateau of automaticity, with a range from 18 to 254 days — and, importantly, that missing a single day did not measurably impair the formation curve. Two findings follow for a believer starting an obligatory prayer for the first time: it will feel effortful for roughly two months, which is normal and not a verdict; and a missed day is not a reset. The Aqdas already assumed as much when it legislated compensation.

Where the literature does not help is in validating the practice itself, and honesty requires saying so. An online randomised trial published in BMC Psychology in July 2024 assigned 702 self-identifying Christians a daily contemplative practice for 28 days under three framings — explicitly religious, non-religious, and simple daily reflection — and found no differences between groups on the primary well-being outcome at one week, though all groups improved from baseline. Devotional practice is not commanded because it is a validated well-being intervention, and a synthesis that leaned on wellness research to recommend obligatory prayer would be making an argument the Writings do not make. Bahá’u’lláh’s own stated reason is different in kind: obligatory prayer is “conducive to humility and submissiveness, to setting one’s face towards God,” as ‘Abdu’l-Bahá explains — a relationship, not an outcome.

Tools have improved. The Bahá’í Prayers app (iOS and Android) carries the authorised prayers in scores of languages with search, bookmarks and reminders; Dawn — Bahá’í Writings exists specifically to support the daily reading and obligatory prayer. They are genuinely useful, and they carry a design problem worth naming: the device that serves the prayer is the device that serves the feed. Opening a prayer app requires unlocking a screen that has been optimised by other parties to keep you there. A printed prayer book on the nightstand is not nostalgia; it is the removal of a failure mode.

If you build software, the leverage is unusual and specific. Nearly every devotional app is designed to maximise engagement, because that is the default grammar of the medium. The right design goal here is the opposite: an application that gets you out. No streaks, no badges, no feed of others’ devotions, no notification that shames. Open, read one passage, close. A tool whose success metric is time not spent in it is unusual to build and, on the evidence of ¶149, exactly what the law asks for.

Taking a position: a rule of life you can actually keep

The recommendation follows the structure of the law rather than the aspirations of the enthusiastic.

Keep the core exact and keep it small. One obligatory prayer, said individually. Ninety-five repetitions of the Greatest Name, seated, after washing hands and face. A passage of Bahá’u’lláh or the Báb in the morning and again in the evening. That is the whole of it, and it is defensible on a bad day, in an airport, during a family crisis, in the third trimester, and at the end of a fourteen-hour shift. A practice that survives the worst week of the year is a practice; a practice that requires a good week is a hobby.

Choose the short prayer without embarrassment, and change it when your life changes. The obligation is to say one, not to say the impressive one. Bahá’u’lláh Himself indicates that the long prayer “should be said at those times when one feeleth himself in a prayerful mood.”

Fix the sequence, not the duration. The reading goes before the phone. This is the single change with the highest ratio of effect to effort, because it does not ask for more time — it asks for an ordering. Bahá’u’lláh points the same way: “Occupy thyself in remembrance of the Beauty of Him Who is the Unconstrained at early morn, and seek communion with Him at the hour of dawn.”

Let the second layer be free. Meditate in whatever manner suits you; memorise what moves you; keep the daily accounting in whatever form you will actually keep. Resist the temptation to systematise these into a personal liturgy — the Guardian’s caution that free practice “should not acquire too rigid a character” was written for exactly the temperament that reads articles like this one.

Attach one communal beat to the private core. A devotional gathering, a study circle, a home visit, the Feast. Individual devotion that never becomes communal worship is, on the 1999 letter’s own account, missing half of what it was given for.

When it collapses, resume without ceremony. The law provides compensation for missed prayers because the Lawgiver expected them. Two months of effort before it feels natural is the ordinary human curve, not evidence of a defective soul.

The measure of all of it is the verse that closes ¶149 — the standard by which a Bahá’í should judge their own devotional life: not volume, not consistency records, not the elaborateness of the routine, but whether the practice “will lighten and uplift” the soul, “so that they may soar on the wings of the Divine verses.” A devotional life that has become a source of guilt has, by the Book’s own criterion, gone wrong — and the correction is almost always to make it smaller and more faithful, not larger.

Open questions / honest limits

  • “Verses of God” and what satisfies the morning and evening exhortation. Shoghi Effendi’s clarification that the term excludes the writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and his own is recorded in the notes to the Aqdas, but the sources do not specify a minimum length, and Questions and Answers 68 explicitly resists quantification. This page does not supply a word count, because the Text declines to.
  • Whether the morning and evening reading is a law or an exhortation. It is codified among the Synopsis’s Miscellaneous Exhortations, which the House of Justice made universally applicable in 1999 “insofar as it is possible for the friends to implement them” — while Bahá’u’lláh’s own language in ¶149 is as strong as any in the Book. This page treats it as binding in substance and does not attempt to resolve the classification further.
  • Timing questions the sources leave open. The intervals for the medium prayer are defined in the notes; what an individual should do when shift work, polar latitudes or travel disrupt them is addressed only in outline (¶10 permits gauging times by clock where days and nights grow long). Specific cases belong to Assemblies, not to a synthesis.
  • The self-accounting practice has no prescribed form, and this page has not invented one. Descriptions of how Bahá’ís actually keep it are anecdotal.
  • The external research will age and is contested. Screen-time figures are self-reported and vary substantially by source; the 66-day habit finding comes from a single study of 96 people; the 2024 contemplative-practice trial is one negative result, not a settled literature. None of it grounds a Bahá’í principle, and none should be cited as if it did.
  • This page does not settle what constitutes an adequate personal deepening programme, how Ḥuqúqu’lláh computation fits an annual rhythm (huqququllah-not-a-tax), or questions of ritual purity beyond the ablutions named in the Text.
