Nature, God and the Educator: Some Answered Questions 1–3

Last updated 12 October 2026

What this page covers

Some Answered Questions opens with three short chapters, about 2,500 words together, that set out an argument in three steps. Chapter 1 describes nature as a system of exact laws that has no awareness and no will. Chapter 2 gives several proofs that a Creator exists. Chapter 3 argues that every kind of being, the human being included, needs an educator, and that the educator humanity needs must have a power above human power. The rest of Part 1 then presents Abraham, Moses, Christ, Muḥammad, the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh as instances of that educator.

The chapters make six claims:

  1. Nature is ordered law, not a mind. All things, from atoms to stars, follow “one universal law from which they never depart”, but nature itself “has neither awareness nor will” (ch. 1).
  2. Ordered law without understanding points beyond itself. Order of this kind cannot be credited to nature alone, and a part (the human being, with will and consciousness) cannot have perfections its source lacks (ch. 1).
  3. What is dependent and deficient points to what is independent and perfect. Man did not make himself; a work is less than its maker; weakness, ignorance and dependence imply power, knowledge and an independent Being (ch. 2).
  4. These arguments are offered for people who need arguments. Someone whose inner vision is open needs none (ch. 2 ¶8).
  5. Every realm needs an educator, and education has three kinds: material, human and spiritual (ch. 3).
  6. The educator of all three kinds must be more than human. The evidence is what the founders of religions achieved, alone, against all the powers of their time (ch. 3).

The page is also the research base for a video episode on these chapters, with a host, a sceptical evolutionary biologist as guest, and a narrator who reads the passages. It walks through each chapter paragraph by paragraph; gathers the same arguments from other texts of Bahá’u’lláh and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, above all the Tablet to Auguste Forel; describes the debate these chapters entered, in Europe and America and in the Arab and Persian world around 1900; and sets out the objections a modern biologist would raise, with what the texts say and what they leave open.

The setting: the opening of the book

Some Answered Questions records answers ‘Abdu’l-Bahá gave to Laura Clifford Barney in ‘Akká between 1904 and 1906. The Foreword to the 2014 translation says that “Many of the conversations took place at the lunch table”, that His replies were taken down in Persian, and that “‘Abdu’l‑Bahá then corrected these notes twice in His own hand, sometimes substantially revising them in the process as well as carefully reviewing the final wording.” That review is why the book carries the authority of His own writing, unlike notes of His talks that He did not see.

The order of the chapters is not the order of the talks. Barney’s preface says: “Originally they were not given in any special order, but have now been roughly classified for the convenience of the reader.” So chapters 1–3 stand first because she, with His permission, put them first. There is one piece of evidence that chapter 1 was also among the first talks. In a letter of 1937, printed in Youness Afroukhteh’s Memories of Nine Years in ‘Akká, Barney writes: “The first subject which the Master revealed is presented in the initial chapter of the book. I think this is the same chapter which was originally written in English.” Afroukhteh’s translator notes that the letter survives only in Persian and was translated back into English, so these are not her own English words, and she herself says “I think”. It is a reasonable reading, not a settled fact, that chapter 1 records the first answer she wrote down.

Three features of the chapters fit that history:

  • Chapter 1 has no question. Most chapters in the book begin “QUESTION:”. Chapters 1–3 begin directly with the answer.
  • The table is present in the text. In chapter 2 ¶6 the example is “this piece of bread”. The Persian has ín nán, “this bread”: something on the table in front of the speaker.
  • The part title is about education. The English title of Part 1 is “On the Influence of the Prophets in the Evolution of Humanity”. The Persian heading in the 1908 edition is dar ta’thír-i anbiyá dar taraqqí va tarbiyat-i naw’-i insání, literally “on the influence of the prophets on the progress and education of the human race”, followed by “(conversation at lunch)”. Tarbiyat (education, upbringing, cultivation) is the key word of chapter 3, so the Persian title already names the conclusion the first three chapters reach.

The Foreword gives a reading rule that applies strongly here: “the concepts required to fully understand a given subject may be spread across different chapters, or the contents of a subsequent chapter may form the basis for the understanding of an earlier one.” Chapters 1 and 2 are compressed. Their fuller treatment is in chapters 47 (the origin of the universe), 48 (man and animal), 59 (man’s knowledge of God), and in the Tablet to Forel of 1921, all discussed below.

Chapter 1: “Nature Is Governed by a Universal Law”

The Persian title is ṭabí’at dar taḥt-i qánún-i ’umúmí-st, “nature is under a universal law”.

¶1. What “nature” means

“NATURE IS THAT condition or reality which outwardly is the source of the life and death, or, in other words, of the composition and decomposition, of all things.” (¶1)

The definition is narrow and functional. Nature is what “outwardly” accounts for things coming together and coming apart. “Composition and decomposition” (tarkíb va taḥlíl) is a phrase ‘Abdu’l-Bahá uses throughout the book: a being exists when elements combine and ceases when they separate. The word “outwardly” matters. The chapter does not deny that nature really does these things; it says that this is the level at which nature is the cause.

¶2. Order from the atom to the stars

“This nature is subject to a sound organization, to inviolable laws, to a perfect order, and to a consummate design, from which it never departs. To such an extent is this true that were you to gaze with the eye of insight and discernment, you would observe that all things—from the smallest invisible atom to the largest globes in the world of existence, such as the sun or the other great stars and luminous bodies—are most perfectly organized, be it with regard to their order, their composition, their outward form, or their motion, and that all are subject to one universal law from which they never depart.” (¶2)

This is the premise, and it is one any physicist of 1905 or today would accept: nature is lawful, and its laws hold everywhere. The phrase translated “a consummate design” is handasa-yi báligha in the Persian, literally “a consummate geometry” or “a perfect measurement”. The word handasa is the ordinary Persian and Arabic word for geometry and engineering. The English “design” is a fair translation, but readers who know the English design debate should note that the Persian word stresses exact proportion more than purpose.

¶3. Nature has neither awareness nor will

“When you consider nature itself, however, you see that it has neither awareness nor will. For instance, the nature of fire is to burn; it burns without consciousness or will. The nature of water is to flow; it flows without consciousness or will. The nature of the sun is to shed light; it shines without consciousness or will. The nature of vapour is to rise; it rises without consciousness or will. It is therefore evident that the natural movements of all created things are compelled, and that nothing moves of its own will save animals and, in particular, man.” (¶3)

Here the chapter agrees with the materialists of its day. A talk in Chicago in 1912 says so in as many words: “it is philosophically predicated that nature is without volition and innate perception. In this statement and principle we agree with the materialists” (Promulgation, 2 May 1912). The four examples (fire, water, sun, vapour) are the classical elements and the sun: the most basic natural processes. The conclusion of the paragraph is that natural movements are “compelled” (Persian majbúr), the same word used for the compelled matters of human life in chapter 70.

¶4. Man rules over nature

“Man is able to resist and oppose nature inasmuch as he discovers the natures of things and, by virtue of this discovery, has mastery over nature itself. Indeed, all the crafts that man has devised proceed from this discovery. For example, he has invented the telegraph, which connects the East and the West. It is therefore evident that man rules over nature.” (¶4)

The human being is the exception to ¶3, and the mark of the exception is discovery: man learns the laws of nature and uses them. The telegraph was the obvious example in 1904. ‘Akká had a telegraph office. God Passes By records that when an Ottoman commission of inquiry came to investigate ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, its members “placed under their direct and exclusive control both the Telegraph and Postal services in ‘Akká”. The Tablet to Forel and the American talks extend the list: flight, submarines, the phonograph, photography (“all shadows are fleeting, whereas man fixeth them upon the plate”), the electric lamp.

The paragraph does not say that man breaks natural law in the sense of a miracle. A telegraph obeys the laws of electricity. The claim is that a being who understands the laws can direct them to ends nature itself does not pursue.

¶5. The inference

“Now, can such organization, order, and laws as you observe in existence be attributed merely to the effect of nature, notwithstanding that nature itself has neither consciousness nor understanding? It is therefore evident that this nature, which has neither consciousness nor understanding, is in the grasp of the omnipotent Lord, Who is the Ruler of the world of nature and Who causes it to manifest whatsoever He desires.” (¶5)

This is the first argument for God in the book, and it is not the argument Paley made. Paley argued from particular contrivances, such as the eye, to a designer of each. Chapter 1 argues from the existence of law itself. Its question is whether lawfulness, the fact that everything “never departs” from “one universal law”, can be “attributed merely to the effect of nature” when nature has no understanding. This difference matters for the modern debate. Natural selection explains how particular adaptations arise from the operation of laws. It does not explain why there are laws, and chapter 1’s question is about the laws.

¶6. The branch and the root

“Some say that human existence is among those things that have appeared in the world of being and that are due to the exigencies of nature. Were this true, man would be the branch and nature the root. But is it possible that there could exist a will, a consciousness, and certain perfections in the branch which are absent in the root?” (¶6)

“Exigencies of nature” (muqtaḍayát-i ṭabí’at) means what nature necessarily produces. The view answered here is that human beings, with their will and consciousness, are simply one more product of natural processes. The answer is a principle about parts and wholes, or effects and causes: a branch cannot have what the root lacks. The Tablet to Forel states the same principle: “the part dependeth upon the whole; the part cannot possess perfections whereof the whole is deprived.” The Chicago talk gives examples: “Is it conceivable that a drop should be imbued with qualities of which the ocean is completely deprived?”

This paragraph is the one a modern biologist will contest most directly (see objection 2 below). It is also the one that connects chapter 1 to the book’s later chapters on the human spirit (chs. 48–55).

¶7. Conclusion

“Hence it is clear that nature, in its very essence, is in the grasp of God’s might, and that it is that Eternal and Almighty One Who subjects nature to ideal laws and organizing principles, and Who rules over it.” (¶7)

The conclusion keeps both halves of the chapter. Nature really is governed by law; God is the One “Who subjects nature to ideal laws”. God is not presented as an alternative to natural law but as its source. Bahá’u’lláh had put the same point in the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat: “Nature is God’s Will and is its expression in and through the contingent world.” (See below.)

Chapter 2: “Proofs and Arguments for the Existence of God”

The Persian title is dalá’il va baráhín-i ulúhiyyat, “proofs and arguments of divinity”. The chapter has eight paragraphs and at least four distinct arguments. The traditional names in the notes below are this article’s labels, not the text’s.

¶1–2. Man did not make himself; the painting and the painter (an argument from causation and perfection)

“AMONG THE PROOFS and arguments for the existence of God is the fact that man has not created himself, but rather that his creator and fashioner is another than he. And it is certain and indisputable that the creator of man is not like man himself, because a powerless being cannot create another being, and an active creator must possess all perfections to produce his handiwork.” (¶1)

“Is it possible for the handiwork to be perfect and the craftsman imperfect? Is it possible for a painting to be a masterpiece and the painter to be deficient in his craft, notwithstanding that he is its creator? No: The painting cannot be like the painter, for otherwise it would have painted itself. And no matter how perfect the painting may be, in comparison with the painter it is utterly deficient.” (¶2)

Two steps. First, a person did not cause his own existence; something other than himself did. Second, the cause must have at least the perfections found in the effect. The painting analogy adds a third point: the work is always less than its maker, “for otherwise it would have painted itself.” The same reasoning appears in the Chicago talk of 2 May 1912, which uses a book: “These words have not written themselves, and these letters have not come together of their own volition. It is evident there must be a writer.”

The Qur’an makes the first step in the form of a question: “Or were they created by nothing, or are they ˹their own˺ creators?” (52:35, tr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s listeners in ‘Akká would have known the verse. Chapter 2 does not cite it.

¶3–4. Deficiency testifies to perfection (an argument from privation)

“Thus the contingent world is the source of deficiencies and God is the source of perfection. The very deficiencies of the contingent world testify to God’s perfections. For example, when you consider man, you observe that he is weak, and this very weakness of the creature betokens the power of One Who is Eternal and Almighty; for were it not for power, weakness could not be imagined. Thus the weakness of the creature is evidence of the power of God: Without power there could be no weakness. This weakness makes it evident that there is a power in the world.” (¶3)

“Again, in the contingent world there is poverty; hence there must be wealth for there to be poverty in the world. In the contingent world there is ignorance; hence there must be knowledge for there to be ignorance. If there were no knowledge, neither could there be ignorance; for ignorance is the non-existence of knowledge, and if there were no existence, non-existence could not be.” (¶4)

“Contingent” (Persian imkán) is a technical term of Islamic philosophy: what exists but could have not existed, and so depends on something else for its existence. The argument rests on the idea that a deficiency is the absence of a perfection: weakness is lack of power, ignorance is lack of knowledge. An absence can only be defined against the thing that is absent, so the existence of deficiency presupposes the reality of the perfection.

The same idea, that a lack has no positive existence of its own, carries chapter 74 on evil: “ignorance is the want of knowledge, error is the want of guidance … weakness is the want of strength.” The Tablet to Forel states it most briefly: “limitation itself proveth the existence of the unlimited, for the limited is known through the unlimited, just as weakness itself proveth the existence of power … Darkness itself is a proof of the existence of light for darkness is the absence of light.”

A careful reader should notice what this argument needs to be strong. “Weakness presupposes power” is easy to grant if it means “the idea of weakness presupposes the idea of power”. It is harder if it means “there exists, somewhere, a being of unlimited power”. The step from the first to the second is the step a sceptic will question (objection 4 below).

¶5. Compulsion and dependency (an argument from contingency)

“It is certain that the entire contingent world is subject to an order and a law which it can never disobey. Even man is forced to submit to death, sleep, and other conditions—that is, in certain matters he is compelled, and this very compulsion implies the existence of One Who is All-Compelling. So long as the contingent world is characterized by dependency, and so long as this dependency is one of its essential requirements, there must be One Who in His own Essence is independent of all things. In the same way, the very existence of a sick person shows that there must be one who is healthy; for without the latter the existence of the former could not be established.” (¶5)

This is the strongest argument in the chapter philosophically, and the one least affected by biology. The key phrase is “One Who in His own Essence is independent of all things” (Persian ghaní bi’dh-dhát, “rich”, that is self-sufficient, “by essence”). Everything in the world depends on something else for its existence; dependency is not an accident of the world but one of “its essential requirements”. A world made only of dependent things needs something that is not dependent. In the vocabulary of Avicenna (d. 1037), this is the argument from the contingent (mumkin) to the Necessary Existent (wájib al-wujúd). In Western philosophy it is the argument from contingency, best known from Leibniz and from the third of Aquinas’s “five ways”.

The paragraph also links chapter 2 back to chapter 1. The “order and a law which it can never disobey” is the “one universal law” of chapter 1 ¶2, and man’s compulsion in “death, sleep, and other conditions” is the list that chapter 70 gives of the matters for which man is “not accountable”.

The Tablet to Forel gives the fuller form, as a chain of causes: “Such process of causation goes on, and to maintain that this process goes on indefinitely is manifestly absurd. Thus such a chain of causation must of necessity lead eventually to Him who is the Ever-Living, the All-Powerful, who is Self-Dependent and the Ultimate Cause.”

¶6–7. The bread and the universe (an argument from making)

“It is therefore evident that there is an Eternal and Almighty One Who is the sum of all perfections, for otherwise He would be even as the creatures. Likewise, throughout the world of existence the smallest created thing attests to the existence of a creator. For instance, this piece of bread attests that it has a maker.” (¶6)

“Gracious God! The change in the outward form of the smallest thing proves the existence of a creator: Then how could this vast, boundless universe have created itself and come to exist solely through the mutual interaction of the elements? How patently false is such a notion!” (¶7)

The bread is the nearest thing to Paley’s watch in the chapter. Paley had written in 1802 that from the arrangement of a watch’s parts “the inference, we think, is inevitable; that the watch must have had a maker”. The bread is a plainer example than the watch. It is not intricate; it is just something whose form was changed by a baker. The argument is correspondingly modest in its premise: any change of form has an agent. Paragraph 7 then argues from the smallest made thing to the whole universe.

The view rejected in ¶7 is precise: that the universe “created itself and come to exist solely through the mutual interaction of the elements”. The word “solely” is important. Chapter 47 affirms that beings do come into existence through the interaction of elements, “from the composition and combination of these elements countless beings appeared”, and adds that this happened “through the wisdom of God and His ancient might, from one natural order”. What chapter 2 denies is that the interaction of elements is the whole explanation.

¶8. “Theoretical arguments adduced for weak souls”

“These are theoretical arguments adduced for weak souls, but if the eye of inner vision be opened, a hundred thousand clear proofs will be seen. Thus, when man feels the indwelling spirit, he is in no need of arguments for its existence; but for those who are deprived of the grace of the spirit, it is necessary to set forth external arguments.” (¶8)

This last paragraph changes the status of everything before it. The Persian is adilla-yi naẓarí ... baráy-i nufús-i ḍa’ífa: “theoretical (or speculative) proofs for weak souls”. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not withdraw the arguments, but He says that they are not how a person who knows God knows Him. The analogy is self-awareness: no one needs an argument to know that he has a spirit; he “feels” it. Arguments are for those who do not have that awareness, and they are “external”.

Two consequences for discussion. First, a Bahá’í need not rest belief in God on the success of any one argument in ¶1–7, and the text itself invites that reading. Second, a sceptic will say that an appeal to inner vision cannot be checked from outside (objection 7). William James made a closely parallel point in 1902: “That vast literature of proofs of God’s existence drawn from the order of nature, which a century ago seemed so overwhelmingly convincing, to‐day does little more than gather dust in libraries”, because, he argued, religious conviction rests on experience more than on argument (The Varieties of Religious Experience, Lecture III).

Chapter 3: “The Need for an Educator”

The Persian title is mas’ala dar ithbát-i luzúm-i murabbí, “a question in proof of the necessity of an educator”. Murabbí is the person who brings up, trains or cultivates; tarbiyat, from the same root, is education, upbringing and also the cultivation of plants. The single Persian word covers the farmer, the gardener, the animal trainer and the teacher, which is why the chapter can move between them without a change of terms.

¶1–2. Land and trees

“WHEN WE CONSIDER existence, we observe that the mineral, the vegetable, the animal, and the human realms, each and all, are in need of an educator.” (¶1)

“If the land is deprived of a cultivator, it becomes a thicket of thriving weeds, but if a farmer is found to cultivate it, the resulting harvest provides sustenance for living things. It is therefore evident that the land is in need of the farmer’s cultivation. Consider the trees: If they remain uncultivated, they bear no fruit, and without fruit they are of no use. But when committed to a gardener’s care, the barren tree becomes fruitful, and, through cultivation, crossing, and grafting, the tree with bitter fruit yields sweet fruit. These are rational arguments, which are what the people of the world require in this day.” (¶2)

The chapter starts where chapter 1 left off. Chapter 1 said nature, left to itself, follows its laws without purpose. Chapter 3 says nature, left to itself, does not reach its best: untended land grows weeds and an untended tree bears bitter fruit. The Persian for “cultivation, crossing, and grafting” is tarbiyat va tarkíb va paywand. “Crossing” (hybridising) was a topic of the moment in 1904–1906: Gregor Mendel’s experiments on crossing peas had been rediscovered in 1900, and the American plant breeder Luther Burbank was famous for producing new fruits by crossing and grafting (see the context dossier for the episode). The text does not refer to either; the parallel is an observation.

The last sentence of ¶2 is a statement of method: “These are rational arguments, which are what the people of the world require in this day.” It is repeated at the end of the chapter (¶17–18), and it is the opposite emphasis from chapter 2 ¶8. There, arguments were for “weak souls”; here, rational arguments are what the present age requires. The two are compatible: arguments are not the source of faith for the person who has inner vision, but they are the shared ground on which a case can be made to anyone.

¶3–4. Animals and human beings

“Consider likewise the animals: If an animal is trained, it becomes domesticated, whereas man, if he is left without education, becomes like an animal. Indeed, if man is abandoned to the rule of nature, he sinks even lower than the animal, whereas if he is educated he becomes even as an angel. For most animals do not devour their own kind, but men in the Sudan, in the middle of Africa, rend and eat each other.” (¶3)

“Now observe that it is education that brings East and West under man’s dominion, produces all these marvellous crafts, promotes these mighty arts and sciences, and gives rise to these new discoveries and undertakings. Were it not for an educator, the means of comfort, civilization, and human virtues could in no wise have been acquired. If a man is left alone in a wilderness where he sees none of his own kind, he will undoubtedly become a mere animal. It is therefore clear that an educator is needed.” (¶4)

The argument of ¶3–4 is that what distinguishes human beings in practice, their crafts, sciences and virtues, is not given by nature but acquired through education, and that a human being raised without others would not have them.

The sentence about the Sudan. The last sentence of ¶3 says that “men in the Sudan, in the middle of Africa, rend and eat each other.” This needs careful handling in any public presentation, and the following points are offered for that:

  • In 1904, “the Sudan” (Persian súdán, from Arabic bilád al-súdán, “land of the black peoples”) was the usual Arabic and Persian name for the whole belt of Africa south of the Sahara, not only the modern country. Accounts of cannibalism in that region, some reliable and many exaggerated, were common in European travel literature of the nineteenth century. The sentence repeats a claim that was widely believed at the time. Modern historians treat many of those reports as unreliable.
  • The point of the sentence is not a claim about any people’s nature. The argument of the paragraph is that any human being left without education “sinks even lower than the animal”, and the next paragraph says the same of a man “left alone in a wilderness”. A talk given in St Paul, Minnesota on 20 September 1912 makes the same comparison between America and “central Africa” and draws the conclusion explicitly: “All are human beings. … The difference and distinction between them is the degree of education.” In 1912 that was a statement against the racial theories of the time, which held that such differences were innate.
  • For the episode: the narrator reads only verbatim text, so the choice is whether to read ¶3 in full or to stop before the last sentence. Either choice should be made openly. If the sentence is read, the host should give the historical context and the point of the argument in plain words. This article does not recommend reading the sentence without comment.

¶5–7. Three kinds of education

“But education is of three kinds: material, human, and spiritual. Material education aims at the growth and development of the body, and consists in securing its sustenance and obtaining the means of its ease and comfort. This education is common to both man and animal.” (¶5)

“Human education, however, consists in civilization and progress, that is, sound governance, social order, human welfare, commerce and industry, arts and sciences, momentous discoveries, and great undertakings, which are the central features distinguishing man from the animal.” (¶6)

“As to divine education, it is the education of the Kingdom and consists in acquiring divine perfections. This is indeed true education, for by its virtue man becomes the focal centre of divine blessings and the embodiment of the verse “Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness.” This is the ultimate goal of the world of humanity.” (¶7)

This threefold division is the core of the chapter. Material education (tarbiyat-i jismání) serves the body and is shared with animals. Human education (tarbiyat-i insání) is civilisation: government, order, trade, science, art. Divine or spiritual education (tarbiyat-i iláhiyya) is the acquiring of “divine perfections”, and it is called “true education” and “the ultimate goal of the world of humanity”. The verse quoted is Genesis 1:26 (the 2014 translation’s footnote gives the reference).

Each kind includes the one before. A society can have the first two without the third, and the chapter’s argument from here on is that the third needs a different kind of teacher.

¶8–11. One educator for all three

“Now, we need an educator who can be at the same time a material, a human, and a spiritual educator, that his authority may have effect at every degree of existence. And should anyone say, “I am endowed with perfect reason and comprehension, and have no need for such an educator”, he would be denying the obvious. It is as though a child were to say, “I have no need of education, but will act and seek the perfections of existence according to my own thinking and intelligence”, or as though a blind man were to claim, “I have no need of sight, for there are many blind people who get by.”” (¶8)

Paragraph 8 answers in advance the objection that reason is enough. The two images (the child who needs no school, the blind man who needs no sight) are pointed: the objector is compared not to a fool but to someone who cannot see what he lacks.

Paragraphs 9–11 describe what the educator does at each level. At the material level he must “organize and administer their material affairs and establish a social order” (¶9). At the human level he must “so educate human minds and thoughts that they may become capable of substantive progress; that science and knowledge may expand; that the realities of things, the mysteries of the universe, and the properties of all that exists may be revealed” (¶10). At the spiritual level he must impart an education by which “all the names and attributes of God may be reflected in the mirror of the human reality” (¶11). The middle item is worth stressing for a scientific audience: in this account, the expansion of science is part of what the divine educator is for.

¶12–14. The evidence: what one person achieved

“It is clear, however, that mere human power is incapable of fulfilling this great office, and that the results of human thought alone cannot secure such bounties. How can a single person, with no aid or assistance, lay the foundations of such a lofty edifice? A divine and spiritual power is therefore needed to enable him to carry out this mission. Behold! One sanctified Soul revives the world of humanity, transforms the face of the globe, develops the minds, quickens the souls, inaugurates a new life, establishes new foundations, orders the world, gathers the nations and religions under the shadow of one banner, delivers man from the realm of baseness and deficiency, and exhorts and encourages him to develop his innate and acquired perfections. Certainly nothing short of a divine power could accomplish this feat! One must examine this matter fairly, as this indeed is an occasion for fairness.” (¶12)

The argument is from effect to cause, the same form as chapter 2 but applied to history. A single person, without an army or a government, changes the beliefs and conduct of many nations over centuries. Ordinary human power, which “the governments and peoples of the earth, notwithstanding all their powers and their armies” (¶13) possess in far greater measure, does not do this. So the cause is more than ordinary human power.

The example is Christ: “Christ, alone and single-handed, raised the banner of peace and amity—a feat that the combined forces of all the mighty governments of the world are unable to accomplish” (¶13). The text names “Italy, France, Germany, Russia, England, and the like” gathered under one faith, and then itself raises the obvious historical objection: “After a time, however, and as a result of differing motives, divisions broke out again among them. Our meaning is that Christ united these nations, but after a long while the governments caused the resurgence of discord.” (¶13) And again: “Although after a long while the governments disrupted this unity, Christ had indeed accomplished His task.” (¶14) The phrase “One must examine this matter fairly” (¶12) invites the listener to judge the historical claim.

¶15–18. The educator must be perfect; rational, not traditional, proofs

“Our meaning is that the universal Educator must be at once a material, a human, and a spiritual educator, and, soaring above the world of nature, must be possessed of another power, so that He may assume the station of a divine teacher. Were He not to wield such a celestial power, He would not be able to educate, for He would be imperfect Himself. How then could He foster perfection? If He were ignorant, how could He make others wise? If He were unjust, how could He make others just? If He were earthly, how could He make others heavenly?” (¶15)

Paragraph 15 applies the principle of chapter 2 ¶1–2 (the maker has at least the perfections of the work) to the educator: a teacher cannot give what he does not have. The phrase “soaring above the world of nature” links back to chapter 1: the educator, like the human being but more so, is not a “captive of nature”.

“Therefore it is through rational arguments that we must prove to rational minds the prophethood of Moses, of Christ, and of the other divine Manifestations. And the proofs and arguments which we provide here are based on rational and not on traditional arguments.” (¶17)

“It has thus been established by rational arguments that the world of existence stands in utmost need of an educator, and that its education must be achieved through a celestial power. There is no doubt that this celestial power is divine revelation, and that the world must be educated through this power which transcends human power.” (¶18)

The distinction between “rational” (’aqlí) and “traditional” (naqlí) proofs is standard in Islamic theology: rational proofs rest on reason and observation, traditional proofs on scripture and reports. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says that in these chapters He uses only the first kind. Chapter 10 later uses both, and its title says so: “Rational Proofs and Traditional Arguments from the Sacred Scriptures”.

How the three chapters fit together

Read as one argument, the chapters run:

  1. Nature is lawful and has no awareness or will (1 ¶1–3).
  2. Human beings discover nature’s laws and direct them; they have will and consciousness (1 ¶3–4).
  3. Lawful order in a nature without understanding, and the presence of will and consciousness in one of nature’s products, both point to a source with understanding and will (1 ¶5–7).
  4. Dependence, deficiency and made things point to an independent and perfect Creator; but these are arguments for those who need arguments (2).
  5. Nature left to itself does not reach its best: land, trees, animals and people all need cultivation (3 ¶1–4).
  6. Education has three levels, and the highest needs a teacher with more than human power (3 ¶5–15).
  7. History shows such teachers: one person, alone, changing nations (3 ¶12–14).

The link from chapter 3 to chapter 4 is direct. Chapter 4 begins: “AMONG THOSE WHO possessed this divine power and were assisted by it was Abraham.” “This divine power” is the power that chapter 3 ¶12 and ¶18 said the educator needs. The rest of Part 1 applies the test of chapter 3 to each founder in turn.

Chapter 1 also has a second link, forward to chapter 3. Chapter 1 says nature has no will and that man rules over nature by discovering its laws. Chapter 3 says that man left to nature “sinks even lower than the animal”. The two together give a picture of the human being as standing between nature and something above it: ruling nature by intelligence, but needing education to rise above it in character. The Stanford talk of 1912 puts it in one sentence: “it is not intended that the world of humanity should be left to its natural state. It is in need of the education divinely provided for it.”

The same themes elsewhere in the Writings

Bahá’u’lláh

Nature as God’s Will (Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat). The Tablet of Wisdom, revealed in the ‘Akká period and addressed to Nabíl-i-Akbar, contains the clearest statement of the view that chapter 1 assumes:

“Say: Nature in its essence is the embodiment of My Name, the Maker, the Creator. Its manifestations are diversified by varying causes, and in this diversity there are signs for men of discernment. Nature is God’s Will and is its expression in and through the contingent world. It is a dispensation of Providence ordained by the Ordainer, the All-Wise. Were anyone to affirm that it is the Will of God as manifested in the world of being, no one should question this assertion. It is endowed with a power whose reality men of learning fail to grasp.” (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat)

This passage removes a possible misreading of chapter 1. Chapter 1 says nature has no will of its own. Bahá’u’lláh says nature is God’s Will expressed in the world. The two fit: nature does not will, because it is the expression of a will that is not its own. The same Tablet contains the shortest form of chapter 2’s argument from making: “Every thing must needs have an origin and every building a builder.” And it says, as chapter 47 does, that creation as such has no beginning in time: “God was, and His creation had ever existed beneath His shelter from the beginning that hath no beginning”. The arguments of chapter 2 are therefore arguments for the world’s dependence on God, not for a first moment of creation.

The Name of God, the Educator. Bahá’u’lláh describes education as a revelation of a divine Name, in two kinds:

“Consider, for instance, the revelation of the light of the Name of God, the Educator. Behold, how in all things the evidences of such a revelation are manifest, how the betterment of all beings dependeth upon it. This education is of two kinds. The one is universal. Its influence pervadeth all things and sustaineth them. It is for this reason that God hath assumed the title, “Lord of all worlds.” The other is confined to them that have come under the shadow of this Name, and sought the shelter of this most mighty Revelation.” (Gleanings, XCIII)

This is the background to chapter 3 ¶1, where “the mineral, the vegetable, the animal, and the human realms, each and all, are in need of an educator”. On Bahá’u’lláh’s account, the education of all things is itself a sign of God, and the education given through a Revelation is a second, particular kind.

The mine rich in gems. The best-known statement of the need for education is:

“Man is the supreme Talisman. Lack of a proper education hath, however, deprived him of that which he doth inherently possess. … Regard man as a mine rich in gems of inestimable value. Education can, alone, cause it to reveal its treasures, and enable mankind to benefit therefrom.” (Gleanings, CXXII; also in the Lawḥ-i-Maqṣúd)

Chapter 3 says the same thing in negative form: without an educator, “the means of comfort, civilization, and human virtues could in no wise have been acquired” (¶4).

Every created thing a sign. “KNOW thou that every created thing is a sign of the revelation of God. Each, according to its capacity, is, and will ever remain, a token of the Almighty.” (Gleanings, XCIII). This is the positive form of chapter 2 ¶6, “the smallest created thing attests to the existence of a creator”.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the Tablet to Auguste Forel (1921)

The Tablet to the Swiss psychiatrist and entomologist Auguste Forel, dated Haifa, 21 September 1921, is the fullest statement by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá of the arguments of chapters 1 and 2, written for a scientist who had described himself as a materialist. It was written fifteen years after the table talks and repeats their arguments in more developed form. Its main points, in order of their relevance to chapters 1–2:

Which materialists are meant. Forel had evidently asked whether ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s criticism of “materialists” applied to philosophers in general. The answer limits it:

“By materialists, whose belief with regard to Divinity hath been explained, is not meant philosophers in general, but rather that group of materialists of narrow vision who worship that which is sensed, who depend upon the five senses only, and whose criterion of knowledge is limited to that which can be perceived by the senses.”

And: “In like manner we regard the materialistic, accomplished, moderate philosophers, who have been of service (to mankind).” This matters for staging a fair argument. The target of chapters 1–2 is not science or scientists, but the claim that only what the senses can detect is real.

Nature as captive; man as free (ch. 1 ¶3–4). “The sun itself with all its majesty is so subservient to nature that it hath no will of its own and cannot deviate a hair’s-breadth from the laws thereof. … Man, however, though in body the captive of nature is yet free in his mind and soul, and hath the mastery over nature.” And: “Man hath the powers of will and understanding, but nature hath them not. Nature is constrained, man is free.”

The part and the whole (ch. 1 ¶6). “Should any one suppose that man is but a part of the world of nature, and he being endowed with these perfections, these being but manifestations of the world of nature, and thus nature is the originator of these perfections and is not deprived therefrom, to him we make reply and say: the part dependeth upon the whole; the part cannot possess perfections whereof the whole is deprived.”

Three kinds of formation. This argument is not in chapters 1–2 but completes them:

“Now, formation is of three kinds and of three kinds only: accidental, necessary and voluntary. The coming together of the various constituent elements of beings cannot be accidental, for unto every effect there must be a cause. It cannot be compulsory, for then the formation must be an inherent property of the constituent parts and the inherent property of a thing can in no wise be dissociated from it, such as light that is the revealer of things, heat that causeth the expansion of elements and the solar rays which are the essential property of the sun. Thus under such circumstances the decomposition of any formation is impossible, for the inherent properties of a thing cannot be separated from it. The third formation remaineth and that is the voluntary one, that is, an unseen force described as the Ancient Power, causeth these elements to come together, every formation giving rise to a distinct being.”

The structure is an elimination: composition is by chance, by necessity or by will; not chance (every effect has a cause); not necessity (what is necessary to a thing cannot be separated from it, but compositions do come apart); therefore will. Charles Darwin had framed the same alternatives in his autobiography, written in 1876: he found it impossible to conceive the universe “as the result of blind chance or necessity”, and so felt “compelled to look to a First Cause having an intelligent mind in some degree analogous to that of man” (see the section on the wider debate). The parallel is close; no connection between the two texts is claimed.

The chain of causes (ch. 2 ¶5). Quoted above: “to maintain that this process goes on indefinitely is manifestly absurd. Thus such a chain of causation must of necessity lead eventually to Him who is the Ever-Living, the All-Powerful, who is Self-Dependent and the Ultimate Cause.”

The organism in the fruit (ch. 2 ¶6–7). “And as we reflect, we observe that man is like unto a tiny organism contained within a fruit; this fruit hath developed out of the blossom, the blossom hath grown out of the tree, the tree is sustained by the sap, and the sap formed out of earth and water. How then can this tiny organism comprehend the nature of the garden, conceive of the gardener and comprehend his being? That is manifestly impossible. Should that organism understand and reflect, it would observe that this garden, this tree, this blossom, this fruit would in no wise have come to exist by themselves in such order and perfection.” This image joins the design argument to the limits of knowledge: the organism can infer a gardener without being able to understand him.

What the attributes of God mean. The Tablet adds a qualification that chapter 2 does not state. When we call God all-knowing or all-powerful, “these attributes and perfections that we recount for that Universal Reality are only in order to deny imperfections, rather than to assert the perfections that the human mind can conceive. Thus we say His attributes are unknowable.” This is the “negative theology” of Islamic and Christian philosophy: we know what God is not. Applied to chapter 2 ¶1 (“an active creator must possess all perfections”), it means the argument concludes that the Creator lacks no perfection, not that we understand what His perfections are. Chapter 59 says the same: “Knowing God, therefore, means the comprehension and knowledge of His attributes and not of His Reality.”

The prophets as proof (ch. 3 ¶12–14). The Tablet ends with the argument of chapter 3: “The will of every sovereign prevaileth during his reign, the will of every philosopher findeth expression in a handful of disciples during his lifetime, but the Power of the Holy Spirit shineth radiantly in the realities of the Messengers of God, and strengtheneth Their will in such wise as to influence a great nation for thousands of years and to regenerate the human soul and revive mankind. … It is an extraordinary Power, an all-sufficient proof of the truth of the mission of the Prophets of God”.

The Tablet also uses the ether of nineteenth-century physics as an example of something known only by its effects: “the nature of ether is unknown, but that it existeth is certain by the effects it produceth”. Chapter 48 ¶12 uses the same example, together with gravity. The ether hypothesis was abandoned after 1905. The logical point, that some realities are inferred from effects rather than perceived, does not depend on the example, and gravity (or, today, fields or dark matter) serves it as well. A sceptical guest is likely to raise the ether, and the honest answer is that the example is dated and the point is not.

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: other chapters of Some Answered Questions

  • Chapter 47 (“The Origin of the Universe and the Evolution of Man”) says the universe “has no beginning” (¶1), that “originally matter was one” (¶4), and that beings arise from the composition of elements “through the wisdom of God and His ancient might, from one natural order”, so that creation “is a divine creation and not an accidental composition and arrangement” (¶5). It describes the earth and the human form developing gradually “over a myriad ages and centuries” (¶8). Chapter 47 is the place where the argument of chapters 1–2 meets the subject of evolution directly.
  • Chapter 48 (“The Difference between Man and Animal”) reports the “present-day philosophers” who “place man in the lineage of the animal” (¶3), answers with the human “power of discovery” (¶4–7), and says that the human spirit, like the animal spirit, is inferred from its effects: “Infer then that there is a power in him of which the animal is bereft.” (¶11). Its ¶1 also records a fact about the compiling of the book: “WE HAVE ALREADY had one or two conversations on the subject of the spirit, but they were not written down.”
  • Chapter 59 (“Man’s Comprehension of God”) says that “differences of degree in the world of creation are a barrier to knowledge” (¶6), so God’s essence cannot be known, and that “the knowledge of the Manifestations of God is the knowledge of God” (¶9). This is where the argument of chapter 3 (the educator) and chapter 2 (the Creator) meet: the Creator is known through the educator.
  • Chapter 74 (“On Good and Evil”) develops the privation argument of chapter 2 ¶3–4 into an account of evil as “simply their non-existence” (¶3).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: talks in America, 1912

The talks published in The Promulgation of Universal Peace are compiled from notes and stenographic records made by listeners. Unlike Some Answered Questions, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá did not review them. They are useful as parallels; where they go beyond the authenticated texts, that difference should be stated.

  • New York, 15 April 1912. On His conversations in Paris: “Most of the scholars, professors and learned men proved to be materialists.” The talk lists man’s inventions and concludes: “Man has intelligence; nature has not. Man has volition; nature has none.” It states the part-and-whole principle of chapter 1 ¶6: “this is equivalent to claiming that a part may be endowed with qualities which are absent in the whole.”
  • Chicago, 2 May 1912. The closest parallel to chapters 1–2 as a pair. It agrees with the materialists that nature has no will, asks “Is it conceivable that a drop should be imbued with qualities of which the ocean is completely deprived?”, and then makes the argument from making with an object in the room: “Consider the lady beside me who is writing in this little book. … These words have not written themselves, and these letters have not come together of their own volition. It is evident there must be a writer.” It also tells of a materialist philosopher in Alexandria who said, “I see no difference between myself and the donkey”.
  • St Paul, 20 September 1912. The closest parallel to chapter 3: “The materialists hold to the opinion that the world of nature is complete. The divine philosophers declare that the world of nature is incomplete.” It gives the same three kinds of education (physical, intellectual, spiritual) and concludes: “For this reason the holy, divine Manifestations are the first Teachers and Educators of humanity”.
  • Stanford University, 8 October 1912 (the “discourse” that the Tablet to Forel says philosophers praised). It joins the language of Darwinism to chapter 3: “In nature there is the law of the survival of the fittest. … The holy, heavenly Manifestations of God have been the Teachers. They are the divine Gardeners Who transform the jungles of human nature into fruitful orchards and make the thorny places blossom as the rose.” The same talk contains the sentence “The lost link of Darwinian theory is itself a proof that man is not an animal. … It will never be found.” That claim is about the fossil record, and many hominin fossils have been found since 1912. See objection 2 below for how the guidance of the Universal House of Justice bears on statements of this kind.

The Universal House of Justice

Two texts approved by the Universal House of Justice bear directly on how chapters 1–3 meet modern biology.

The Foreword to the 2014 translation says of the chapters on evolution: “His essential argument, then, is not directed towards scientific findings but towards the materialist assertions that are built upon them. For Bahá’ís, the science of evolution is accepted, but the conclusion that humanity is merely an accidental branch of the animal kingdom—with all its attendant social implications—is not.”

A letter written on behalf of the House of Justice on 21 February 2016, answering a question about that paragraph, says:

“In the passages found in Some Answered Questions, as well as in numerous other Tablets and talks, ‘Abdu’l‑Bahá elaborates upon the principle of the harmony of science and religion, observes that human beings and animals have in common the same physical nature, emphasizes that it is the mind and the soul that distinguish humanity, and rejects the idea that human beings are merely animals, a haphazard accident, and captives of nature trapped in the struggle for existence. In light of all such statements, it is possible for a Bahá’í to conclude that one can disagree with the materialistic philosophical interpretation of scientific findings—that man is merely an animal and a random expression of nature—without contesting the scientific findings themselves, such as those in genetics which are incompatible with a concept of “parallel” evolution.”

The same letter asks Bahá’ís to avoid two extremes: “The first is to simply dismiss the truths found in the Revelation owing to a dogmatic attachment to materialistic interpretations of scientific findings. The second is to assume that in every instance where one’s personal understanding of the teachings conflicts with scientific findings, it is these findings that must change in future, for such a posture would place Bahá’ís in the position of constantly contending with science.”

For a discussion of chapters 1–3 with a biologist, this guidance sets the line. The Bahá’í position does not dispute evolution by natural selection as a scientific account. It disputes the further claim that the scientific account is the whole account: that order, consciousness and moral purpose are “merely” products of unguided matter.

The debate these chapters entered

This section is field description from outside sources.

The Islamic and Persian background

The vocabulary of chapter 2 is that of Islamic philosophy and theology, and a Persian listener in 1904 would have recognised its arguments:

  • From temporal origin (ḥudúth). The standard argument of kalám (dialectical theology), developed by al-Ghazálí and others: the world began to exist, and whatever begins to exist has a cause. Chapter 2 does not use this argument, and chapter 47 and the Lawḥ-i-Ḥikmat deny its premise, since they hold that creation has no beginning in time.
  • From contingency (imkán) to the Necessary Existent (wájib al-wujúd). Avicenna’s argument, which does not depend on the world having a beginning: even an eternal world of dependent things needs a being that exists by its own essence. Chapter 2 ¶3–5 uses this vocabulary (imkán, ghaní bi’dh-dhát). Joshua Hall’s study “Bahá’u’lláh and the God of Avicenna” (Journal of Bahá’í Studies 31:3, 2021) argues that Bahá’u’lláh’s statements affirm the principles of Avicenna’s argument.
  • From providence and from invention. Ibn Rushd (Averroes, d. 1198), in al-Kashf ‘an manáhij al-adilla, held that the Qur’an’s own arguments are two: the argument from providence (dalíl al-’ináya), from the fitness of the world for human life, and the argument from invention (dalíl al-ikhtirá’), from the fact that living things are made. These are the closest Islamic parallels to the Western design argument and to chapter 2 ¶6–7.
  • Rational and traditional proofs (’aqlí and naqlí), the distinction chapter 3 ¶17 invokes.

The term “materialists” in Persian and Arabic writing of the period had a specific recent use. Jamál al-Dín al-Afghání’s Persian tract of 1881 against the “nícharís” (from the English word “nature”, that is, “naturalists”), translated into Arabic by Muḥammad ‘Abduh as al-Radd ’alá al-dahriyyín, “The Refutation of the Materialists”, attacked Darwin along with Democritus and treated evolution as a doctrine of blind chance. In Beirut, a speech by Professor Edwin Lewis at the Syrian Protestant College in 1882 that seemed to favour Darwin led to resignations and a long debate in the journal al-Muqtaṭaf. In 1884 Shiblí Shumayyil published an Arabic version of Ludwig Büchner’s materialist lectures on Darwin. Ḥusayn al-Jisr’s al-Risála al-Ḥamídiyya (Beirut, 1887 or 1888) defended creation but said that if evolution were proved, Muslims should accept it and read the Qur’an accordingly, because either view “would suffice Muslims to prove the existence of God”. Keven Brown, whose study is the main source for this paragraph, concludes that “‘Abdu’l-Bahá was familiar with the contemporary debate on this theory in the Arab world” (Brown, “‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Response to Darwinism: Its Historical and Philosophical Context”, in Evolution and Bahá’í Belief, Kalimát Press, 2001, relying on Adel Ziadat, Western Science in the Arab World, 1986).

The design argument in Europe and America

  • Hume (1779). In the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, Cleanthes states the design argument: the world is “one great machine”, its parts adapted to each other as in “the productions of human contrivance”, so its Author must be “somewhat similar to the mind of man”. Philo replies that the analogy is weak, that a designer would need a cause in turn (“why not stop at the material world?”), and that the argument cannot show the designer is perfect or single: the world might be “the first rude essay of some infant deity”.
  • Paley (1802). Natural Theology opens with the watch found on a heath: its parts are “framed and put together for a purpose”, so “the watch must have had a maker”. Paley’s book was a set text at Cambridge, and Darwin later wrote that it had given him “as much delight as did Euclid”.
  • Darwin (1859, 1876). Natural selection gave a mechanism by which adaptations arise without a designer of each. Darwin wrote in his autobiography: “The old argument from design in Nature, as given by Paley, which formerly seemed to me so conclusive, fails, now that the law of natural selection has been discovered.” But the same text says that the impossibility of conceiving the universe “as the result of blind chance or necessity” led him at one time to “a First Cause having an intelligent mind”, and that he ended as an agnostic, not as an atheist.
  • Haeckel (1899; English 1900). The Riddle of the Universe sold widely and presented “monism”: one substance, governed by one “law of substance”, with no God distinct from nature and no design. Haeckel wrote that “the idea of ‘design’ has wholly disappeared” from the inorganic sciences and called Darwin the “Newton of the organic world” whom Kant had declared impossible. In January 1906, during the period of the talks, Haeckel’s followers founded the German Monist League in Jena.
  • The “eclipse of Darwinism” (c. 1900–1910). Around 1904–1906, natural selection itself was in doubt among biologists. Evolution (common descent) was widely accepted, but many doubted that selection was its main cause; Mendel’s laws, rediscovered in 1900, were at first set against Darwin’s gradualism. Vernon Kellogg wrote in 1907 that “the Darwinian selection theories” stood “seriously discredited in the biological world”. The reconciliation of Mendelian genetics and natural selection (the “modern synthesis”) came in the 1930s and 1940s.
  • William James (1902, 1907). James held that proofs of God from the order of nature “to‐day does little more than gather dust in libraries”, that “darwinism has once for all displaced design from the minds of the ‘scientific’”, and that the word “design” taken by itself is “a blank cartridge”. He located religious conviction in experience, which is close to chapter 2 ¶8.

Where chapters 1–3 sit in this debate

Three observations, offered as this article’s reading:

  1. Chapter 1 does not depend on the argument Darwin defeated. Paley’s argument was from particular adaptations. Chapter 1’s argument is from the lawfulness of nature as a whole, and from the presence of consciousness and will. Natural selection operates within natural law and presupposes it; it does not explain it. Chapter 2 ¶5 (contingency) is likewise untouched by biology. The arguments of chapter 2 ¶6–7 (the bread, the boundless universe) are closer to Paley’s and more exposed.
  2. The book takes the materialists’ premise and contests their conclusion. Chapter 1 ¶3 agrees that nature is without awareness or will. That was Haeckel’s premise too. The difference is the next step: for Haeckel, a nature without mind is the whole of reality; for ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, a nature without mind that nonetheless produces minds and obeys exact law points beyond itself.
  3. Chapter 3 moves the question from biology to history. The argument for the educator does not depend on any claim about how species arose. It rests on observable facts about human beings (they need education) and on a historical claim about the effect of the founders of religions, which can be examined “fairly”, as ¶12 asks.

Objections a modern evolutionary biologist would raise

Each item gives the objection as a careful biologist would put it, what the texts say, and what is left open. The responses are drawn from the texts and from Bahá’í scholarship; they are not the only possible ones.

1. Order needs no orderer. Natural laws plus selection produce all the order we see.

The objection. Snowflakes, crystals and galaxies form by physical law with no designer. Living order arises by variation and natural selection, a process with no foresight. Richard Dawkins’s The Blind Watchmaker (1986) is the standard statement: the watchmaker of biology is natural selection, and it is blind. So chapter 1 ¶5 and chapter 2 ¶7 are answered.

What the texts say. Chapter 1 accepts that nature produces order by law; its question is about the source of the law (¶5, ¶7). Chapter 47 says beings arise “from one natural order” and that this order is the “wisdom of God”. The Foreword and the 2016 letter accept the science of evolution. So the dispute is narrower than it looks: not whether selection explains adaptation, but whether “laws acting around us”, in Darwin’s phrase, need an explanation themselves.

What is left open. A biologist may reply that “why are there laws at all” is a question for physics or philosophy, not biology, and that no answer is needed or available. The text’s answer, that lawful order without understanding must be “in the grasp of” an understanding, is a philosophical claim. It is not refuted by biology, and biology does not prove it. Modern versions of the question (the “fine-tuning” of physical constants for life; the fact that the world is intelligible to mathematics) are live in the philosophy of physics, with the multiverse hypothesis as the main naturalistic reply.

2. The branch can have what the root lacks. Consciousness emerged.

The objection. Chapter 1 ¶6 and the Tablet to Forel say the part cannot have perfections the whole lacks. But science is full of properties of wholes that their parts lack (wetness is not a property of a water molecule; life is not a property of a carbon atom), and of later stages that have what earlier stages lacked. Eyes evolved in lineages that had none. On this view consciousness and will emerged in nervous systems from matter that has neither.

What the texts say. The principle in ¶6 is about cause and effect, not about wholes and parts in the chemist’s sense: the “root” is nature as the cause of man. The Tablet to Forel and the Chicago talk apply it in that sense. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá does not deny that the human body developed over time; chapter 47 says it did. Chapters 47–50 hold that the distinctively human capacity, which the Foreword calls “a capacity for rational consciousness”, “is not a product of the evolutionary process, but exists potentially in creation”. So the claim is not that the human form could not develop gradually but that what is realised in it was latent from the start in the order of creation.

What is left open. Whether consciousness can be explained by physical processes is unresolved in philosophy of mind (the “hard problem”, as David Chalmers named it in 1995). Some secular philosophers, such as Thomas Nagel in Mind and Cosmos (2012), argue that mind cannot be explained by current physical science; most neuroscientists expect it can. The Bahá’í argument needs the first position, and a biologist will hold the second. The fair statement is that this is a disagreement about an open question, not a case of science against religion. On the “missing link” sentence in the Stanford talk of 1912: it is a claim about fossils in notes of a talk that ‘Abdu’l-Bahá did not review, and the 2016 letter asks Bahá’ís not to assume that scientific findings “must change” to fit a personal reading. The episode should not defend that sentence as a statement about fossils.

3. The maker must be more perfect than the work? Evolution builds complex from simple.

The objection. Chapter 2 ¶1–2 says “an active creator must possess all perfections to produce his handiwork” and that “the painting cannot be like the painter”. In biology, simple ancestors give rise to complex descendants all the time. Effects regularly exceed their causes in complexity.

What the texts say. The argument is about the ultimate cause, not about the process by which things develop. Within nature, chapter 47 accepts development from simple to complex (“the original matter, which is like unto the embryo”). The claim of chapter 2 is that the whole process, including its capacity to produce complexity, has a cause with at least the perfections found in its results. Again, the Tablet to Forel limits what is claimed: the perfections ascribed to God are “only in order to deny imperfections”.

What is left open. Whether the principle “the cause contains the perfections of the effect” is true is a question in metaphysics, held by the Aristotelian and Islamic traditions and rejected by most modern naturalists. The episode can state both views; it cannot settle them.

4. Weakness does not prove an all-powerful being.

The objection. Chapter 2 ¶3–5: weakness implies power, ignorance implies knowledge, sickness implies health. A biologist (or a philosopher) will say that this proves only that some things are stronger than others, or that the concept of power is prior to the concept of weakness. It does not prove that there is a being of unlimited power.

What the texts say. The text moves from relative to absolute in ¶5, through dependency: everything in the world depends on something else, so there must be “One Who in His own Essence is independent of all things”. The Tablet to Forel adds that “the limited is known through the unlimited”. The privation argument is best read together with the contingency argument, not on its own.

What is left open. The objection has force against ¶3–4 taken alone. The contingency argument in ¶5 is the stronger form, and it is contested on different grounds (see 6).

5. From the bread to the universe is a fallacy.

The objection. Chapter 2 ¶6–7 reasons from “this piece of bread attests that it has a maker” to the universe. But we know bread has a maker because we have seen bakers make bread. We have never seen universes made. Hume made this point in 1779. Reasoning from a property of parts to the same property of the whole is the “fallacy of composition”.

What the texts say. Paragraph 7 argues from the change of form (“The change in the outward form of the smallest thing proves the existence of a creator”) rather than from experience of makers. The underlying principle is causal: every change has a cause. Paragraph 8 then says plainly that these are “theoretical arguments adduced for weak souls”, that is, arguments offered to help, not demonstrations on which faith depends.

What is left open. This is the argument in the three chapters most exposed to Hume’s criticism. The script should not present it as the strongest argument. It is the most vivid one.

6. Who made God? And why not stop at the universe?

The objection. If everything needs a cause, so does God. If God needs no cause, why not say the universe needs none? This is Philo’s question in Hume (“why not stop at the material world?”) and a standard modern reply.

What the texts say. The texts do not say everything needs a cause. They say everything dependent (contingent) needs a cause, and that a chain of dependent causes cannot go on without end (Tablet to Forel). What ends the chain is “Self-Dependent”, independent “in His own Essence”. The universe, on this account, is not self-dependent, because each of its parts depends on others: “every being hath come to exist under numerous influences and continually undergoeth reaction” (Tablet to Forel).

What is left open. Whether an infinite chain of dependent causes is impossible, and whether the universe as a whole could be the self-dependent thing, are both disputed by philosophers. This is the classical debate over the cosmological argument, and it has not been settled. A guest can reasonably say he is not persuaded; a host can reasonably say the objection “who made God?” misstates the argument.

7. “Arguments for weak souls” sounds like a retreat into private feeling.

The objection. Chapter 2 ¶8 says that with inner vision “a hundred thousand clear proofs will be seen”, and that arguments are for those “deprived of the grace of the spirit”. A sceptic hears this as: if the arguments fail, faith is safe anyway, and those who are not persuaded lack something.

What the texts say. The analogy is to self-awareness: no one argues himself into knowing he has a mind. The claim is that knowledge of God, for those who have it, is like that, and that arguments serve others. Chapter 3 balances this: “rational arguments … are what the people of the world require in this day” (¶2), and the proofs are “rational and not … traditional” (¶17). The Tablet to Forel lists the ways a person comes to believe in God: “by his reasoning power, by observation, by his intuitive faculties and the revealing power of his faith”.

What is left open. Whether inner experience counts as evidence for anyone other than the person who has it is a standing question in philosophy of religion (James’s Varieties, 1902, is the classic treatment). A fair script lets the guest say that, for him, it does not.

8. Culture, not revelation, explains human progress.

The objection. Chapter 3 is right that human beings need education and that a person raised alone would lack language and most skills. But human culture is cumulative: each generation learns from the last and adds a little. Researchers in cultural evolution (Michael Tomasello, Joseph Henrich and others) explain human achievement by this process, without any teacher of more than human power.

What the texts say. Chapter 3 agrees on the first two kinds of education: material and human education are real and are passed on by ordinary teachers, and the divine educator promotes science and “learning, discoveries, and major undertakings” (¶10). The claim specific to chapter 3 is about the third kind, “divine education” (¶7), and about the historical effect of particular persons: “One sanctified Soul revives the world of humanity” (¶12). That is a claim that the major moral and religious changes in history start with individuals whose effect is out of proportion to their means.

What is left open. Whether the historical effect of Moses, Christ or Muḥammad needs more than human explanation is a judgment about history, not a scientific finding. A historian can explain the spread of Christianity by social causes; a believer can reply that those causes are themselves what needs explaining. The text asks only that the matter be examined “fairly”.

9. Christianity did not unite the nations; it divided them.

The objection. Chapter 3 ¶13 says Christ “raised the banner of peace and amity” and united Italy, France, Germany, Russia and England. A sceptic will list the wars of religion and the divisions of the churches.

What the texts say. The text raises this objection itself, twice: “divisions broke out again among them … after a long while the governments caused the resurgence of discord” (¶13), and “Although after a long while the governments disrupted this unity, Christ had indeed accomplished His task” (¶14). The claim is that Christ’s teaching produced a fellowship across peoples (most clearly among the early Christians, “until the days of Constantine”) that no government had produced, and that later divisions came from “differing motives” and from governments.

What is left open. How much of later Christian conflict can be laid to governments rather than churches is a historical question on which people will differ.

10. The sentence about the Sudan is wrong and offensive.

See the note under chapter 3 ¶3–4. The fair response concedes that the sentence repeats a belief common in 1904 and widely questioned today, and states the point of the argument, which is about education and applies to all people equally.
