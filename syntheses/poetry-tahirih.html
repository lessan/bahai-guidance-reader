Ṭáhirih (Qurratu’l-‘Ayn): the poet

Last updated 17 October 2026

Ṭáhirih was a Persian woman poet and one of the Letters of the Living, the first disciples of the Báb. She was killed in Ṭihrán in 1852. She is not one of the Central Figures of the Bahá’í Faith, and her poems are not scripture. Shoghi Effendi and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá honour her as a heroine of the Faith and say that she wrote verse. They do not quote any of her poems, and they do not vouch for any particular poem as hers. Her poetry is therefore treated here as historical and literary material, to be read with the scholarship that discusses it. The series hub is Poetry in the Writings and by Bahá’í poets.

Two things make her poetry difficult to use. Few of her poems exist in English, and the authorship of the best-known ones is disputed. This page sets out what the Writings say about her as a poet, the English texts that are in the public domain, what scholars report, and which attributions are unsettled.

The poet

What the Writings say. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes her work after she accepted the Báb’s message in Karbilá. In Memorials of the Faithful (translated by Marzieh Gail) He says:

“She translated and expounded “The Best of Stories,” also writing in Persian and Arabic, composing odes and lyrics, and humbly practicing her devotions, performing even those that were optional and supernumerary.”

(Memorials of the Faithful 69.8; authorised translation)

This is the only statement in these sources by ‘Abdu’l-Bahá that she composed verse. He does not quote a poem.

Shoghi Effendi introduces her in God Passes By among the Letters of the Living:

“A poetess, less than thirty years of age, of distinguished birth, of bewitching charm, of captivating eloquence, indomitable in spirit, unorthodox in her views, audacious in her acts, immortalized as Ṭáhirih (the Pure One) by the “Tongue of Glory,” and surnamed Qurratu’l-‘Ayn (Solace of the Eyes) by Siyyid Káẓim, her teacher”

(GPB I.7; authorised translation)

Later he quotes tributes from outside the Faith. One is from Lord Curzon, who calls her “the lovely but ill-fated poetess of Qazvín”, and another from the Turkish poet Sulaymán Náẓim Bey (GPB V.20). These tributes are about her as a person; they do not describe her verse.

The odes on the road to Mázindarán. The Dawn-Breakers (Nabíl’s narrative, translated and edited by Shoghi Effendi) says that after the gathering at Badasht the companions set out for Mázindarán:

”On their way, Táhirih each day composed an ode which she instructed those who accompanied her to chant as they followed her howdah.“

(The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XVI; authorised translation)

None of these odes is reproduced in the book, and none can be identified with a surviving poem. Nothing in the sources connects any particular poem to this journey, so a lyric must not say that a given poem was composed on it.

What scholars report. Scholars are cited here as scholarship, not as authority.

  • E. G. Browne printed three poems of hers, with versified translations, in Materials for the Study of the Bábí Religion (Cambridge, 1918). He says that her fame as a poetess is considerable but that he knows “only two other poems commonly ascribed to her”. These are two ghazals in the Kámil metre, which he found “very fine” though marred by incorrect Arabic phrases.
  • Amin Banani (editor and translator), Táhirih: A Portrait in Poetry (Kalimat Press, 2004), and his essay “Táhirih: A Portrait in Poetry” in the Journal of Bahá’í Studies 10:1–2 (2000), whose abstract says the aim is “to allow her own voice, through her poems, to speak for herself”. Copyrighted.
  • John S. Hatcher and Amrollah Hemmat, The Poetry of Táhirih (George Ronald, 2002), Adam’s Wish (2008) and The Quickening: Unknown Poetry of Tahirih (2011). A reviewer of the last says the authors translated forty-two of her poems into English. Copyrighted.
  • Sahba Shayani, “Literary Imitation in Three Poems Attributed to Tahirih Qurrat al-ʿAyn”, Hawwa 21:4 (2023), argues that three of the best-known poems attributed to her are istiqbál (imitations of earlier poems by Rumi, Hátif and Jámí).
  • Franklin Lewis has also treated some of the attributed poems as taẓmín (a poet’s incorporation of an earlier poet’s lines), according to the Persian Wikipedia article on her poems.
  • Anthony Lee, in Religions (2023), examines her writings for her social, religious and political beliefs, most of which, the library’s abstract says, “broke with Islam’s traditional theology”.

Only the Browne volume and Martha Root’s article are available as full text in the library. The other works are cited from their abstracts, a review and secondary mentions.

The text in English

Browne’s versified translations (1918, public domain)

Browne’s versions are old and loose. He says that he made “a few trifling alterations” of earlier printings. They are in the public domain, so they may be printed in a published lyric (`ok`). Credit E. G. Browne, 1918. They are not a precise guide to the Persian, and they are not by a Bahá’í translator or approved by the Bahá’í institutions.

Ghazal 1. Browne adds a note: “This poem is presumably addressed to the Báb.” It begins:

”The thralls of yearning love constrain in the bonds of pain and calamity

These broken-hearted lovers of thine to yield their lives in their zeal for thee“

It goes on to the beloved who comes to the speaker in a dream at the break of day, and then to the speaker’s reproach of a worldly listener:

”With you, who contemn both love and wine for the hermit’s cell and the zealot’s shrine,

What can I do, for our Faith divine you hold as a thing of infamy?“

It ends with a call to give up the self:

”Pass from the station of “I” and “We,” and choose for thy home Nonentity,

For when thou has done the like of this, thou shalt reach the supreme Felicity.“

Ghazal 2. Browne calls it the second of the two ghazals. It opens with the beloved’s face appearing and the Qur’anic question “Am I not your Lord?”:

”The effulgence of thy face flashed forth and the rays of thy visage arose on high;

Then speak the word, “Am I not your Lord?” and “Thou art, thou art!” we will all reply.“

Its central image is calamity gathering at the gate of the heart:

”Thy trumpet-call “Am I not?” to greet how loud the drums of affliction beat!

At the gates of my heart there tramp the feet and camp the hosts of calamity.“

The beloved then laughs at “the hail of woe” and cries out “The Martyr of Karbalá am I!”, and later:

”What harm if thou with the fire of amaze should’st set my Sinai-heart ablaze“

The poem ends with her name, in the third person:

”Sit mute like Táhira, hearkening to the whale of “No” and its ceaseless sigh.“

Full text of both ghazals: [source], and Browne, “Selected Poems by Qurratu’l-‘Ayn, Nabíl and other Bábís”, pp. 348–352 ([source]).

Martha Root’s translations (1941, © Bahá’í International Community)

Martha Root quotes two items in “Táhirih’s Message to the Modern World” (The Bahá’í World, vol. 8, 1941). The library file carries the notice “© BIC, public sharing permitted”. Quote only short extracts and credit the translation; for a published lyric, ask the Bahá’í International Community (`ok-with-credit` at most, to be checked).

The first is a verse reply that, in Root’s account, Ṭáhirih wrote on the back of a letter from Náṣiri’d-Dín Sháh. The Sháh had offered her an honoured place if she gave up her ideas. Root gives it as:

”Kingdom, wealth and ruling be for thee, Wandering, becoming a poor dervish and calamity be for me.“

The second is a single line that Root links to her death: “At the gates of my heart I behold the feet and the tents of hosts of calamity.” It is the same image as in Browne’s ghazal 2.

Root’s story of the Sháh’s offer appears in her article but not in the Writings or in Browne. It should be presented as Root’s account, with the verse as Root’s rendering of a poem whose Persian text and source the article does not give.

Hatcher and Hemmat, Banani and others (copyrighted)

The English texts in the three Hatcher and Hemmat volumes and in Banani’s book are not in the library, and they may be quoted in a published lyric only with permission (`permission-needed`). The reviewer of The Quickening quotes two couplets, for example “For you there should be detachment / that you might discern the (one) hidden Countenance now dawned in the world”, to show the translators’ plain style. A review quotation is not a source for a lyric.

Machine-drafted translations

Four poems whose Persian text is saved in the library (`sources/web/poetry/originals/`) are being translated. A machine-drafted provisional translation (Gemini 4 Argon, 2026-10-17), not reviewed by a translator, is kept in the library for each of them: `kb/library/provisional-translations/tahirih-gar-ba-to-oftadam-nazar.md`, `tahirih-saqi-eshqat-ey-sanam.md`, `tahirih-bikhalq-i-jahan-saqia-bisharat.md` and `tahirih-dar-rah-i-eshqat-ey-sanam.md`. They are not quoted here, and their attribution to Ṭáhirih is disputed (see below).

Themes and images

The two Browne ghazals and the Root lines share a small set of themes.

  • The Beloved’s appearance and the speaker’s surrender. In ghazal 1 the beloved visits “at the break of day” and the speaker is “well content” with the beloved’s severity. In ghazal 2 the appearance of the beloved’s face leads at once to the confession “Thou art, thou art!”. Browne’s note reads the first as addressed to the Báb.
  • Calamity as a sign of love. “The hosts of calamity” camp at the heart’s gate in ghazal 2. In Root’s verse, the poor dervish and calamity are what she chooses, and the throne is left to the Sháh.
  • Contempt for the learned and the pious. Ghazal 1 sets the poet against the listener who prefers “the hermit’s cell and the zealot’s shrine”. The speaker takes the Qalandar’s habit, the dervish who has given up reputation, and leaves “Sikandar’s pomp” (Alexander’s splendour) to the other.
  • Self-effacement. Ghazal 1 ends “Pass from the station of ‘I’ and ‘We’”, and ghazal 2 ends with the poet telling herself, by name, to be silent.
  • Martyrdom. “The Martyr of Karbalá am I!” identifies the lover with Imám Ḥusayn. Whoever reads the poem in the light of Ṭáhirih’s death will hear a double meaning, but the poem itself does not refer to her death, and the sources do not date it.

What is not settled

Which poems are hers. Browne himself hedges: the two ghazals are “commonly ascribed” to her. He prints a third poem, a Persian mathnawí (Sáqí-náma), which he received in manuscript from Shaykh Aḥmad Rúḥí of Kirmán, who told him she composed it. Browne writes that he is “inclined to credit” this claim without being able to “guarantee” it. He also prints a poem in the same metre and rhyme as ghazal 1 that is “sometimes ascribed to Qurratu’l-‘Ayn, but more often, and with greater probability, to Nabíl of Zarand”. Browne also prints an ode in praise of Bahá’u’lláh that he assigns to Nabíl; a lyric should not give it to her.

“Gar be tu uftadam nazar.” The ghazal beginning gar bih tu uftádam nazar, chihra bih chihra rú bih rú (“If I should meet you face to face”) is described by the Persian Wikipedia article as one of the best-known ghazals attributed to her. Its attribution is disputed, as shown by the notes on the saved Persian texts, which come from the Persian Wikipedia article on her poems:

  • It cites Naṣru’lláh Muḥammad-Ḥusayní, a Bahá’í author, who holds that it is most probably by Ṭáhir Káshání.
  • It cites Muḥít Ṭabáṭabá’í, who holds that the ghazal is by Ṭáhirih al-Anjudání Káshí, of the middle of the tenth century of the Hijra (the sixteenth century CE), and who records a mukhammas (five-line stanza form) built on it by Mírzá Váḥid Qazvíní.
  • It notes that Franklin Lewis treats such poems as taẓmín.

A version with a different opening, sáqí-i ‘ishqat ay ṣanam, is given as that mukhammas by Mírzá Váḥid Qazvíní and closes with the name “Ṭáhirá”. Both forms share the refrain couplet and the closing phrase parda bih parda tu bih tu (“veil upon veil, you upon you”), and the saved Persian texts differ in their stanzas.

A further piece of evidence comes from a Bahá’í source. The Afnan Library holds a typescript of the díván of Sayyid Muṣṭafá Ṭabáṭabá’í Samnání, who writes under the pen-name “Sayyid”. The poem appears in it as a mukhammas (inba_v093, pp. 61–62, lines 1182–1185 and 1204–1209 of the library text). It contains the refrain couplet “gar bi tu uftadam nazar, chihra bih chihra rú bih rú / sharḥ dihim gham-i tu rá nukta bih nukta mú bih mú”, and it also contains the poet’s own pen-name (“Sayyid”) in the penultimate stanza, with “Ṭáhira” in the last. The typescript does not attribute the poem to Ṭáhirih. This shows that the poem circulated in Bahá’í hands in the twentieth century in a form that carries a different poet’s pen-name. It does not show who wrote the refrain, because a mukhammas may build on another poet’s lines. The library text is a machine transcription of the scan, and the typescript’s own text of the stanzas differs from the Wikipedia version, so no firm conclusion about the original poem follows.

Practical rule: do not write that Ṭáhirih composed “Gar be tu uftadam nazar” without “attributed to” or “said to be by”.

The other attributed poems. The Persian Wikipedia article quotes a Persian scholar who is said to confirm dar rah-i ‘ishqat ay ṣanam as surely hers, but the article gives no corroboration, and other poems on the same page are marked disputed. The excerpt bih khalq-i jahán sáqiá dih navíd is given there as verses Browne printed, and the article supplies only part of the poem. Shayani’s three poems (Ay ‘áshiqán, Ay bi sar-i zulf-i tu, Jadhabát shawqik) are not saved in the library; Shayani argues that they are imitations of Rumi, Hátif and Jámí. An imitation can still be the poet’s own work, but it makes the poem a response to an earlier one rather than an original statement.

Dates. None of the poems in the sources is dated. The only dated activity is the daily ode-making on the road to Mázindarán, and no text of those odes is identified. Root says she was martyred at thirty-six, while Shoghi Effendi calls her “less than thirty years of age” in his portrait of her among the Letters of the Living. The sources read here do not reconcile the two statements.

The Sháh’s offer. Root’s verse reply is not in Browne or the Writings, and the article does not name the source of the poem.

For songwriting

These are notes for a lyric writer, not lyrics.

Exact phrases that can be sung (5–15 words). All are from Browne (1918, public domain, `ok`, credit E. G. Browne) unless marked.

  • ”The effulgence of thy face flashed forth“ (ghazal 2, line 1)
  • ”At the gates of my heart there tramp the feet and camp the hosts of calamity“ (ghazal 2; Root’s variant is `ok-with-credit`)
  • ”Sit mute like Táhira, hearkening to the whale of “No”“ (ghazal 2, last couplet)
  • ”Pass from the station of “I” and “We,”“ (ghazal 1, near the end)
  • ”Then speak the word, “Am I not your Lord?” and “Thou art, thou art!”“ (ghazal 2)
  • ”The Martyr of Karbalá am I!“ (ghazal 2; take care, because it makes the speaker the martyred Imám)

From ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, in Gail’s authorised translation (`ok-with-credit`): ”composing odes and lyrics“. This phrase is about her, not by her.

Concrete images. Dawn after a night of waiting; the beloved’s face as light; drums of affliction; an army camped at the heart’s gate; a dervish’s patched robe set against Alexander’s court; a howdah on a mountain road with a chorus of followers chanting an ode that is new each day (this last scene comes from The Dawn-Breakers, not from a poem).

A possible arc. Ghazal 2 gives one: the face appears; the speaker answers “Thou art”; calamity arrives and is welcomed; the beloved is martyred and mourned; the speaker ends in silence. Ghazal 1 gives another: a dream at dawn; defiance of the pious; renunciation of “I” and “We”.

What a lyric must not claim.

  • It must not call her poems scripture or revelation, or present them as the words of the Báb or Bahá’u’lláh.
  • It must not state that “Gar be tu uftadam nazar” is certainly by Ṭáhirih, or that she wrote any particular poem on the road to Mázindarán or on the night of her death.
  • It must not give her Nabíl’s ode, or quote a Hatcher and Hemmat, Banani or machine-drafted translation as if it were a settled text.
  • It must not claim that ghazal 1 was written to the Báb. Browne says only that it is “presumably addressed” to Him.
  • It must not put Root’s verse reply in her mouth as a historical quotation without saying that Root is the source.
