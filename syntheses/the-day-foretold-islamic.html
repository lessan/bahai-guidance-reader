The Day Foretold in Islám: the Day of God, the Qá’im and the Return of Jesus

Last updated 17 October 2026

This page is part of the series the-day-foretold. Related pages: promises-of-the-future, the-day-foretold-hindu, the-day-foretold-jewish, the-day-foretold-zoroastrian, the-day-foretold-buddhist, the-day-foretold-christian, the-day-foretold-babi.

What this page covers

The Qur’án speaks of a Day of God, an Hour, a Resurrection and a Judgment. Muslim tradition adds expectations that are not in the Qur’án itself. Shí‘ih Muslims await the Qá’im (He who ariseth) and the return of the Imám Ḥusayn. Sunní Muslims await the Mihdí and the return of Jesus. Shoghi Effendi names what Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is to each:

“…to Christendom Christ returned ‘in the glory of the Father,’ to Shí‘ah Islám the return of the Imám Ḥusayn; to Sunní Islám the descent of the ‘Spirit of God’ (Jesus Christ)…” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.11)

He names the Báb, in the same book, as “the promised Qá’im (He who ariseth), the Ṣáḥibu’z-Zamán (the Lord of the Age)” (GPB I.2) and as “the ‘Qá’im’ (He Who ariseth) promised to the Shí‘ahs, the ‘Mihdí’ (One Who is guided) awaited by the Sunnís” (GPB IV.17). The fulfilment is therefore twofold, through the Báb and through Bahá’u’lláh. Shoghi Effendi quotes ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “All the peoples of the world… are awaiting two Manifestations, Who must be contemporaneous; all wait for the fulfillment of this promise” (GPB VI.7).

  • The Qur’án. Quotations are from J. M. Rodwell’s translation (1861, public domain), saved at [source], which holds suras 2, 4, 7, 13, 14, 32, 33, 39, 43, 50, 55, 81, 82 and 89 only. Rodwell’s text has no verse numbers, so references use the standard numbering, located by content. For verses from other suras (for example 17, 18, 25, 29, 44, 54, 78) the page has only the rendering that Bahá’u’lláh or Shoghi Effendi quotes. Where the Writings quote a verse that is also in Rodwell, both renderings are given; the Writings’ rendering is the authorised one.
  • Traditions (ḥadíth). Sayings attributed to Muḥammad and, in Shí‘ih tradition, to the Imáms. The Qá’im, the Mihdí, the signs that accompany them and the return of Ḥusayn come mainly from these. The page does not quote the collections. It gives the traditions as Bahá’u’lláh quotes them in the Kitáb-i-Íqán and the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, and says what He does with them (section 2).
  • The Writings and Shoghi Effendi. The Báb, Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá and Shoghi Effendi, in the authorised translations of the Bahá’í Reference Library. Provisional translations by Adib Masumian and Todd Lawson are marked as provisional wherever they appear. History and Bahá’í commentary come in their own sections, labelled; they are not authoritative interpretation.

Sections 1 and 2 give the Qur’án and the traditions. Section 3 gives the Báb as the Qá’im. Section 4 gives the Writings’ statements of fulfilment. Sections 5 and 6 give Bahá’í commentary and history. Sections 7 to 10 cover music, open questions, songwriting and sources.

1. The promise: the Qur’án

Each theme below gives the Qur’án first. Where Bahá’u’lláh or Shoghi Effendi quotes the same verse, the Bahá’í text follows with its address, and then what the Writings say the verse means. The Kitáb-i-Íqán does not say that these verses are untrue. It says what their terms refer to, and it states at the outset that “sovereignty, wealth, life, death, judgment and resurrection” are terms that “are not what this generation hath conceived and vainly imagined” (KI 114, quoted in full in section 2).

The Day of God and the coming of the Lord

“What can such expect but that God should come down to them overshadowed with clouds, and the angels also, and their doom be sealed? And to God shall all things return.” (Qur’án 2:210, Rodwell)

“…thy Lord shall come and the angels rank on rank” (Qur’án 89:22, Rodwell)

Bahá’u’lláh quotes the first of these verses in the Kitáb-i-Íqán, and also says how the Imáms read it:

“In like manner, the Imáms, those unquenchable lights of divine guidance, have interpreted the verse: ‘What can such expect but that God should come down to them overshadowed with clouds,‘—a sign which they have unquestionably regarded as one of the features of the Day of Resurrection—as referring to the Qá’im and His manifestation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 152)

“These ‘clouds’ signify, in one sense, the annulment of laws, the abrogation of former Dispensations, the repeal of rituals and customs current amongst men, the exalting of the illiterate faithful above the learned opposers of the Faith. In another sense, they mean the appearance of that immortal Beauty in the image of mortal man, with such human limitations as eating and drinking, poverty and riches, glory and abasement, sleeping and waking, and such other things as cast doubt in the minds of men, and cause them to turn away.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 79)

“These are the ‘clouds’ that cause the heavens of the knowledge and understanding of all that dwell on earth to be cloven asunder. Even as He hath revealed: ‘On that day shall the heaven be cloven by the clouds.‘ Even as the clouds prevent the eyes of men from beholding the sun, so do these things hinder the souls of men from recognizing the light of the divine Luminary.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 80, with Qur’án 25:25)

Shoghi Effendi lists these verses among those in which, in the Bahá’í understanding, Muḥammad alluded to Bahá’u’lláh’s Day:

“To Him Muḥammad, the Apostle of God, had alluded in His Book as the ‘Great Announcement,’ and declared His Day to be the Day whereon ‘God’ will ‘come down’ ‘overshadowed with clouds,’ the Day whereon ‘thy Lord shall come and the angels rank on rank,’…” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.17)

“The appearance of His Manifestation is mentioned explicitly in the Qurʼán, without any need for commentary or interpretation: ‘And thy Lord shall come and the angels rank on rank’” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, provisional translation by Adib Masumian, “A Tablet from ‘Abdu’l-Bahá regarding the Twelfth Imám”)

The Hour

The Qur’án says that the time of the Hour is known to God alone, and that it will come suddenly.

“They will ask thee of the Hour-for what time is its coming fixed? SAY: The knowledge of it is only with my Lord… not otherwise than on a sudden will it come on you.” (Qur’án 7:187, Rodwell)

The Íqán quotes a verse of the same kind and says that, in it, Gabriel was unveiling the meaning of the terms “resurrection,” “judgment,” “paradise” and “hell”:

“Thus at the hour when Muḥammad, that divine Beauty, purposed to unveil one of the mysteries hidden in the symbolic terms ‘resurrection,’ ‘judgment,’ ‘paradise,’ and ‘hell,’ Gabriel, the Voice of Inspiration, was heard saying: ‘Erelong will they wag their heads at Thee, and say, “When shall this be?” Say: “Perchance it is nigh.”‘ The implications of this verse alone suffice the peoples of the world, were they to ponder it in their hearts.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 123, with Qur’án 17:51)

In the Tablet to Pope Pius IX Bahá’u’lláh writes: “The Hour that was concealed within the knowledge of God hath struck” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, SLH 1.107). His answer to the question “Hath the Hour come?” is in section 4.

The days of God

“Of old did we send Moses with our signs: and said to him, ‘Bring forth thy people from the darkness into the light, and remind them of the days of God.‘” (Qur’án 14:5, Rodwell)

The Báb uses the same phrase when He writes of the Revelation He brings: “My Revelation is indeed far more bewildering than that of Muḥammad, the Apostle of God, if thou dost but pause to reflect upon the days of God” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 13.35). Bahá’u’lláh uses the phrase in the Gleanings, in a tablet to a believer: “Render thanks unto God for having sent down unto thee this Tablet in this Prison, that thou mayest remind the people of the days of thy Lord, the All-Glorious, the All-Knowing” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XVIII.9).

The trumpet and the two blasts

“And there shall be a blast on the trumpet, and all who are in the Heavens and all who are in the Earth shall expire, save those whom God shall vouchsafe to live. Then shall there be another blast on it, and lo! arising they shall gaze around them: And the earth shall shine with the light of her Lord, and the Book shall be set, and the prophets shall be brought up, and the witnesses; and judgment shall be given between them with equity; and none shall be wronged” (Qur’án 39:68–69, Rodwell)

“And there shall be a blast on the trumpet,-it is the threatened day!” (Qur’án 50:20, Rodwell)

The Íqán quotes 50:20, and gives its own rendering:

“‘And there was a blast on the trumpet—lo! it is the threatened Day! And every soul is summoned to a reckoning—with him an impeller and a witness.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 122)

“Nay, by ‘trumpet’ is meant the trumpet-call of Muḥammad’s Revelation, which was sounded in the heart of the universe, and by ‘resurrection’ is meant His own rise to proclaim the Cause of God. He bade the erring and wayward arise and speed out of the sepulchers of their bodies, arrayed them with the beauteous robe of faith, and quickened them with the breath of a new and wondrous life.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 123)

Bahá’u’lláh adds that “the Day of Resurrection was ushered in through the Revelation of Muḥammad” (KI 124).

The earth His handful and the heaven cleft

“But they have not deemed of God as is His due; for on the resurrection day the whole Earth shall be but his handful, and in his right hand shall the Heavens be folded together. Praise be to Him! and high be He uplifted above the partners they join with Him!” (Qur’án 39:67, Rodwell)

The Íqán takes up two of these pictures: the heaven cloven (82:1, “WHEN the Heaven shall CLEAVE asunder” in Rodwell) and the earth in His handful (39:67).

“By ‘heaven’ is meant the heaven of divine Revelation, which is elevated with every Manifestation, and rent asunder with every subsequent one. By ‘cloven asunder’ is meant that the former Dispensation is superseded and annulled.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 46, on Qur’án 82:1)

“By the term ‘earth’ is meant the earth of understanding and knowledge, and by ‘heavens’ the heavens of divine Revelation. Reflect thou, how, in one hand, He hath, by His mighty grasp, turned the earth of knowledge and understanding, previously unfolded, into a mere handful, and, on the other, spread out a new and highly exalted earth in the hearts of men.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 51, on Qur’án 39:67)

The Great Announcement

Sura 78 of the Qur’án asks about the Great Announcement. Rodwell’s saved text does not include it, so the rendering here is the one Bahá’u’lláh gives in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, where He sets it beside the Day of the Lord in Joel:

“Joel saith: ‘For the Day of the Lord is great and very terrible; and who can abide it?‘… Likewise, in the Qur’án He saith: ‘Of what ask they of one another? Of the Great Announcement.‘ This is the Announcement, the greatness of which hath been mentioned in most of the Books of old and of more recent times. This is the Announcement that hath caused the limbs of mankind to quake, except such as God, the Protector, the Helper, the Succorer, hath willed to exempt.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 207, with Qur’án 78:1–2)

The same title is applied in the Writings to both the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh. Shoghi Effendi records it among the titles the Báb gave Himself, “the ‘Great Announcement’” (GPB IV.17), and among the titles of Bahá’u’lláh (GPB VI.12). Bahá’u’lláh calls Himself “the Revealer of this Most Great Announcement” in the Bishárát (Glad-Tidings) (Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh, TB 3.19), and in another tablet writes of “this Great Announcement before which, as soon as it shone forth, every momentous announcement bowed low in adoration” (TB 17.62).

The Crier and the Summoner

“And list for the day whereon the crier shall cry from a place near to every one alike: The day on which men shall in truth hear that shout will be the day of their coming forth from the grave.” (Qur’án 50:41–42, Rodwell)

Meeting with God

The Qur’án speaks again and again of meeting the Lord. In Rodwell:

“Who bear in mind that they shall meet their Lord, and that unto Him shall they return.” (Qur’án 2:46, Rodwell)

“…He maketh his signs clear, that ye may have firm faith in a meeting with your Lord.” (Qur’án 13:2, Rodwell)

Bahá’u’lláh quotes five verses of this kind in one passage of the Íqán (29:23, 2:46, 2:249, 18:110 and 13:2, as the Íqán’s own notes give them), excerpted here:

“As for those who believe not in the signs of God, or that they shall ever meet Him, these of My mercy shall despair, and for them doth a grievous chastisement await.”… “Let him then who hopeth to attain the presence of his Lord work a righteous work.” And also He saith: “He ordereth all things. He maketh His signs clear, that ye may have firm faith in attaining the presence of your Lord.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 148)

The seal of the prophets

“Muhammad is not the father of any man among you, but he is the Apostle of God, and the seal of the prophets: and God knoweth all things.” (Qur’án 33:40, Rodwell)

Most Muslims understand this verse to mean that prophethood ended with Muḥammad. The Writings address the phrase directly. Bahá’u’lláh writes in the Íqán:

“Hath not Muḥammad, Himself, declared: ‘I am all the Prophets’? Hath He not said as We have already mentioned: ‘I am Adam, Noah, Moses, and Jesus’? Why should Muḥammad, that immortal Beauty, Who hath said: ‘I am the first Adam’ be incapable of saying also: ‘I am the last Adam’?” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 172)

He continues that “the ‘Seal of the Prophets’ is also applicable unto that Divine Beauty”, and that “being the ‘First of the Prophets,’ He likewise is their ‘Seal.’” In another passage He writes of “veils of glory” and names “such terms as the ‘Seal of the Prophets’ and the like” among them (KI 178). The rest of what the Writings say on this verse is gathered in section 4.

“He of Whom they had knowledge”

“And when a Book had come to them from God, confirming that which they had received already-although they had before prayed for victory over those who believed not-yet when that Koran come to them, of which they had knowledge, they did not recognise it. The curse of God on the infidels!” (Qur’án 2:89, Rodwell)

Rodwell’s rendering takes the object to be the Qur’án. The rendering Bahá’u’lláh gives in the Íqán has a person as the object:

“Although they had before prayed for victory over those who believed not, yet when there came unto them He of Whom they had knowledge, they disbelieved in Him. The curse of God on the infidels!” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 159, with Qur’án 2:89)

He uses this verse in His explanation of “return”, which is in section 4.

2. The traditions (ḥadíth) as the Writings quote them

A ḥadíth is a report of what Muḥammad said or did and, in Shí‘ih tradition, of what the Imáms said. Each is passed down through a chain of narrators and gathered into books. The Qá’im and the Mihdí, the signs that come with them, and the return of the Imám Ḥusayn come mainly from these books. Muslims differ over which traditions are reliable.

This section is not an account of what Islamic tradition says. It gives the traditions that Bahá’u’lláh quotes, in His wording as translated, and records how He reads them. He names His sources (the Káfí, the Biḥáru’l-Anvár, the ‘Aválim, the Yanbú‘, the Arba‘ín, the Rawḍiy-i-Káfí, the Prayer of Nudbih and a Zíyárat); this page has not compared His wording with those books. The traditions are Islamic; the reading is Bahá’u’lláh’s.

In what sense Bahá’u’lláh reads them

He cites them to readers who accept them. In the Íqán He introduces them this way:

“Although We did not intend to make mention of the traditions of a bygone age, yet, because of Our love for thee, We will cite a few which are applicable to Our argument.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 266)

He says that the traditions He cites have been fulfilled, and distinguishes them from others.

“They ignore these well-founded traditions, all of which have been fulfilled, and cling unto those of doubtful validity, and ask why these have not been fulfilled.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 268)

He says that the Imáms spoke in two ways. Some sayings are plain. Others are veiled, and the literal meaning is not the one intended:

“The other language is veiled and concealed, so that whatever lieth hidden in the heart of the malevolent may be made manifest and their innermost being be disclosed.… In such utterances, the literal meaning, as generally understood by the people, is not what hath been intended. Thus it is recorded: ‘Every knowledge hath seventy meanings, of which one only is known amongst the people. And when the Qá’im shall arise, He shall reveal unto men all that which remaineth.’ He also saith: ‘We speak one word, and by it we intend one and seventy meanings; each one of these meanings we can explain.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 283)

He treats the Qur’án as the standard by which traditions are confirmed.

Shoghi Effendi marks two of the traditions as reliable. He calls the saying on the twenty-seven letters “a correct and authorized tradition” (WOB 2.23), and the Ḥadíth-i-Jábir “authenticated by Bahá’u’lláh in the Kitáb-i-Íqán” (GPB V.28). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s assessment of the traditions about the twelfth Imám is different, and is given at the end of this section.

“When the Qá’im riseth, that day is the Day of Resurrection”

“Have they not heard the well-known tradition: ‘When the Qá’im riseth, that day is the Day of Resurrection’?” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 152)

Bahá’u’lláh quotes this tradition in His argument about the Day of Resurrection. The Imáms’ reading of Qur’án 2:210, quoted in section 1, follows it in the same passage. His conclusion, given in section 4, is that “Resurrection” means “the rise of the Manifestation of God to proclaim His Cause.”

The sovereignty of the Qá’im

The traditions say that the Qá’im will have sovereignty. The Íqán asks why this has not appeared in the form people expected, and answers:

“Yea, the sovereignty attributed to the Qá’im and spoken of in the scriptures is a reality, the truth of which none can doubt. This sovereignty, however, is not the sovereignty which the minds of men have falsely imagined.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 113)

“You will readily recognize that the terms sovereignty, wealth, life, death, judgment and resurrection, spoken of by the scriptures of old, are not what this generation hath conceived and vainly imagined. Nay, by sovereignty is meant that sovereignty which in every dispensation resideth within, and is exercised by, the person of the Manifestation, the Daystar of Truth.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 114)

The Prayer of Nudbih, the Zíyárat and the Mihdí

Bahá’u’lláh quotes a Shí‘ih prayer of longing, a Zíyárat (a supplication of salutation) and a saying of Imám Ṣádiq about the Mihdí:

“Among the utterances that foreshadow a new Law and a new Revelation are the passages in the ‘Prayer of Nudbih’: ‘Where is He Who is preserved to renew the ordinances and laws? Where is He Who hath the authority to transform the Faith and the followers thereof?’ He hath, likewise, revealed in the Zíyárat: ‘Peace be upon the Truth made new.’ Abú-‘Abdi’lláh, questioned concerning the character of the Mihdí, answered saying: ‘He will perform that which Muḥammad, the Messenger of God, hath performed, and will demolish whatever hath been before Him even as the Messenger of God hath demolished the ways of those that preceded Him.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 269)

A Youth from Baní-Háshim

Baní-Háshim is the clan of Muḥammad. The Íqán calls the ‘Aválim “an authoritative and well-known book”, and says it records:

“A Youth from Baní-Háshim shall be made manifest, Who will reveal a new Book and promulgate a new law” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 270, quoting the ‘Aválim)

Shoghi Effendi writes that in the Íqán Bahá’u’lláh acclaimed the Báb as the promised Qá’im: “He Whom Bahá’u’lláh has acclaimed in the Kitáb-i-Íqán as that promised Qá’im Who has manifested no less than twenty-five out of the twenty-seven letters which all the Prophets were destined to reveal” (Shoghi Effendi, The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, WOB 6.9). The Báb’s own claim is in section 3.

“Knowledge is twenty and seven letters”

“Thus it is related in the ‘Biḥáru’l-Anvár,’ the ‘Aválim,’ and the ‘Yanbú‘’ of Ṣádiq, son of Muḥammad, that he spoke these words: ‘Knowledge is twenty and seven letters. All that the Prophets have revealed are two letters thereof. No man thus far hath known more than these two letters. But when the Qá’im shall arise, He will cause the remaining twenty and five letters to be made manifest.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 272)

Bahá’u’lláh’s comment on it:

“Behold from this utterance how great and lofty is His station! His rank excelleth that of all the Prophets, and His Revelation transcendeth the comprehension and understanding of all their chosen ones.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 272)

Shoghi Effendi quotes the tradition and this comment in The World Order of Bahá’u’lláh, where he introduces it as “a correct and authorized tradition” (WOB 2.23; also WOB 4.30 and WOB 6.60).

The Tablet of Fáṭimih

“Even as it hath been recorded in the ‘Káfí,’ in the tradition of Jábir, in the ‘Tablet of Fáṭimih,‘ concerning the character of the Qá’im: ‘He shall manifest the perfection of Moses, the splendor of Jesus, and the patience of Job. His chosen ones shall be abased in His day. Their heads shall be offered as presents even as the heads of the Turks and the Daylamites. They shall be slain and burnt. Fear shall seize them; dismay and alarm shall strike terror into their hearts. The earth shall be dyed with their blood. Their womenfolk shall bewail and lament. These indeed are my friends!‘ Consider, not a single letter of this tradition hath remained unfulfilled.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 273)

Shoghi Effendi applies this tradition to the Bábí martyrs; his statement is in section 4. The Íqán then asks why such traditions would have been recorded if the Qá’im were to reveal only the law of a former Dispensation.

The Standard of Truth

The divines of that age

Bahá’u’lláh quotes one more saying of Imám Ṣádiq on the divines of the age of the Qá’im. It is a saying attributed to an Imám and quoted by Bahá’u’lláh, and is reported here as part of what the Íqán quotes:

“The religious doctors of that age shall be the most wicked of the divines beneath the shadow of heaven. Out of them hath mischief proceeded, and unto them it shall return.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 275, quoting Imám Ṣádiq)

The year sixty

“Consider, that even the year in which that Quintessence of Light is to be made manifest hath been specifically recorded in the traditions… According to the tradition, Mufaḍḍal asked Ṣádiq saying: ‘What of the sign of His manifestation, O my master?’ He made reply: ‘In the year sixty, His Cause shall be made manifest, and His Name shall be proclaimed.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 281)

Years are counted in the Islamic calendar, and 1260 A.H. began in 1844. Of the Qur’án the Íqán says: “All have been enjoined to follow the precepts of that Book until ‘the year sixty’—the year of the advent of God’s wondrous Manifestation” (KI 221). Bahá’u’lláh states the identification outright in the Gleanings:

“…energies which manifested themselves in the plenitude of their glory when the Ancient Beauty appeared, in the year sixty, in the person of ‘Alí-Muḥammad, the Báb.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XXXIII.2)

Four signs from four Prophets

“In the ‘Biḥár’ it is recorded: ‘In our Qá’im there shall be four signs from four Prophets, Moses, Jesus, Joseph, and Muḥammad. The sign from Moses is fear and expectation; from Jesus, that which was spoken of Him; from Joseph, imprisonment and dissimulation; from Muḥammad, the revelation of a Book similar to the Qur’án.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 282)

Bahá’u’lláh’s comment is that the tradition “in such unmistakable language hath foreshadowed the happenings of the present day” (KI 282).

The traditions on ‘Akká

Near its end, the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf gives a series of traditions about the city of ‘Akká, where Bahá’u’lláh was imprisoned. He names the narrators (‘Abdu’l-‘Azíz son of ‘Abdu’-Salám, Ibn-i-Mas‘úd and Anas son of Malík). Two of the traditions:

“I announce unto you a city, on the shores of the sea, white, whose whiteness is pleasing unto God—exalted be He! It is called ‘Akká.… There are kings and princes in Paradise. The poor of ‘Akká are the kings of Paradise and the princes thereof. A month in ‘Akká is better than a thousand years elsewhere.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 263)

These traditions are about a place, not directly about the Day. Bahá’u’lláh does not comment on them; He gives them as recorded and ends the series with “Verily, the Apostle of God—may the blessings of God, exalted be He, and His salutations be upon Him—hath spoken the truth.” (ESW 268)

A tablet of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on the traditions about the twelfth Imám

Twelver Shí‘ih belief in the occultation of the twelfth Imám, Muḥammad son of Ḥasan al-‘Askarí, rests on the traditions. The sources in this repository do not give the Twelver account. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s tablet to Ḥájí Ṣadr-i-Hamadání, in a provisional translation by Adib Masumian (2016), says:

“As to the question of the twelfth Imám, there existeth in the ḥadíths an apparent contradiction. In one ḥadíth, Muḥammad ibn Ḥasan —peace be upon Him—is considered the promised Qáʼim, yet in another, it is mentioned that the death of that same Muḥammad ibn Ḥasan will precede the birth of the awaited Qáʼim in the End of Time. One cannot reconcile these two ḥadíths, unless he be certain that the twelfth Imám—the promised Qáʼim—existed spiritually in the Kingdom of God, and was manifested physically, in the form of another person, at the Hour of Revelation.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, provisional translation by Adib Masumian)

The tablet also calls the matter “a chain of highly questionable ḥadíths.” This is ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s assessment. Bahá’u’lláh, in the Íqán, quotes other traditions as sound and as fulfilled, and in the Gems of Divine Mysteries He affirms that what is said of Muḥammad son of Ḥasan is true while reading it symbolically (section 4). The page reports each and does not reconcile them further.

3. The Shí‘ih expectation fulfilled: the Báb as the Qá’im

The Qá’im (He who ariseth) and the return of the Imám Ḥusayn belong to Shí‘ih tradition. They are not named in the Qur’án. The traditions that Bahá’u’lláh quotes about the Qá’im are in section 2. This section covers what the Shí‘ih awaited, what the two teachers before the Báb said about the nearness of the Day, and what the Báb said of Himself. Each quotation is labelled: the Writings, Shoghi Effendi, or history (Nabíl’s The Dawn-Breakers, translated and edited by Shoghi Effendi).

3.1 What the Shí‘ih awaited

Shoghi Effendi describes the expectation among the Shí‘ih of Persia when the Báb appeared. He writes of its clergy that for a thousand years they had invoked the Hidden Imám:

“…the members of this caste had for one thousand years invoked the name of the Hidden Imám, their breasts had glowed with the expectation of His advent, their pulpits had rung with the praises of His world-embracing dominion, their lips were still devoutly and perpetually murmuring prayers for the hastening of His coming.” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB I.2)

In the same paragraph he gives the Báb’s titles: “the promised Qá’im (He who ariseth), the Ṣáḥibu’z-Zamán (the Lord of the Age), Who assumed the exclusive right of annulling the whole Qur’ánic Dispensation” (GPB I.2). He also records how large the claim was. The Faith’s Founder “did not content Himself with the claim to be the Gate of the Hidden Imám,” but “assumed a rank that excelled even that of the Ṣáḥibu’z-Zamán” and “regarded Himself as the precursor of one incomparably greater than Himself” (GPB I.13).

Shoghi Effendi also records a tradition that sets out the signs of the Qá’im, and says Bahá’u’lláh authenticated it:

“Who else could be meant by this tradition, called Ḥadíth-i-Jábir, recorded in the Káfí, and authenticated by Bahá’u’lláh in the Kitáb-i-Íqán, which, in indubitable language, sets forth the signs of the appearance of the promised Qá’im? ‘His saints shall be abased in His time, and their heads shall be exchanged as presents, even as the heads of the Turk and the Daylamite are exchanged as presents; they shall be slain and burned, and shall be afraid, fearful and dismayed; the earth shall be dyed with their blood, and lamentation and wailing shall prevail amongst their women; these are My saints indeed.’” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB V.28)

Shoghi Effendi applies the tradition to those who, with their life-blood, “ushered in the Promised Day” (GPB V.28). The Íqán’s own use of it is in section 2 (KI 273).

3.2 The two teachers who announced the nearness of the Day (history)

Two Shí‘ih teachers, Shaykh Aḥmad-i-Aḥsá’í and then Siyyid Káẓim-i-Rashtí, taught that the Day of the Qá’im was near. Shoghi Effendi writes that Shaykh Aḥmad “laid stress upon ‘the twin Revelations which are to follow each other in rapid succession’” (GPB VI.7). The Báb names the two teachers in the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’:

“Formerly I sent forth unto you Aḥmad and more recently Káẓim, but apart from the pure in heart amongst you no one followed them.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 10.33, from the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’)

The compilers of the Selections do not name them; that they are Shaykh Aḥmad and Siyyid Káẓim is the usual reading.

A witness, Mírzá Maḥmúd-i-Qamsarí, tells Nabíl (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. I) that he found Shaykh Aḥmad at Náyin at dawn, fallen on his face in prayer, and that Shaykh Aḥmad said:

“That which I have been announcing to you is now revealed. At this very hour the light of the promised One has broken and is shedding illumination upon the world. O Mahmud, verily I say, you shall live to behold that Day of days.” (Shaykh Aḥmad, reported by Maḥmúd-i-Qamsarí in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. I)

Nabíl records that Siyyid Káẓim told his disciples that the promised One was “of noble lineage,” a descendant of the Prophet, young and possessed of innate knowledge (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. II). Mullá Ḥusayn recites these signs in section 6.2.

Shoghi Effendi quotes the saying with which Siyyid Káẓim linked the Qá’im and the return of Ḥusayn:

“Verily, I say, after the Qá’im the Qayyúm will be made manifest. For when the star of the former has set the sun of the beauty of Ḥusayn will rise and illuminate the whole world.” (Siyyid Káẓim-i-Rashtí, quoted in God Passes By, GPB VI.20)

The saying is Siyyid Káẓim’s. The Bahá’í reading in which the Báb is the Qá’im and Bahá’u’lláh is the return of Ḥusayn rests on Shoghi Effendi’s application of it (GPB VI.11–12, 18, 20).

3.3 The Báb’s own words

The Primal Point and the Qá’im. In an Epistle to Muḥammad Sháh the Báb writes:

“I am the Primal Point from which have been generated all created things. I am the Countenance of God Whose splendor can never be obscured, the Light of God Whose radiance can never fade.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 4.4)

In the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ the Báb uses the title of the Gate for Himself. The compilers’ note says that in these passages the name Qurratu’l-‘Ayn (Solace of the Eyes) refers to the Báb Himself.

“Verily I am the ‘Gate of God’ and I give you to drink, by the leave of God, the sovereign Truth, of the crystal-pure waters of His Revelation which are gushing out from the incorruptible Fountain situate upon the Holy Mount.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 10.30, from the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’)

The Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ is the Báb’s commentary on the Súrih of Joseph (GPB II.10). Shoghi Effendi says it “was this Book which the Bábís universally regarded, during almost the entire ministry of the Báb, as the Qur’án of the people of the Bayán” (GPB II.11).

The Qá’im. In the Seven Proofs (Dalá’il-i-Sab‘ih) the Báb addresses the Muslims directly with the expectation of the Qá’im:

“He directed everyone to await the advent of the Promised Qá’im. All deeds which in the Islamic Dispensation began with Muḥammad should find their consummation through the appearance of the Qá’im.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 12.3, from the Seven Proofs)

In the next paragraph He describes the stages of His claim:

“…identified Himself as the Gate [Báb] for the advent of the promised Qá’im, a descendant of Muḥammad, and in His first Book enjoined the observance of the laws of the Qur’án, so that the people might not be seized with perturbation by reason of a new Book and a new Revelation…” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 12.4, from the Seven Proofs)

In the Kitáb-i-Asmá’ (the Book of Names) the Báb speaks in the first person and names the Qá’im, the Guide and the Mihdí as the one to whom the Bayán is revealed:

“He Who hath revealed the Qur’án unto Muḥammad, the Apostle of God, ordaining in the Faith of Islám that which was pleasing unto Him, hath likewise revealed the Bayán, in the manner ye have been promised, unto Him Who is your Qá’im, your Guide, your Mihdí, your Lord, Him Whom ye acclaim as the manifestation of God’s most excellent titles. Verily the equivalent of that which God revealed unto Muḥammad during twenty-three years, hath been revealed unto Me within the space of two days and two nights.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 13.34, from the Kitáb-i-Asmá’)

The referent of “your Qá’im” in this passage is the speaker: the next sentence says that what was revealed to the Qá’im “hath been revealed unto Me.” The compilers’ footnotes gloss “Qá’im” as “He Who ariseth” and “Mihdí” as “One Who is guided,” and point to GPB IV.17.

The Day of God. In the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ the Báb also ties the Day of God to His own appearance. He addresses the Hour of the Dawn:

“O HOUR of the Dawn! Ere the resplendent glory of the divine Luminary sheddeth its radiance from the Dayspring of this Gate, call thou to mind that the appointed Day of God will indeed be at hand in less than the twinkling of an eye.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 10.94, from the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’)

3.4 The Tabríz claim

Shoghi Effendi describes the examination of the Báb at Tabríz in 1848 before the heir to the throne, Náṣiri’d-Dín Mírzá, and the clergy of Ádharbayján. He calls it “the supreme opportunity of His mission to assert in public, formally and without any reservation, the claims inherent in His Revelation” (GPB II.7). In a later chapter he writes of what the Báb claimed:

“The formal assumption by the Báb of the authority of the promised Qá’im, in such dramatic circumstances and in so challenging a tone, before a distinguished gathering of eminent Shí‘ah ecclesiastics, powerful, jealous, alarmed and hostile, was the explosive force that loosed a veritable avalanche of calamities which swept down upon the Faith and the people among whom it was born.” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB III.3)

‘Abdu’l-Bahá, describing the gathering at Badasht in the same year, writes: “In those days the fact that the Báb was the Qá’im had not yet been proclaimed” (Memorials of the Faithful, MF 69.26). In the next paragraph of God Passes By Shoghi Effendi records that the Báb “adduced verses from the Qur’án to establish the truth of His assertion, and claimed to be able to reveal, within the space of two days and two nights, verses equal to the whole of that Book” (GPB II.8). The words “two days and two nights” are the Báb’s own in the Kitáb-i-Asmá’ (SWB 13.34, above).

Nabíl’s narrative gives the words of the claim at the examination. The Nizámu’l-‘Ulamá asked the Báb, “Whom do you claim to be… and what is the message which you have brought?” The Báb answered:

“‘I am,’ thrice exclaimed the Báb, ‘I am, I am, the promised One! I am the One whose name you have for a thousand years invoked, at whose mention you have risen, whose advent you have longed to witness, and the hour of whose Revelation you have prayed God to hasten. Verily I say, it is incumbent upon the peoples of both the East and the West to obey My word and to pledge allegiance to My person.’” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XVIII, Nabíl’s narrative, in Shoghi Effendi’s translation)

Shoghi Effendi gives the same answer in God Passes By (GPB II.7).

3.5 The year sixty

The Báb dates His own proclamation to the year sixty. In the Epistle to Muḥammad Sháh He writes:

“In the year sixty God graciously infused my soul with the conclusive evidences and weighty knowledge which characterize Him Who is the Testimony of God—may peace be upon Him—until finally in that year I proclaimed God’s hidden Cause and unveiled its well-guarded Pillar, in such wise that no one could refute it.” (the Báb, Selections from the Writings of the Báb, SWB 4.7)

The compilers’ footnote gives the year as 1260 A.H. (1844 A.D.). In the Persian Bayán the Báb counts from the declaration of Muḥammad: “TWELVE hundred and seventy years have elapsed since the declaration of Muḥammad” (SWB 11.40). He says that in the concluding year of this period, “He Who is Himself the Founder of the House went on pilgrimage,” and that only one person recognised Him and performed the pilgrimage with Him; the compilers’ note identifies that person as Quddús (SWB 11.40).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá gives the same date in his explanation of the 1,260 days of Daniel and Revelation: “it is in the very year 1260 from the emigration of Muḥammad, according to the Muslim calendar, that the Báb, the Herald of Bahá’u’lláh, revealed His mission” (SAQ 10.20).

The Íqán quotes the tradition of Imám Ṣádiq and Mufaḍḍal on the same year (KI 281; section 2). How Bahá’í authors have treated the year is in section 5.

3.6 The Mihdí and the Black Standards

The Sunní expectation is of the Mihdí (One Who is guided) and of the descent of Jesus. Shoghi Effendi names the Báb as “the ‘Mihdí’ (One Who is guided) awaited by the Sunnís” (GPB IV.17) and Bahá’u’lláh as, to Sunní Islám, “the descent of the ‘Spirit of God’ (Jesus Christ)” (GPB VI.11). The descent of Jesus is treated in section 4. The Báb’s own words name the Mihdí with the Qá’im (“your Qá’im, your Guide, your Mihdí”, SWB 13.34, above), and He speaks of “Him Who is your promised Mihdí” (SWB 13.56).

Nabíl records a tradition of the Prophet about the Mihdí, which he applies to the standard that Mullá Ḥusayn raised in Mázindarán:

“Should your eyes behold the Black Standards proceeding from Khurásán, hasten ye towards them, even though ye should have to crawl over the snow, inasmuch as they proclaim the advent of the promised Mihdí, the Vicegerent of God.” (tradition of Muḥammad, quoted in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XIX)

4. The fulfilment in the Writings: the Day of God

Shoghi Effendi lists the Qur’ánic titles of the Day in GPB VI.17: the “Great Day,” the “Last Day,” the “Day of God,” the “Day of Judgment,” the “Day of Reckoning,” the “Day of Mutual Deceit,” the “Day of Severing,” the “Day of Sighing,” the “Day of Meeting,” and the Day “when the Decree shall be accomplished.” The subsections below follow the themes of section 1.

Resurrection, life and death (KI 118–129)

Bahá’u’lláh argues in the Íqán that the Day of Resurrection was ushered in by Muḥammad’s own Revelation, and that its terms describe what the Qur’án’s verses did in Muḥammad’s time:

“Hast thou not heard how with one single verse He hath sundered light from darkness, the righteous from the ungodly, and the believing from the infidel? All the signs and allusions concerning the Day of Judgment, which thou hast heard, such as the raising of the dead, the Day of Reckoning, the Last Judgment, and others have been made manifest through the revelation of that verse.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 118)

“By the terms ‘life’ and ‘death,‘ spoken of in the scriptures, is intended the life of faith and the death of unbelief.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 120)

Meeting with God (KI 148–153)

The Íqán’s argument takes the verses in section 1 at their word: “No theme hath been more emphatically asserted in the holy scriptures” (KI 149). It asks what “attainment unto the Divine Presence” can mean. It considers three meanings that might be given to the phrase, and sets two of them aside:

  • A universal revelation of God, present in all things. This, Bahá’u’lláh writes, “already existeth in all things”, so there would be no reason to restrict it to the Day of Resurrection (KI 149).
  • A revelation in the divine Essence. In this sense “attainment unto the divine Presence” would be “obviously possible to no one” (KI 150).
  • A revelation in the Prophets and chosen ones of God. This, He says, is attainable:

“By attaining, therefore, to the presence of these holy Luminaries, the ‘Presence of God’ Himself is attained. From their knowledge, the knowledge of God is revealed, and from the light of their countenance, the splendor of the Face of God is made manifest.… Attainment unto such presence is possible only in the Day of Resurrection, which is the Day of the rise of God Himself through His all-embracing Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 151)

He states the conclusion in one sentence when He discusses the Seal of the Prophets:

“It hath been demonstrated and definitely established, through clear evidences, that by ‘Resurrection’ is meant the rise of the Manifestation of God to proclaim His Cause, and by ‘attainment unto the divine Presence’ is meant attainment unto the presence of His Beauty in the person of His Manifestation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 182)

“Return” (KI 159–162)

The Íqán’s use of the verse in section 1 about “He of Whom they had knowledge” opens its explanation of “return”:

“Strive therefore to comprehend the meaning of ‘return’ which hath been so explicitly revealed in the Qur’án itself, and which none hath as yet understood.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 160)

The Manifestations are one in their station of unity. Bahá’u’lláh concludes:

“Wherefore, should one of these Manifestations of Holiness proclaim saying: ‘I am the return of all the Prophets,‘ He verily speaketh the truth. In like manner, in every subsequent Revelation, the return of the former Revelation is a fact, the truth of which is firmly established.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 162)

This is the principle on which the Writings call Bahá’u’lláh the return of the Imám Ḥusayn and of Jesus, below.

The Seal of the Prophets (KI 172–196, SLH 1.243–245)

Section 1 gives 33:40 and the Íqán’s argument at KI 172. The Íqán then sets the verse beside the promise of meeting God:

“Even as the Lord of being hath in His unerring Book, after speaking of the ‘Seal’ in His exalted utterance: ‘Muḥammad is the Apostle of God and the Seal of the Prophets,’ hath revealed unto all people the promise of ‘attainment unto the divine Presence.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 181, with Qur’án 33:40)

“And were they all to proclaim: ‘I am the Seal of the Prophets,‘ they verily utter but the truth, beyond the faintest shadow of doubt.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Íqán, KI 196)

In the Kitáb-i-Aqdas He addresses the divines:

“Take heed lest the word ‘Prophet’ withhold you from this Most Great Announcement, or any reference to ‘Vicegerency’ debar you from the sovereignty of Him Who is the Vicegerent of God, which overshadoweth all the worlds.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Kitáb-i-Aqdas, KA 167)

These are statements of the Bahá’í understanding. They do not describe how Muslims understand the verse (section 1). In the Tablet to Náṣiri’d-Dín Sháh Bahá’u’lláh writes of Muḥammad’s sufferings with reverence: “Consider the dispensation of Him Who is the Seal of the Prophets and the King of the Chosen Ones—may the souls of all mankind be offered up for His sake!” (SLH 1.243).

“Hath the Hour come?” “Nay, more; it hath passed” (ESW 192)

Bahá’u’lláh quotes verses He sent in answer to certain individuals. They take up questions people ask about the Day (the Hour, the Resurrection, Paradise and Hell, the Balance, the stars) and answer each one in the Qur’án’s own language:

“Among them are those who have said: ‘Have the verses been sent down?’ Say: ‘Yea, by Him Who is the Lord of the heavens!’ ‘Hath the Hour come?’ ‘Nay, more; it hath passed, by Him Who is the Revealer of clear tokens! Verily, the Inevitable is come, and He, the True One, hath appeared with proof and testimony.’… Say: ‘The stunning trumpet blast hath been loudly raised, and the Day is God’s, the One, the Unconstrained.’ ‘Is the Resurrection come?’ ‘Nay, more; He Who is the Self-Subsisting hath appeared with the Kingdom of His signs.‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 192)

The same passage says that the stars fell “when He Who is the Self-Subsisting dwelt in the Land of Mystery (Adrianople)”, where He lived from 1863 to 1868. Asked where Paradise and Hell are, He answers: “The one is reunion with Me; the other thine own self.” In another passage of the Epistle Bahá’u’lláh says that the present Day is the Day of which Luqmán spoke to his son, and which God announced to Muḥammad in the words of Qur’án 31:16:

“This Day the deceitful of eye, and all that men’s breasts conceal, are made known and laid bare before the throne of His Revelation.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 162)

The Great Announcement, the trumpet and the Crier in Bahá’u’lláh’s voice

The Gleanings contain tablets in which Bahá’u’lláh speaks in the language of the Qur’án’s Day:

“BY Him Who is the Great Announcement! The All-Merciful is come invested with undoubted sovereignty. The Balance hath been appointed, and all them that dwell on earth have been gathered together. The Trumpet hath been blown, and lo, all eyes have stared up with terror, and the hearts of all who are in the heavens and on the earth have trembled, except them whom the breath of the verses of God hath quickened, and who have detached themselves from all things.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XVII.1)

“Speed out of your sepulchers. How long will ye sleep? The second blast hath been blown on the trumpet. On whom are ye gazing? This is your Lord, the God of Mercy.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XVIII.5)

“This is the Day whereon the All-Merciful hath come down in the clouds of knowledge, clothed with manifest sovereignty.… The heaven of every religion hath been rent, and the earth of human understanding been cleft asunder, and the angels of God are seen descending.… The mountains have passed away, and the heavens have been folded together, and the whole earth is held within His grasp, could ye but understand it.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XVIII.6)

“This is the flowing water ye were promised in the Qur’án, and later in the Bayán, as a recompense from your Lord, the God of Mercy. Blessed are they that quaff it.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XVIII.8)

These passages apply the pictures of section 1 to the Day of His own Revelation. Bahá’u’lláh also writes of what Muḥammad would have said of that Day:

“Had Muḥammad, the Apostle of God, attained this Day, He would have exclaimed: ‘I have truly recognized Thee, O Thou the Desire of the Divine Messengers!‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Súriy-i-Ra’ís, in The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, SLH 2.18)

“…the promised hour is now come.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XII)

“This is indeed the Day of which it hath been written: ‘Whose shall be the Kingdom this Day?‘” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB LXXVIII.3)

The Qá’im, the Dajjál, the Sufyání and Jábulqá (Gems of Divine Mysteries)

Bahá’u’lláh answers the objection that signs from Islamic tradition have not appeared. The signs named in His text are the sun rising in the West, the Crier crying out between earth and heaven, water inundating certain lands, the Dajjál (the Antichrist), the Sufyání and the Temple seen in the sun.

“And all this, when the Day of Resurrection hath been ushered in, and the Trumpet hath been sounded, and all the denizens of earth and heaven have been gathered together, and the Balance hath been appointed… and the souls have been raised to life, and the breath of the Spirit hath blown, and the angels have been arrayed in ranks, and Paradise hath been brought nigh, and Hell made to blaze!” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Gems of Divine Mysteries, GDM 24)

On the Twelfth Imám, Muḥammad son of Ḥasan, and the city of Jábulqá, where Shí‘ih tradition places him:

“All that thou hast heard regarding Muḥammad the son of Ḥasan—may the souls of all that are immersed in the oceans of the spirit be offered up for His sake—is true beyond the shadow of a doubt, and we all verily bear allegiance unto Him. But the imáms of the Faith have fixed His abode in the city of Jábulqá, which they have depicted in strange and marvellous signs. To interpret this city according to the literal meaning of the tradition would indeed prove impossible, nor can such a city ever be found.” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Gems of Divine Mysteries, GDM 48)

“For by Jábulqá is meant none other than the treasure-houses of eternity in the all-highest heaven and the cities of the unseen in the supernal realm.” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Gems of Divine Mysteries, GDM 49)

“But as to Him Who appeared in the year sixty, He standeth in need of neither transformation nor interpretation, for His name was Muḥammad, and He was a descendent of the Imáms of the Faith.” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Gems of Divine Mysteries, GDM 50)

The return of the Imám Ḥusayn

Shoghi Effendi’s statement is that Bahá’u’lláh’s coming is, “to Shí‘ah Islám the return of the Imám Ḥusayn” (GPB VI.11). The Writings that bear on this are these. Bahá’u’lláh addresses a Ḥusayn on the expectation of the Imám’s return:

“O ḤUSAYN! Consider the eagerness with which certain peoples and nations have anticipated the return of Imám Ḥusayn, whose coming, after the appearance of the Qá’im, hath been prophesied, in days past, by the chosen ones of God, exalted be His glory. These holy ones have, moreover, announced that when He Who is the Dayspring of the manifold grace of God manifesteth Himself, all the Prophets and Messengers, including the Qá’im, will gather together beneath the shadow of the sacred Standard which the Promised One will raise. That hour is now come.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB IX)

In the Súriy-i-Mulúk Bahá’u’lláh calls Himself by that name, and writes: “The tribulations We have sustained are like unto the trials endured aforetime by Imám Ḥusayn” (SLH 5.48).

“THIS is a Tablet from this Servant, who is called Ḥusayn in the kingdom of names, to the concourse of the kings of the earth.” (Bahá’u’lláh, The Summons of the Lord of Hosts, SLH 5.1)

Shoghi Effendi writes of the name Bahá’u’lláh bore:

“In the name He bore He combined those of the Imám Ḥusayn, the most illustrious of the successors of the Apostle of God… and of the Imám ‘Alí, the Commander of the Faithful” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.12)

and of Ḥusayn’s own testimony, according to Bahá’u’lláh:

“To the transcendent character of His mission the Imám Ḥusayn had, again according to Bahá’u’lláh, borne witness as a ‘Revelation whose Revealer will be He Who revealed’ the Apostle of God Himself” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB VI.18)

In the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf Bahá’u’lláh replies to a man who practised the Shí‘ih lamentation for Ḥusayn (Rawḍih-khání), and says of the Imáms:

“For it is the belief of this people that during the Revelation of the Qá’im, the Imáms—may the peace of God be upon them—have arisen from their sepulchres. This verily is the truth, and no doubt is there about it.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Epistle to the Son of the Wolf, ESW 177)

The Sunní expectation: the descent of the Spirit of God

Shoghi Effendi names what Bahá’u’lláh is to Sunní Islám: “the descent of the ‘Spirit of God’ (Jesus Christ)” (GPB VI.11). He names the Báb as “the ‘Mihdí’ (One Who is guided) awaited by the Sunnís” (GPB IV.17). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states both expectations in one sentence:

“In the religion of Muḥammad, there is the promise of the Mahdi and the Messiah.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 10.8)

The Qur’án verses most often read in this connection are these two, in Rodwell’s translation:

“And he shall be a sign of the last hour; doubt not then of it, and follow ye me: this is the right way” (Qur’án 43:61, Rodwell)

“There shall not be one of the people of the Book but shall believe in Him before his death, and in the day of resurrection, He will be a witness against them.” (Qur’án 4:159, Rodwell)

Rodwell’s note on 43:61 reads “He” as Jesus: “At his return to this earth.” The sources here do not set out how Muslim commentators read either verse.

  • The Writings record Bahá’u’lláh’s statement that He is the return of Jesus, “the Spirit of God”, and that He comes under the Qur’án’s image of the clouds. Addressing the followers of the Gospel, He also says of Muḥammad: “seize Me and put an end to My life, for I am He, and My Self is His Self.”

“O JEWS! If ye be intent on crucifying once again Jesus, the Spirit of God, put Me to death, for He hath once more, in My person, been made manifest unto you.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XLVII)

“I have come in the shadows of the clouds of glory, and am invested by God with invincible sovereignty.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings, GWB XLVII, addressed to “Followers of Muḥammad”)

  • The passages found contain no verse-by-verse treatment of Qur’án 43:61 or 4:159. The identification rests on Shoghi Effendi’s statement in GPB VI.11, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s sentence in SAQ 10.8, and Bahá’u’lláh’s principle of return.

An anonymous Bahá’í study, Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Qur’an (commentary, not authoritative interpretation), reads Qur’án 4:158 (the study’s numbering; Rodwell’s verse above is 4:159) and 43:61 as “the advent of the promised Apostle of God with the same qualities as Jesus evinced during His mission.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá: the 1,260 years and the two witnesses

Some Answered Questions reads the periods of Daniel and the Book of Revelation in terms of the Islamic dispensation. These are ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s readings, recorded as table talks. On the forty-two months of Revelation 11:2:

“This is a prophecy concerning the duration of the Dispensation of Islam, when Jerusalem was trodden underfoot, meaning that it was dishonoured, while the Holy of Holies remained preserved, guarded, and honoured. This state of affairs continued until the year 1260. This 1,260 years is a prophecy concerning the advent of the Báb, the ‘Gate’ leading to Bahá’u’lláh, which took place in the year A.H. 1260.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 11.6)

On Daniel 12, counted in lunar years from the proclamation of Muḥammad’s mission:

“And it was in the year 1290 from the proclamation of the mission of Muḥammad that Bahá’u’lláh announced His Revelation.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 10.22; the editors’ note in the Bahá’í Reference Library edition, SAQ n. 24, says: “this date corresponds to the year A.H. 1280, or A.D. 1863”)

On the two witnesses of Revelation 11:3:

“By these two witnesses are intended Muḥammad the Messenger of God and ‘Alí the son of Abú-Ṭálib.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Some Answered Questions, SAQ 11.12)

Shoghi Effendi uses the same identification when he writes that Bahá’u’lláh’s name combined those of the Imám Ḥusayn and of “the Imám ‘Alí, the Commander of the Faithful, the second of the two ‘witnesses’ extolled in that same Book” (GPB VI.12), where the Book is the Revelation of St. John. The Christian page, the-day-foretold-christian, treats Revelation 11 and 12 in more detail.

Shoghi Effendi on Islám and the Day

Shoghi Effendi writes of Islám as the parent of the Faith and puts respect for it first:

“As to Muḥammad, the Apostle of God, let none among His followers who read these pages, think for a moment that either Islám, or its Prophet, or His Book, or His appointed Successors, or any of His authentic teachings, have been, or are to be in any way, or to however slight a degree, disparaged.” (Shoghi Effendi, The Promised Day Is Come, PDC 265)

Quddús, according to Shoghi Effendi, quoted this saying of Muḥammad to his companions at the Fort of Shaykh Ṭabarsí, and Shoghi Effendi follows it with the Ḥadíth-i-Jábir (section 3.1):

“O how I long to behold the countenance of My brethren, my brethren who will appear at the end of the world! Blessed are We, blessed are they; greater is their blessedness than ours.” (Shoghi Effendi, God Passes By, GPB V.28, quoting Muḥammad)

5. How Bahá’í authors have read the Qur’án and the traditions

This section is commentary. The authors are Bahá’í writers and scholars, and none of their readings is an authoritative interpretation. For each reading the section says whether it rests on a text of the Writings. Where the Writings make the same reading, the Writings are cited and the author is secondary.

Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání (1844–1914) states the method in The Bahá’í Proofs (Ḥujaja’l-Bahíyyih, first published 1902, translated by Ali-Kuli Khan; citations are to the 1983 Bahá’í Publishing Trust facsimile of the third edition, 1929). He says that prophecies “are regarded as secondary proofs in comparison with the Divine Book and Logical Argument.” Section 6 says more about him.

5.1 Qur’án 2:210 and 89:22: the Lord in the clouds

Gulpáygání reads these verses as a prophecy of the Day of God: “Muḥammad prophesied the coming of the Lord in the clouds, with His angels,” and he adds that “The same signs are spoken of in the Gospels, as revealed by God in Matthew 24:29.” Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Qur’an (compiled by the National Spiritual Assembly of the Bahá’ís of Central and East Africa; year not given in the saved file) says: “‘God’ here, refers to the Manifestation of God who is to appear on the Last Day.” These readings rest on the Writings: KI 152, GWB XLVII, GPB VI.17, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá on 89:22 (section 1).

5.2 Qur’án 39:68–69: the two blasts of the trumpet

Several Bahá’í authors read the two blasts as two Manifestations. Some Notes… says they signify “two Manifestations of God, to follow each other, as did the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh.” Peter Khan, in a 1971 talk at an Institute on Islam (a transcript, not a published paper), says of 39:68–69: “The Glory of God, of course, Bahá’u’lláh.” R. Mustapha (Islam: The Road Ahead, 2014) dates the first blast, “the Announcement of The Báb,” to 1260 A.H. (1844) and the second, “the Great Announcement of Bahá’u’lláh,” to 1863.

The Writings support the sequence in part. Shoghi Effendi writes that with the Báb “The first ‘Trumpet-Blast’, destined to smite the earth with extermination, announced in the latter Book, had finally been sounded” (GPB IV.17), and lists “the second ‘Trumpet blast’” among the titles of Bahá’u’lláh’s Day (GPB VI.17). Bahá’u’lláh writes “The second blast hath been blown on the trumpet” (GWB XVIII.5). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá writes of Zaynu’l-Muqarrabín that “when the Trump had been sounded a second time, he was restored to life” (Memorials of the Faithful, MF 58.4). The Íqán reads a different trumpet verse (50:20) as “the trumpet-call of Muḥammad’s Revelation” (KI 123).

5.3 Qur’án 50:41: the Caller from a near place

Three Bahá’í sources place the “near place” differently, and no authoritative reading was found. Some Notes… says: “The ‘Crier’, referred to, is also Bahá’u’lláh, and the ‘near place’ from where He ‘called’ the people, was Baghdad, which is near Arabia.” Khazeh Fananapazir (see 5.6) reports in a footnote: “According to Mírzá ‘Abú’l-Fadl’s Fara’id, ‘The Place Near’ is Palestine in relation to Arabia.” That is his report of a work by Gulpáygání that is not among the files saved for this page. Peter Khan, in the 1971 talk, offers Medina as the place where Muḥammad spoke and “Baghdad, where Bahá’u’lláh called, or Shíráz from where the Báb called” for the Caller. The Writings found for this page do not name the near place. A songwriter should not state it as fact.

5.4 Qur’án 33:40 and 7:52–53

The Íqán’s own argument is at KI 172–196 (sections 1 and 4). Christopher Buck (“Beyond the ‘Seal of the Prophets’: Bahá’u’lláh’s Book of Certitude,” in Religious Texts in Iranian Languages; year not in the saved file) describes it as “calculated to establish the priority of Q. 33:44 over Q. 33:40”; 33:44 promises a meeting with God. His point is about the structure of the argument, which rests on the Íqán’s reading of “attainment unto the divine Presence” (KI 182). Stephen Lambden (“Islam, Muhammad, and the Qur’an”; year not given) reasons that because the Báb and Bahá’u’lláh “represent the coming of ‘God’ on the ‘Day of God,’ the term ‘Seal of the Prophets’ in no way precludes their manifestation.” Some Notes… states the Bahá’í belief briefly: “The Bahá’ís believe that Muhammad was indeed the Seal of the Prophets.” On 7:52–53, Gulpáygání reads a promise that “the interpretation of the Words of God will be effected at His coming and at its appointed and determined time.” This page has not located a text of the Writings that gives that reading.

5.5 The year 1260

The Writings give the date: the Íqán quotes the tradition of the year sixty (KI 281), the Báb writes “In the year sixty” of His own proclamation (SWB 4.7), and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá dates the Báb’s mission to 1260 from the Hijrah (SAQ 10.20). Authors add derivations. Y. A. Ioannesyan (“Reflections on Some Messianic Prophecies in Shaykhi Works,” Lights of ‘Irfán, Book Eleven) notes that the principle of interpretation applied to the Shaykhí texts “is similar to the one employed in the Bahá’í Writings for ‘the year sixty,‘ which is identified with the year 1260.” R. Mustapha combines Qur’án 32:5, in which a day with God is “a thousand of such years as ye reckon,” with the end of the Imáms in A.H. 260: “The two periods then are 1000 plus 260, that is to say 1260 years.” Wanden Mathews LaFarge (The Bahá’í World, vol. 3, 1928–1930) makes the same calculation from a Shí‘ih tradition about the Twelfth Imám’s disappearance in A.H. 260. The derivations are the authors’. The Writings give the date, not these derivations, in the files searched.

5.6 The Sermons of Imám ‘Alí

Khazeh Fananapazir’s translation, “Bahá’u’lláh as fulfilment of the theophanic promise in the Sermons of Imam ‘Alí ibn Abí Ṭálib” (Online Journal of Bahá’í Studies, vol. 1, 2007), gives three sermons that Shí‘ih tradition ascribes to Imám ‘Alí and argues that they promise a theophany that Bahá’u’lláh fulfils. This page does not assess their origin. His notes read the Return as the name ‘Alí-Muḥammad (the Báb) and the Raj‘at as the return of Ḥusayn. His main claim about the Writings is that Bahá’u’lláh Himself called the Sermon of the Gulf the Pivot (the Quṭb) in the Tablet of Jawhar-i-ḥamd (“The Essence of Praise”; cited as INBA 35). That Tablet is not among the files saved for this page, so the claim is Fananapazir’s until checked.

5.7 The Shaykhí tradition and the year nine

Ioannesyan translates a Shaykhí tradition quoted by Siyyid Káẓim-i-Rashtí: “The first of them to return fifty-nine years after the rising of the Qá’im… is Husayn b. ‘Ali.” He counts from the Báb’s mission (1260 A.H.), adds nine, and arrives at 1269 A.H., “the time when Bahá’u’lláh (Husayn-‘Alí) first received His revelation in the Siyáh-Chál prison.” Shoghi Effendi’s text makes the 1269 link in part: in the Síyáh-Chál “the ‘year nine’ (1269), anticipated in such glowing terms by the Báb, and alluded to as the year ‘after Ḥín’ by Shaykh Aḥmad-i-Aḥsá’í, was ushered in” (GPB VII.1). The step from “fifty-nine” to the Síyáh-Chál is Ioannesyan’s.

5.8 The return of Ḥusayn

Velasco (“Reflections on Bahá’u’lláh’s Claims to Being the Return of Imam Husayn,” an unpublished article on the Bahá’í Library; year not given) says that the “hadith literature on the subject of the return of Husayn is extensive” and contains “a great deal of often contradictory detail.” He cites a tradition in the Bihár al-Anwár that Imám ‘Alí would return twice, once with Muḥammad and once with Ḥusayn: “This became the basis for reflections on the names ‘Ali Muhammad, and Husayn ‘Ali, respectively the Bab and Bahá’u’lláh.” Shoghi Effendi writes that in the name Bahá’u’lláh bore, “He combined those of the Imám Ḥusayn… and of the Imám ‘Alí” (GPB VI.12). Velasco’s further details from Shaykhí tradition are not taken up in the Writings cited here.

5.9 The Báb and the traditions of Islám

LaFarge explains the title of the Gate from the saying ascribed to Muḥammad, “I am the City of Knowledge and ‘Alí is the Gate thereof.” She writes that the Báb “announced His mission by crying: ‘The Gate is open and I am that Gate,’” and so “declared Himself the continuation of the Imáms, in fact, the reappearance of the Twelfth Imám.” This page has not found that cry in the Writings. The Báb’s claim to be the Gate is in SWB 10.30, and Shoghi Effendi says the Báb went beyond that claim (GPB I.13; section 3.1).

5.10 The Qur’án in the Bahá’í scriptures

Todd Lawson (“The Qur’an and the Bahá’í Faith,” chapter 7; the saved file gives neither volume nor year) writes that the Íqán “was a Qur’an commentary in the sense that the author explained various Qur’an verses that spoke of Judgment Day, the Hour and the Afterlife,” and that the Báb’s commentary on the súrih of Joseph “represents the ‘True Qur’an’ expected to be in the possession of the Qa’im upon his return (ruju‘).” Shoghi Effendi says the Bábís regarded the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ as “the Qur’án of the people of the Bayán” (GPB II.11).

6. Those who recognised it (history)

This section is history, not scripture. Its main source is Nabíl-i-A‘ẓam’s The Dawn-Breakers, translated and edited by Shoghi Effendi and written from the testimony of survivors; where a person speaks, the section says whose account it is. Shoghi Effendi’s summaries in God Passes By are marked as his. The people below were Muslims, most of them Shí‘ih, and several were followers of Shaykh Aḥmad and Siyyid Káẓim. They tested the Báb’s claim against what their own teachers and traditions had told them to expect.

6.1 Siyyid Káẓim’s death and the shepherd’s dream

Nabíl records that in the first days of Dhi’l-Qádih 1259 A.H., on his yearly journey from Karbilá to Kázimayn, Siyyid Káẓim finished his noon prayer at the Masjid-i-Baratha when an Arab shepherd came up and embraced him. The shepherd said he had dreamt of the Prophet Muḥammad, who told him to give Siyyid Káẓim this message:

“Rejoice, for the hour of your departure is at hand. When you shall have performed your visits in Kazímayn and shall have returned to Karbilá, there, three days after your return, on the day of Árafih, you will wing your flight to Me. Soon after shall He who is the Truth be made manifest. Then shall the world be illuminated by the light of His face.” (the Prophet, in the shepherd’s dream, reported in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. II)

Nabíl writes that Siyyid Káẓim said: “Would you not wish me to die, that the promised One may be revealed?” He died “On the day of Árafih, in the year 1259 A.H., at the ripe age of sixty,” and left “a band of earnest and devoted disciples who, purged of all worldly desire, set out in quest of their promised Beloved” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. II).

6.2 Mullá Ḥusayn in Shíráz, 22–23 May 1844

The search. Nabíl records that Mullá Ḥusayn-i-Bushrú’í returned to Karbilá on the first day of Muḥarram 1260 A.H. and asked the leading disciples what their teacher had wished. They told him:

“…quit their homes, scatter far and wide, purge their hearts from every idle desire, and dedicate themselves to the quest of Him to whose advent he had so often alluded.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III)

Mullá Ḥusayn went to other well-known disciples with the same message and was given evasive answers. The historian H. M. Balyuzi (Bahá’u’lláh: The King of Glory) writes that Siyyid Káẓim had told his students “to keep watch, because the advent of the Sahibu’z-Zaman (the Lord of the Age), the Qa’im of the House of Muhammad, was close at hand.”

The meeting. Nabíl had Mullá Ḥusayn’s account from Mírzá Aḥmad-i-Qazvíní. Outside the gate of Shíráz, a few hours before sunset, Mullá Ḥusayn met a Youth in a green turban, who greeted him “with a smile of loving welcome” and invited him to His house. At the door “an Ethiopian servant” opened it, and the Youth said, “Enter therein in peace, secure.” Mullá Ḥusayn’s prayer, as he later told it, was: “I have striven with all my soul, O my God, and until now have failed to find Thy promised Messenger.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III).

The signs. The Youth asked whether Siyyid Káẓim had given any detailed indications of the promised One. Mullá Ḥusayn answered with the signs his teacher had given:

“‘He is of a pure lineage, is of illustrious descent, and of the seed of Fátimih. As to His age, He is more than twenty and less than thirty. He is endowed with innate knowledge. He is of medium height, abstains from smoking, and is free from bodily deficiency.’” (Mullá Ḥusayn, in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III)

Nabíl continues in Mullá Ḥusayn’s words: “He paused for a while and then with vibrant voice declared: ‘Behold, all these signs are manifest in Me!’” The Youth then considered each sign separately and showed that it applied to Him.

The Youth resolved the difficulties in the treatise that Mullá Ḥusayn had written on the hidden teachings of Shaykh Aḥmad and Siyyid Káẓim. Mullá Ḥusayn had asked Siyyid Káẓim for a commentary on the Súrih of Joseph and had been told that the One who came after him would reveal it unasked. The Youth said: “Now is the time to reveal the commentary on the Súrih of Joseph.” Mullá Ḥusayn’s account continues:

“He took up His pen and with incredible rapidity revealed the entire Súrih of Mulk, the first chapter of His commentary on the Súrih of Joseph.” (Mullá Ḥusayn, in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III)

Shoghi Effendi adds that this first chapter of the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ “was revealed in the presence of Mullá Ḥusayn, on the night of its Author’s Declaration” (GPB II.11).

The hour. Nabíl records that “At that moment the clock registered two hours and eleven minutes after sunset.” A footnote in Shoghi Effendi’s edition gives the date as “the evening of May 22, 1844 A.D.” LaFarge’s article gives 23 May.

The Youth told Mullá Ḥusayn: “This night,” He declared, “this very hour will, in the days to come, be celebrated as one of the greatest and most significant of all festivals.” Mullá Ḥusayn says: “I sat spellbound by His utterance, oblivious of time and of those who awaited me. Suddenly the call of the muadhdhín, summoning the faithful to their morning prayer, awakened me from the state of ecstasy into which I seemed to have fallen.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III).

The declaration. Then the Youth spoke:

“O thou who art the first to believe in Me! Verily I say, I am the Báb, the Gate of God, and thou art the Bábu’l-Báb, the gate of that Gate. Eighteen souls must, in the beginning, spontaneously and of their own accord, accept Me and recognise the truth of My Revelation.” (the Báb to Mullá Ḥusayn, in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III)

Shoghi Effendi summarises the account. Mullá Ḥusayn received “conclusive replies” that “had established beyond the shadow of a doubt the validity of His claim to be the promised Qá’im” (GPB I.4). Shoghi Effendi also quotes Mullá Ḥusayn on what followed: “I seemed to be the voice of Gabriel personified, calling unto all mankind: ‘Awake, for, lo! the morning Light has broken. Arise, for His Cause is made manifest.’” (Mullá Ḥusayn, quoted in God Passes By, GPB I.5).

6.3 The Letters of the Living

The Báb told Mullá Ḥusayn to keep the secret. One night He said: “Seventeen Letters have thus far enlisted under the standard of the Faith of God. There remains one more to complete the number.”

Mullá ‘Alí. Mullá ‘Alíy-i-Bastámí, after fasting and prayer, saw a light on the third night: “Allured by its splendour, he followed it, till at last it led him to his promised Beloved.” Nabíl writes of the first meeting: “Everything in that room seemed to be vibrating with this testimony: ‘Verily, verily, the dawn of a new Day has broken. The promised One is enthroned in the hearts of men. In His hand He holds the mystic cup, the chalice of immortality. Blessed are they who drink therefrom!’” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III).

Quddús. The next evening a dishevelled, travel-stained youth embraced Mullá Ḥusayn and said: “Why seek you to hide Him from me? I can recognise Him by His gait.” Nabíl writes: “The acceptance by Quddús of the truth of the Báb’s Revelation completed the assigned number of His chose disciples.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III; “chose” is the printed text). Quddús, whose name was Muḥammad-‘Alí, was, through his mother, a descendant of the Imám Ḥasan.

6.4 Ṭáhirih, who recognised the Báb unseen

Ṭáhirih was a woman from Qazvín, the daughter of a mujtahid, devoted to Siyyid Káẓim. She gave her sister’s husband, Mírzá Muḥammad-‘Alí, a sealed letter for “that promised One whom she said he was sure to meet in the course of his journey,” and told him to say to the Báb, from her:

“‘The effulgence of Thy face flashed forth, and the rays of Thy visage arose on high. Then speak the word, “Am I not your Lord?” and “Thou art, Thou art!” we will all reply.’” (Ṭáhirih, in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III)

Mírzá Muḥammad-‘Alí gave the Báb the letter and the message, and the Báb “forthwith declared her one of the Letters of the Living.”

‘Abdu’l-Bahá describes the same gathering in Memorials of the Faithful. On a day when Bahá’u’lláh was ill, Ṭáhirih left her garden with her face unveiled and walked to His pavilion, “and as she came, she shouted aloud these words: ‘The Trumpet is sounding! The great Trump is blown! The universal Advent is now proclaimed!‘” (MF 69.27). Bahá’u’lláh said: “Read the Súrih of the Inevitable,” and the reader began “When the Day that must come shall have come suddenly…” (MF 69.28). Those who were present were alarmed, and the conference broke up, “but the universal Advent had been proclaimed” (MF 69.28).

6.5 Zaynu’l-Muqarrabín

‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Memorials of the Faithful describes Zaynu’l-Muqarrabín, the leader of the people of Najaf-Ábád, who became a follower of the Báb:

“He had no sooner learned of the Báb’s Declaration than he cried out from the depths of his heart, ‘O our Lord! we have indeed heard the voice of one that called. He called us to the Faith—“Believe ye on your Lord”—and we have believed.’” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Memorials of the Faithful, MF 58.2)

6.6 Vaḥíd, sent by the Sháh to investigate

Nabíl records that Muhammad Sháh “delegated Siyyid Yahyay-i-Darábí, the most learned, the most eloquent, and the most influential of his subjects, to interview the Báb.” He is known as Vaḥíd.

Nabíl gives Vaḥíd’s own account of his third interview in Shíráz. He decided to ask for nothing aloud, but to ask in his heart for a commentary on the Súrih of Kawthar and see whether the Báb would reveal it unasked. It was revealed. He concludes:

“Such was the state of certitude to which I had attained that if all the powers of the earth were to be leagued against me they would be powerless to shake my confidence in the greatness of His Cause.” (Vaḥíd, in The Dawn-Breakers, ch. IX)

6.7 The pilgrimage and the declaration at Tabríz

Shoghi Effendi writes that in Sha‘bán 1260 A.H. (September 1844) the Báb, “of the seed of the illustrious Fáṭimih” and “a descendant of the Imám Ḥusayn,” “proceeded, in fulfillment of Islamic traditions, to visit the Kaaba” (GPB I.10). In the Persian Bayán the Báb says that “not one recognized Him, though He recognized every one of them” (SWB 11.40). The public claim before the clergy is in section 3.4.

6.8 The Black Standards from Khurásán

Nabíl records that Quddús told Mullá Ḥusayn: “Please God, you too, later on, at the head of a large company of the faithful and preceded by the ‘Black Standards,’ will depart from Mashhad and join me.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XVI). A messenger brought Mullá Ḥusayn the Báb’s turban and the message to wear it, “and, with the Black Standard unfurled before you, hasten to the Jazíriy-i-Khadrá, and lend your assistance to My beloved Quddús.” Mullá Ḥusayn “hoisted the Black Standard, placed the turban of the Báb upon his head.” (The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XIX). In the same chapter Nabíl connects this with the tradition of Muḥammad on the Mihdí (section 3.6).

6.9 Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání

Mírzá Abu’l-Faḍl Gulpáygání (1844–1914) was the son of a Muslim religious leader. Moojan Momen’s article for the Bahá’í Encyclopedia project (1995) records that he read two Tablets of Bahá’u’lláh that contain prophecies of the fall of the Ottoman vizier ‘Alí Páshá and of Sulṭán ‘Abdu’l-‘Azíz, and decided that if the events came to pass he would believe in Bahá’u’lláh: “A few months later, precisely the events foretold occurred and Gulpaygani became a Bahá’í on 20 September 1876.” He taught in Hamadan, Cairo, Paris and the United States, and died in Cairo on 21 January 1914. His readings of the Qur’án are in section 5.

7. The sacred music and poetry of Islám

These are descriptions from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0, retrieved 2026-10-16), saved under `sources/web/the-day-foretold/islamic/`, so that a songwriter can choose an idiom with care. They are descriptions, not texts for lyrics.

  • The adhan (call to prayer), recited five times a day by a muezzin. Each phrase is followed by a pause and repeated according to fixed rules. The first statement is shorter and less ornamented. On repetition the phrase is longer, has melismas, and may range over more than an octave. The form moves between tonal centres of one maqam, a fourth or a fifth apart. Medina uses Maqam Bayati and Mecca uses Maqam Hijaz. The tempo is mostly slow. The wording differs between traditions: the Sunní fajr (dawn) adhan includes “Prayer is better than sleep,” and the Shí‘ih adhan includes “Come to the best of deeds.” Mullá Ḥusayn’s account in section 6.2 ends with the muadhdhín’s call to the morning prayer.
  • Qira’at, the recitations of the Qur’án. They differ in prolongation, intonation and pronunciation, but “the variations do not change the overall message or doctrinal meanings of the Quran.” Ten readings were canonised in the early centuries of Islám.
  • Ta’ziyeh, an Iranian Shí‘ih drama often called “Iranian passion plays.” It re-enacts the Battle of Karbalá (680 CE) and the death of the Imám Ḥusayn. It has instrumental music of drums and trumpets, and sympathetic characters “chant their lines using poetic texts in classical Persian musical modes.”
  • Rowzeh-khani and noha. Rowzeh-khani is the Shí‘ih Iranian ritual of mourning, in which the rowzeh-khan recites loudly chapters of the Garden of the Martyrs. A noha is an elegy about the martyrdom of Ḥusayn and his family and companions at Karbalá.

The Shí‘ih forms are mourning forms for Ḥusayn and belong to that devotion. Bahá’u’lláh’s reply to a man who practised the lamentation for Ḥusayn is in the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf (ESW 177, section 4).

8. What the sources do not settle

  • The Qur’án and the Qá’im. The Qá’im and the return of Ḥusayn are in Shí‘ih tradition, not in the Qur’án. The Qur’án verses on this page speak of the Day of God, the Hour, the Resurrection and Jesus. How Muslim commentators read them is not in the sources gathered here.
  • The descent of Jesus (Qur’án 43:61, 4:159). No passage found in the Writings gives a verse-by-verse authoritative reading of these two verses, and none sets out the Mihdí and the descent of Jesus as a Sunní expectation in detail. The identification rests on Shoghi Effendi (GPB VI.11, IV.17), ‘Abdu’l-Bahá (SAQ 10.8), Bahá’u’lláh’s principle of return (KI 20, 159–162) and His statements about Himself (GWB XLVII). The reading in section 4 from Some Notes on Bahá’í Proofs Based on the Qur’an is commentary.
  • Qur’án 50:41, the near place. Three readings are on the page (section 5.3): Baghdád (the NSA study), Palestine in relation to Arabia (Fananapazir, citing Gulpáygání’s Farā’id, not seen here), and Medina, Baghdád or Shíráz (Khan, in a 1971 talk). No authoritative reading was found.
  • Which title belongs to whom. The same Qur’ánic Day is referred in different passages to the Báb’s coming and to Bahá’u’lláh’s.
  • Rodwell’s coverage and numbering. The saved Rodwell text holds only suras 2, 4, 7, 13, 14, 32, 33, 39, 43, 50, 55, 81, 82 and 89. Other verses appear only as the Writings render them. Rodwell has no verse numbers, so the numbers here are the standard ones; the NSA study numbers some verses differently (2:206 for 2:210, 4:158 for 4:159).
  • Provisional translations. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s Tablet to Ḥájí Ṣadr-i-Hamadání on the twelfth Imám (Adib Masumian, 2016) and the chapter of the Qayyúmu’l-Asmá’ translated by Todd Lawson are provisional. No statement of fulfilment rests on them.
  • Fananapazir’s claim. That Bahá’u’lláh called the Sermon of the Gulf the Pivot rests on a Tablet that is not in the corpus (section 5.6). It stays his claim until the Tablet is checked.
  • Two assessments of the ḥadíths on the twelfth Imám. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls them “a chain of highly questionable ḥadíths”; Bahá’u’lláh quotes others as sound and fulfilled, and says in the Gems of Divine Mysteries that what is said of Muḥammad son of Ḥasan is true. The page reports both and does not reconcile them.
  • Correction to an earlier version of this page. It said the Báb described the signs of the promised One to Mullá Ḥusayn. In The Dawn-Breakers (ch. III) Mullá Ḥusayn recites the signs that Siyyid Káẓim had given, and the Báb answers “Behold, all these signs are manifest in Me!” (section 6.2).
  • Muslim acceptance. Nothing here shows that Muslim scholars or institutions accept the Bahá’í reading.

Concrete images from the sources

  • Clouds, and the angels rank on rank: Qur’án 2:210, 89:22.
  • A blast on the trumpet, and a second: Qur’án 39:68; GWB XVIII.5.
  • A city on the shore of the sea, “white, whose whiteness is pleasing unto God”: ‘Akká, ESW 263.
  • Shaykh Aḥmad, fallen on his face in prayer at Náyin at dawn: The Dawn-Breakers, ch. I.
  • An Arab shepherd at the Masjid-i-Baratha with a dream of the Prophet, and Siyyid Káẓim dying on the day of Árafih: The Dawn-Breakers, ch. II.
  • A Youth in a green turban outside the gate of Shíráz, an Ethiopian servant at the door, a clock showing two hours and eleven minutes after sunset: The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III.
  • The pen revealing the Súrih of Mulk “with incredible rapidity,” and the muadhdhín’s call at the end of the night: The Dawn-Breakers, ch. III.
  • Ṭáhirih leaving her garden unveiled and shouting “The Trumpet is sounding!”: MF 69.27.
  • The Black Standard unfurled and the Báb’s green turban on Mullá Ḥusayn’s head: The Dawn-Breakers, ch. XIX.

Two possible arcs

  1. Waiting and answer. A thousand years of invoking the Hidden Imám (GPB I.2); Siyyid Káẓim’s dying charge to scatter far and wide; the search and the forty days of prayer; the Youth at the gate of Shíráz; “Behold, all these signs are manifest in Me!”; the muadhdhín’s call at dawn.
  2. The Day in the Qur’án’s own images. The clouds, the trumpet and the heaven rent (Qur’án 2:210, 39:68, 82:1) heard as warning; the second blast and “Speed out of your sepulchers” (GWB XVIII.5) as the waking; “Whose shall be the Kingdom this Day?” (GWB LXXVIII.3) as the question the song ends on.

What a lyric must not claim

  • It must not say that Muslims have failed to see or have rejected the Day. Bahá’u’lláh’s and the Báb’s severe words about the divines are theirs; they are not to be put in a singer’s voice as a charge (for example the saying of Imám Ṣádiq at KI 275, or the passages on the divines in the Íqán).
  • It must not say that the Qur’án itself names the Qá’im or the return of Ḥusayn. The Qá’im, the Mihdí, the Youth from Baní-Háshim, the Black Standards and the year sixty are from traditions. A ḥadíth is not to be presented as Qur’án.
  • It must not say that Islám’s scholars accept the Bahá’í reading.
  • It must not claim that the Writings reinterpret “seal of the prophets” beyond what is quoted in sections 1 and 4.
  • It must not swap the titles. Shoghi Effendi names the Báb as the Qá’im and the Mihdí, and Bahá’u’lláh as the return of the Imám Ḥusayn and, to Sunní Islám, the descent of the “Spirit of God” (GPB IV.17, VI.11).
  • It must not state the place of the Caller in Qur’án 50:41 as fact (section 5.3).
  • Shí‘ih mourning forms (noha, ta’ziyeh, rowzeh-khani) should be used with care and not as ornament.
