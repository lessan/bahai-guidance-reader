The Fast: Law, Purpose, and the Month of ‘Alá’

Last updated 13 October 2026

What this page covers

Every year the Bahá’í Fast takes nineteen days. During them believers between fifteen and seventy abstain from food and drink between sunrise and sunset, and in the same days they pray and meditate more than usual. This page gathers what the Writings say about it, in six parts:

  1. The law: what Bahá’u’lláh ordained in the Kitáb-i-Aqdas and in His answers to questions about it, and who is exempt.
  2. The calendar: why the Fast falls in the month of ‘Alá’ (Loftiness), after the Intercalary Days and before Naw-Rúz.
  3. The purpose: what Bahá’u’lláh, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Shoghi Effendi and the Universal House of Justice say the Fast is for.
  4. The felt experience: what the texts say about heat, longing, dawn, and power over the lower self. The texts speak of these directly in several places.
  5. Accidents: what is written about eating or drinking without noticing.
  6. Renewal: how the texts connect the Fast to a fresh start in the spiritual life and to service.

The page was written as the source for a song. For that reason it quotes more than it paraphrases, and it marks which words are the Writings’ own and which are the compiler’s.

All quotations are copied from texts held in this knowledge base; the file for each is listed under Sources. Three limits apply. Bahá’u’lláh’s Tablets on the Fast are quoted here from authorised translations in compilations, and the compilations usually do not name the original Tablet; this page says so where it applies. Letters written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi are labelled that way and are not his own writing. Reports of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s table talks taken down by pilgrims are labelled as such and are not authoritative texts.

1. The law

The ordinance

The Fast is set out in two paragraphs of the Kitáb-i-Aqdas. The first fixes its length, its place in the year, and who is exempt:

“O Pen of the Most High! Say: O people of the world! We have enjoined upon you fasting during a brief period, and at its close have designated for you Naw-Rúz as a feast. Thus hath the Daystar of Utterance shone forth above the horizon of the Book as decreed by Him Who is the Lord of the beginning and the end. Let the days in excess of the months be placed before the month of fasting. We have ordained that these, amid all nights and days, shall be the manifestations of the letter Há, and thus they have not been bounded by the limits of the year and its months. It behooveth the people of Bahá, throughout these days, to provide good cheer for themselves, their kindred and, beyond them, the poor and needy, and with joy and exultation to hail and glorify their Lord, to sing His praise and magnify His Name; and when they end—these days of giving that precede the season of restraint—let them enter upon the Fast. Thus hath it been ordained by Him Who is the Lord of all mankind. The traveler, the ailing, those who are with child or giving suck, are not bound by the Fast; they have been exempted by God as a token of His grace. He, verily, is the Almighty, the Most Generous.” — Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶16

The second states the rule itself and sets it among the other laws:

“These are the ordinances of God that have been set down in the Books and Tablets by His Most Exalted Pen. Hold ye fast unto His statutes and commandments, and be not of those who, following their idle fancies and vain imaginings, have clung to the standards fixed by their own selves, and cast behind their backs the standards laid down by God. Abstain from food and drink from sunrise to sundown, and beware lest desire deprive you of this grace that is appointed in the Book.” — Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶17

Two features of these paragraphs matter for what follows. The rule is short: abstain from food and drink from sunrise to sundown. And the Fast is called “this grace that is appointed in the Book”. The warning is against letting “desire” take that grace away.

The age at which the duty begins comes earlier in the Book:

“We have commanded you to pray and fast from the beginning of maturity; this is ordained by God, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers. He hath exempted from this those who are weak from illness or age, as a bounty from His Presence, and He is the Forgiving, the Generous.” — Kitáb-i-Aqdas, ¶10

Prayer and fasting are enjoined together in this verse, and that pairing recurs through the Writings quoted below.

What the Synopsis and Codification add

The Synopsis and Codification, which the Universal House of Justice published with the Book, gathers the law of the Fast into six points. Its items 2 to 4 read:

“The period of fasting commences with the termination of the Intercalary Days, and ends with the Naw-Rúz Festival.”

“Abstinence from food and drink, from sunrise to sunset, is obligatory.”

“Fasting is binding on men and women on attaining the age of maturity, which is fixed at 15.”

The notes to the Book add the upper limit: “Fasting is enjoined on all the believers once they attain the age of 15 and until they reach the age of 70 years.” (note 25). They also say that the period “usually” runs 2–20 March (note 25). Since 2015 the first day of the Fast has depended each year on the day of Naw-Rúz, which is fixed by astronomical computation with Ṭihrán as the reference point (Kitáb-i-Aqdas, editorial notes after the Synopsis and Codification, nos. 1 and 2, citing the Universal House of Justice’s message of 10 July 2014). So a believer should take the dates from the current Bahá’í calendar and not from a fixed March date.

The exemptions, briefly

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas names the traveller, the ailing, women with child or giving suck (¶16), those weak from illness or age (¶10), and women in their courses (¶13). Bahá’u’lláh’s answers to questions add detail. The Synopsis and Codification lists them (IV.B.5):

  • travellers, where the journey exceeds nine hours, or two hours on foot (Q&A 22 and 75), with detailed rules about breaking a journey;
  • the ill, the over-seventy, women who are pregnant or nursing, and women in their courses (who perform ablutions and repeat a revealed verse ninety-five times a day);
  • those engaged in heavy labour.

Four of Bahá’u’lláh’s answers bear directly on the Fast:

“Should the feasts celebrating the Twin Birthdays or the Declaration of the Báb fall within the month of fasting, the command to fast shall not apply on that day.” — Q&A 36

“Such people are excused from fasting; however, in order to show respect to the law of God and for the exalted station of the Fast, it is most commendable and fitting to eat with frugality and in private.” — Q&A 76, on people engaged in hard labour

“In truth, I say that obligatory prayer and fasting occupy an exalted station in the sight of God. It is, however, in a state of health that their virtue can be realized. In time of ill health it is not permissible to observe these obligations; such hath been the bidding of the Lord, exalted be His glory, at all times.” — Q&A 93

“The ordinance of fasting is such as hath already been revealed. Should someone pledge himself, however, to offer up a fast to God, seeking in this way the fulfillment of a wish, or to realize some other aim, this is permissible, now as heretofore. Howbeit, it is God’s wish, exalted be His glory, that vows and pledges be directed to such objectives as will profit mankind.” — Q&A 71, on fasting outside the month of ‘Alá’

Guidance from Shoghi Effendi’s secretaries and from the Universal House of Justice fills in the practice:

  • Keeping the Fast is the responsibility of the individual believer. “No Assembly has the right to enforce it on the friends, or to hold anybody responsible for not observing it.” (letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi to an individual believer, 9 March 1937, in Lights of Guidance, no. 776).
  • A person who is unsure whether fasting would harm his health should take medical advice: “If there is any doubt in the mind of a person as to whether it will really be bad for that person’s health to keep it, the best doctor’s advice should be obtained.” The same letter adds: “It is very good for the health and, once one forms the habit, each year it becomes easier to keep, unless one is rundown. No one is obliged to keep it if it really harms them.” (letter on behalf of the Guardian to an individual believer, 14 April 1947, Lights of Guidance, no. 778).
  • Smoking counts as drinking. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá states in a Tablet that smoking is a form of “drink”; the Universal House of Justice has said this application of the law has not yet been extended to Western believers and “should not be made an issue” (letter of 15 March 1972, Lights of Guidance, no. 782).

2. The place of the Fast in the year

Intercalary Days, then ‘Alá’, then Naw-Rúz

The Báb’s calendar has nineteen months of nineteen days, with four or five extra days. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas places those extra days immediately before the month of fasting. The note explains: “In the Bayán, the Báb ordained the month of ‘Alá’ to be the month of fasting, decreed that the day of Naw-Rúz should mark the termination of that period, and designated Naw-Rúz as the Day of God. Bahá’u’lláh confirms the Badí‘ calendar wherein Naw-Rúz is designated as a feast.” (note 26).

So the year ends in this order: the Intercalary Days (Ayyám-i-Há), a time of giving; the Fast, a time of restraint; and then Naw-Rúz, the first day of the new year, a feast. The Aqdas itself describes the Ayyám-i-Há as “these days of giving that precede the season of restraint”. A letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi says “the intercalary days are specially set aside for hospitality, the giving of gifts, etc.” (quoted in note 29).

Bahá’u’lláh revealed prayers for each of the three. For the days before the Fast:

“My God, my Fire and my Light! The days which Thou hast named the Ayyám-i-Há (the Days of Há, Intercalary Days) in Thy Book have begun, O Thou Who art the King of names, and the Fast which Thy most exalted Pen hath enjoined unto all who are in the kingdom of Thy creation to observe is approaching.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations XLV; Bahá’í Prayers, “Intercalary Days” (which omits the parenthetical gloss)

For the feast that closes it:

“Praised be Thou, O my God, that Thou hast ordained Naw-Rúz as a festival unto those who have observed the Fast for love of Thee and abstained from all that is abhorrent unto Thee. Grant, O my Lord, that the fire of Thy love and the heat produced by the Fast enjoined by Thee may inflame them in Thy Cause, and make them to be occupied with Thy praise and with remembrance of Thee.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations XLVI; Bahá’í Prayers, “Naw-Rúz”

The same Naw-Rúz prayer ends: “He Who is Thy Branch and all Thy company, O my Lord, have broken this day their fast, after having observed it within the precincts of Thy court, and in their eagerness to please Thee.” (Bahá’í Prayers, “Naw-Rúz”).

‘Alá’, “Loftiness”

In the long Prayer for the Fast that is in the 2000 compilation of the Research Department, Bahá’u’lláh Himself explains the name of the month:

“All glory be to Thee, O my God, for Thou hast graciously enabled me to fast during this month which Thou hast related to Thy Name, the Most Exalted, and called ‘Alá (Loftiness). Thou hast commanded that Thy servants and Thy people should fast therein and seek thereby to draw nearer unto Thee. The days and months of the year have culminated with the Fast, even as the first month began with Thy Name, Bahá, that all might testify that Thou art the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden, and be well assured that the glory of all names is conferred only through the glory of Thy Cause and the word expounded by Thy will and revealed through Thy purpose. Thou hast ordained that this month be a remembrance and honor from Thee, and a sign of Thy presence amongst them, that they may not forget Thy grandeur and Thy majesty, Thy sovereignty and Thy glory, and may be well assured that from time immemorial Thou hast ever been and wilt ever be Ruler over the entire creation.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayer for the Fast (VI), in The Importance of Obligatory Prayer and Fasting, a compilation prepared by the Research Department of the Universal House of Justice (May 2000)

This passage supplies three facts. The first month of the year is named Bahá and the last is named ‘Alá’, which is also called Loftiness, and the passage says the year “culminated with the Fast”. The month is “a remembrance and honor”. And it is “a sign of Thy presence amongst them, that they may not forget Thy grandeur and Thy majesty”. The compilation does not name the Tablet this prayer comes from.

3. What the Fast is for

Bahá’u’lláh

Bahá’u’lláh’s statements about the purpose fall into five groups. All are from The Importance of Obligatory Prayer and Fasting (2000) unless noted; the compilation numbers its passages in Roman numerals and does not name the Tablets, so only the section is given.

It draws the believer near to God.

“And We have ordained obligatory prayer and fasting so that all may by these means draw nigh unto God, the Most Powerful, the Well-Beloved.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Bahá’u’lláh section, I

It gives the Fast a rank alongside prayer, and spiritual lift.

“Fasting and obligatory prayer are as two wings to man’s life. Blessed be the one who soareth with their aid in the heaven of the love of God, the Lord of all worlds.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, III

“Cling firmly to obligatory prayer and fasting. Verily, the religion of God is like unto heaven; fasting is its sun, and obligatory prayer is its moon. In truth, they are the pillars of religion whereby the righteous are distinguished from those who transgress His commandments.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, IV

It is both hard and a gift.

“Even though outwardly the Fast is difficult and toilsome, yet inwardly it is bounty and tranquillity.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, XVI

It treats the self.

“Verily, I say, fasting is the supreme remedy and the most great healing for the disease of self and passion.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, XVII

The same sentence is selected in Bahá’í Sacred Writings (2026), chapter 5, “Responding to the Call of Bahá’u’lláh”, passage 23.1, which does not name the Tablet either. There is also a wider meaning of fasting:

“We beseech God to assist His people that they may observe the most great and exalted Fast, which is to protect one’s eye from beholding whatever is forbidden and to withhold one’s self from food, drink and whatever is not of Him.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, XII

It is a law whose reasons are partly hidden.

“There are various stages and stations for the Fast and innumerable effects and benefits are concealed therein. Well is it with those who have attained unto them.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, XIX

“Say: By God! His Law is a fortress unto you, could ye but understand. Verily, He hath no purpose therein save to benefit the souls of His servants, but, alas, the generality of mankind remain heedless thereof.” — Bahá’u’lláh section, XIV (the passage opens: “This is one of the nights of the Fast”)

Another passage, in the Prayers and Meditations and the Bahá’í Prayers, puts the purpose in a prayer for “all men”:

“These are the days whereon Thou hast bidden all men to observe the Fast, that through it they may purify their souls and rid themselves of all attachment to any one but Thee… Grant, O my Lord, that this fast may become a river of life-giving waters and may yield the virtue wherewith Thou hast endowed it.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LVI; Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

Two further statements tie the Fast to others. One is about wealth and hardship: “All praise be unto God, Who hath revealed the law of obligatory prayer as a reminder to His servants, and enjoined on them the Fast that those possessed of means may become apprised of the woes and sufferings of the destitute.” (Bahá’u’lláh section, II). The other is about service. The first-day prayer asks that those who fast become “as hands of Thy Cause amongst Thy servants” (see section 6).

‘Abdu’l-Bahá

‘Abdu’l-Bahá calls the Fast “a fundamental of the Divine law, and one of the pillars of the religion of God” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, XXV). He also says: “In the realm of worship, fasting and obligatory prayer constitute the two mightiest pillars of God’s holy Law” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, II). On what the outward fast stands for:

“Well is it with you, as you have followed the Law of God and arisen to observe the Fast during these blessed days, for this physical fast is a symbol of the spiritual fast. This Fast leadeth to the cleansing of the soul from all selfish desires, the acquisition of spiritual attributes, attraction to the breezes of the All-Merciful, and enkindlement with the fire of divine love.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, XXVI

A second Tablet uses nearly the same words:

“For this material fast is an outer token of the spiritual fast; it is a symbol of self-restraint, the withholding of oneself from all appetites of the self, taking on the characteristics of the spirit, being carried away by the breathings of heaven and catching fire from the love of God.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Selections from the Writings of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, no. 35

And two shorter lines: “Fasting is the cause of the elevation of one’s spiritual station.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, XXVII) and, about prayer and fasting together, “obligatory prayer and fasting produce awareness and awakening in man, and are conducive to his protection and preservation from tests” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, VII).

There is also a longer explanation of why people fast, which is a pilgrim’s record and not an authoritative text. Corinne True took down ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s table talk and it was printed in Star of the West (vol. IV, no. 18, p. 305), and Lights of Guidance (no. 779) quotes it. In it, ‘Abdu’l-Bahá says that the Manifestations fasted because the Word was descending so intensely that “there remains no condition or time for eating and drinking”, and that the believers fast to share that state:

“For every sincere soul who has a beloved longs to experience that state in which his beloved is. If his beloved is in a state of sorrow, he desires sorrow; if in a state of joy, he desires joy; if in a state of rest, he desires rest; if in a state of trouble, he desires trouble.” — ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, as recorded in Star of the West IV:18, quoted in Lights of Guidance, no. 779 (pilgrim’s notes)

The same source preserves a prayer attributed to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá for the Fast:

“O God! as I am fasting from the appetites of the body and not occupied with eating and drinking, even so purify and make holy my heart and my life from aught else save Thy Love, and protect and preserve my soul from self-passions and animal traits. Thus may the spirit associate with the Fragrances of Holiness and fast from everything else save Thy mention.” — attributed to ‘Abdu’l-Bahá, Star of the West IV:18, quoted in Lights of Guidance, no. 784 (pilgrim’s notes)

Shoghi Effendi

The best-known statement of the purpose comes from a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi to the National Spiritual Assembly of the United States, 10 January 1936. It was written by his secretary and is not the Guardian’s own text. It is quoted in the notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (note 25) and in Lights of Guidance (no. 775):

“The fasting period, which lasts nineteen days starting as a rule from the second of March every year and ending on the twentieth of the same month, involves complete abstention from food and drink from sunrise till sunset. It is essentially a period of meditation and prayer, of spiritual recuperation, during which the believer must strive to make the necessary readjustments in his inner life, and to refresh and reinvigorate the spiritual forces latent in his soul. Its significance and purpose are, therefore, fundamentally spiritual in character. Fasting is symbolic, and a reminder of abstinence from selfish and carnal desires.”

Another letter on his behalf adds a point about health: “Keeping the Fast is enjoined upon all Bahá’ís, regardless of nationality; it has a very salutary effect both physically and spiritually, and the friends should realize Bahá’u’lláh never would have instituted it if it were detrimental to the health.” (21 May 1946, Lights of Guidance, no. 777).

The Universal House of Justice

The compilation of May 2000 opens with the House of Justice’s reason for preparing it: it is “imperative for all Bahá’ís ‘to deepen their awareness of the blessings conferred by the laws which directly foster the devotional life of the individual and, thus, of the community.’ Among these laws are obligatory prayer and fasting which have been characterized by the Blessed Beauty as ‘two wings to man’s life.’” The introduction puts the first phrase in quotation marks without saying where it comes from.

4. The felt experience of the Fast

The material in this section answers a request for the song: the heat, the pull toward the spiritual world when the body is hungry, the early waking, and the sense of mastery over the lower self. The Writings describe some of this directly. They do not describe afternoon hunger as such, and where a link is only an inference the page says so.

Heat

Bahá’u’lláh speaks of the Fast producing heat, in two separate places.

“These are the days of the Fast. Blessed is the one who through the heat generated by the Fast increaseth his love, and who, with joy and radiance, ariseth to perform worthy deeds.” — Bahá’u’lláh, compilation section, XV

“Grant, O my Lord, that the fire of Thy love and the heat produced by the Fast enjoined by Thee may inflame them in Thy Cause, and make them to be occupied with Thy praise and with remembrance of Thee.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Naw-Rúz prayer, Prayers and Meditations XLVI

The first says what the heat does: it increases love and prompts “worthy deeds”. The second asks that this heat, joined to the fire of God’s love, should send the believers into the Cause. ‘Abdu’l-Bahá uses the same image: the Fast leads to “enkindlement with the fire of divine love” (XXVI) and to “catching fire from the love of God” (Selections, no. 35). These texts do not say how the heat is felt in the body. “Heat” is the word the Writings use for its effect on the soul.

Hunger, longing, and the pull toward the unseen

The Writings say the Fast detaches the believer from the world. They do not claim that hunger itself calls the mind to prayer, but some of their phrases fit what a faster notices in the late afternoon:

“…that I may so fix my gaze upon Thee and be so detached from all else but Thee, that the world and all that hath been created therein may appear before me as a fleeting day which Thou hast not deigned to create.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayer for the Fast, Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast” (Prayers and Meditations LVI)

“Their eyes have, at all times, been bent upon the Dayspring of Thy loving-kindness, and their faces set towards the Fountainhead of Thine inspiration.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LXXXV

“Cause me to taste, O my Lord, the divine sweetness of Thy remembrance and praise.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LVI

The longing is also in the vocabulary of the prayers: “O Desire of the hearts of ardent lovers and Beloved of the souls of them who are endued with understanding, O Rapture of the breasts of them that yearn after Thee and Object of the desire of them that seek Thee” (Prayer for the Fast, VI, compilation). The same prayer says the day’s fast was kept “until the day drew to an end and the time arrived to break the Fast”. The prayer and the pilgrim’s record agree on the central point: the believer who has a beloved wants to be where the beloved is. The texts quoted here do not say that hunger itself is what turns the mind to prayer, and this page does not claim it.

What the texts say about the experience of difficulty is this: the Fast is “outwardly… difficult and toilsome, yet inwardly it is bounty and tranquillity” (Bahá’u’lláh section, XVI). A person who finds the hungry hours both hard and consoling is describing what Bahá’u’lláh describes.

Power over the lower nature

On this theme the texts are direct. The Fast is “the supreme remedy and the most great healing for the disease of self and passion” (XVII). It leads “to the cleansing of the soul from all selfish desires” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá, XXVI). Shoghi Effendi’s secretary calls it “a reminder of abstinence from selfish and carnal desires” (Lights of Guidance, no. 775). In the prayer where Bahá’u’lláh speaks of the months of the year ending in ‘Alá’, He asks to be numbered “with those whom the breezes of Thy will move as they list, not with those whom the wind of self and passion stirreth and directeth as it pleaseth” (Prayer for the Fast, VI).

The Kitáb-i-Aqdas names the danger in the same terms: “beware lest desire deprive you of this grace that is appointed in the Book” (¶17). One passage in Gleanings uses the Fast as the example of a believer’s will being given up to God:

“Whatsoever the Creator commandeth His creatures to observe, the same must they diligently, and with the utmost joy and eagerness, arise and fulfill. They should in no wise allow their fancy to obscure their judgment, neither should they regard their own imaginings as the voice of the Eternal. In the Prayer of Fasting We have revealed: ‘Should Thy Will decree that out of Thy mouth these words proceed and be addressed unto them, “Observe, for My Beauty’s sake, the fast, O people, and set no limit to its duration,” I swear by the majesty of Thy glory, that every one of them will faithfully observe it, will abstain from whatsoever will violate Thy law, and will continue to do so until they yield up their souls unto Thee.’ In this consisteth the complete surrender of one’s will to the Will of God.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá’u’lláh CLX

It follows from this that the Fast is not mainly a test of toughness. In Bahá’u’lláh’s words the power is the believer’s will given up to God’s. A believer who feels more control over appetite as the nineteen days go on is describing the effect the Writings promise: the Fast treats “the disease of self and passion”. The Writings do not describe such a feeling as an achievement to be proud of. In the same prayer Bahá’u’lláh says: “Alas, alas, O my God, for the things that have escaped me during Thy days, and again alas, alas, O Desire of my heart, for what I have left undone in service and obedience to Thee during these days” (Prayer for the Fast, VI).

Dawn

The dawn is the part of the day on which the Writings speak most vividly in connection with the Fast.

A prayer revealed for the Fast says that the lovers of God rise before light:

“For Thine ardent lovers Thou hast, according to Thy decree, reserved, at each daybreak, the cup of Thy remembrance, O Thou Who art the Ruler of rulers! These are they who have been so inebriated with the wine of Thy manifold wisdom that they forsake their couches in their longing to celebrate Thy praise and extol Thy virtues, and flee from sleep in their eagerness to approach Thy presence and partake of Thy bounty.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LXXXV; Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

Another prayer in the same group speaks of the same thing from the believer’s side: “I beseech Thee, O my God, by the fire of Thy love which drove sleep from the eyes of Thy chosen ones and Thy loved ones, and by their remembrance and praise of Thee at the hour of dawn, to number me with such as have attained unto that which Thou hast sent down in Thy Book and manifested through Thy will.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations CLXXVII; Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”).

A third prayer, in the Gleanings, describes servants who “observe in the daytime the fast prescribed by Thee, who arise, at the earliest dawn of day, to make mention of Thy Name, and to celebrate Thy praise, in the hope of obtaining their share of the goodly things that are treasured up within the treasuries of Thy grace and bounty.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Gleanings CXXXVIII).

The wider teaching on dawn is not limited to the Fast, but it shows why the early hours are valued:

  • “Recite ye the verses of God every morn and eventide.” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶149). Asked what that means, Bahá’u’lláh answers: “To read one verse, or even one word, in a spirit of joy and radiance, is preferable to the perusal of many Books.” (Q&A 68).
  • “At the dawn of every day he should commune with God, and, with all his soul, persevere in the quest of his Beloved.” (Gleanings CXXV, on the seeker).
  • “I have risen from my couch at this dawntide when the Daystar of Thy oneness hath shone forth from the Dayspring of Thy will” (Prayers and Meditations CXLIV).
  • “Blessed is he who, at the hour of dawn, centering his thoughts on God, occupied with His remembrance, and supplicating His forgiveness, directeth his steps to the Mashriqu’l-Adhkár…” (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶115). Bahá’u’lláh says in Q&A 15 that “at the hour of dawn” is acceptable “at the earliest dawn of day, between dawn and sunrise, or even up to two hours after sunrise”.
  • ‘Abdu’l-Bahá: “Each morn God’s infinite grace confirmeth the ardent and tearful invocations of ‘Abdu’l-Bahá. Accordingly, let every awakened soul obtain, to the extent of its capacity, a portion of this spiritual grace. This can be achieved by fervently offering unto God prayers and supplications at every dawn and observing the law of obligatory prayer.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, X).

The Fast begins at sunrise, so the pre-dawn hours are the time to eat and to pray before the day’s abstention begins. The texts do not prescribe a pre-dawn meal or a dawn prayer for the Fast. They describe waking at daybreak as what the lovers of God do, and it is the believer’s choice to wake early in these days.

Nineteen days, and a longer hope

The prayer that contains “all their days are days of fasting” tells of people who have so tasted the sweetness of God’s call that, if told to “set no limit” to the Fast, they would continue it for as long as they lived. It says that for them “all their days are days of fasting” and that this is because “they have tasted the sweetness of Thy call, and become inebriated with Thy remembrance and praise and with the words proceeding from the lips of Thy command” (Prayer for the Fast, VI, compilation).

5. If you eat or drink without noticing

This is the point that matters for the humorous part of the song, and the texts are short and clear.

“If one eats unconsciously during the fasting hours, this is not breaking the Fast as it is an accident.” — Shoghi Effendi, in Principles of Bahá’í Administration, pp. 9–10; quoted in Lights of Guidance, no. 780

That is the whole of what the Writings say. Four details should be kept accurate:

  1. Source. Principles of Bahá’í Administration prints the passage as “(Shoghi Effendi)”; Lights of Guidance gives it under his name without date or recipient. It was probably written on his behalf, as most of his replies to individual questions were, but the compilations do not say. The passage is part of a longer answer that also covers travellers, the age limit of seventy, and resuming the Fast after illness.
  2. Wording. The text says “eats unconsciously” and calls it “an accident”. It does not name drinking. The Kitáb-i-Aqdas always joins “food and drink” (¶17), and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá defines the Fast as abstinence from food and drink, so applying the same reasoning to an unnoticed sip of water is a natural reading. It is the reader’s inference, however, and not a stated ruling.
  3. What it does not say. It does not say anything about what to do once one notices, and it does not grant leave to carry on eating. Stopping on noticing is the plain implication of calling the earlier eating “an accident”, but this is again an inference.
  4. What it is not. It is not a statement about deliberately breaking the Fast. For illness, travel and other grounds for not fasting, see section 1. Neither the Kitáb-i-Aqdas nor its Questions and Answers mention forgetfulness or inadvertent eating, and no letter in this knowledge base sets a penalty or a make-up day. The Guardian’s secretary says that a missed fast cannot be made up later outside its month: “the Fast, as you know, can only be kept during the month set aside for that purpose.” (same passage).

There is one prayer on the broken Fast, and it should not be read as a ruling on accidents. In the Naw-Rúz prayer Bahá’u’lláh says: “Shouldst Thou regard him who hath broken the Fast as one who hath observed it, such a man would be reckoned among them who from eternity had been keeping the Fast. And shouldst Thou decree that he who hath observed the Fast hath broken it, that person would be numbered with such as have caused the Robe of Thy Revelation to be stained with dust, and been far removed from the crystal waters of this living Fountain.” (Prayers and Meditations XLVI). The prayer’s subject is that the worth of every act depends on God’s acceptance (“the doings of men are all dependent upon Thy good pleasure”), not on a bare outward count. It is about acceptance and does not say a sip is excused.

6. Renewal

Readjustment and recovery

Shoghi Effendi’s secretary described the Fast as a time in which the believer “must strive to make the necessary readjustments in his inner life, and to refresh and reinvigorate the spiritual forces latent in his soul” (Lights of Guidance, no. 775). The word “recuperation” in the same sentence describes the Fast as a time of recovery. Elsewhere the same writer says that the Fast “becomes easier” each year once “one forms the habit” (no. 778).

Many believers describe using the Fast to re-base and re-orient, and to push back into spiritual territory. The Writings do not use “re-base”, but “readjustments in his inner life” and “refresh and reinvigorate” name the same activity, and ‘Abdu’l-Bahá’s “awareness and awakening” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, VII) describes its result. The timing also fits: the Fast ends the Bahá’í year, and Naw-Rúz begins a new one. Bahá’u’lláh’s prayer for the month of ‘Alá’ says so in so many words: “The days and months of the year have culminated with the Fast”.

Asking for a fresh start in the prayers themselves

The Fast prayers do not ask only for the day to be kept. They ask for a change in the one who keeps it.

“Illumine their hearts, O my God, with the light of Thy knowledge and brighten their faces with the rays of the Daystar that shineth from the horizon of Thy Will.” — Bahá’u’lláh, first-day prayer, Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

“Do not bring our fasts to an end with this fast, O my Lord, nor the covenants Thou hast made with this covenant. Do Thou accept all that we have done for love of Thee, and for the sake of Thy pleasure, and all that we have left undone as a result of our subjection to our evil and corrupt desires. Enable us, then, to cleave steadfastly to Thy love and Thy good pleasure…” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LVI; Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

That second prayer asks that this Fast not be the last, and it asks God to accept what was done and what was left undone “as a result of our subjection to our evil and corrupt desires”. It is written for a believer who knows he has fallen short and is starting again.

The Fast and service

The Fast prayers also move from the self to work in the world. The prayer for the first day of the Fast continues:

“Assist them, O my God, to render Thee victorious and to exalt Thy Word. Suffer them, then, to become as hands of Thy Cause amongst Thy servants, and make them to be revealers of Thy religion and Thy signs amongst mankind, in such wise that the whole world may be filled with Thy remembrance and praise and with Thy proofs and evidences.” — Bahá’u’lláh, first-day prayer, Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

Another asks: “I beseech Thee by them to assist me in the promotion of Thy Cause and to make me steadfast in Thy love, that my footsteps may not slip on account of the clamor of Thy creatures.” (Bahá’u’lláh, Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”, second prayer). And Bahá’u’lláh blesses “the one who through the heat generated by the Fast increaseth his love, and who, with joy and radiance, ariseth to perform worthy deeds” (compilation section XV). ‘Abdu’l-Bahá makes the same link in a call to action: “O ye loved ones of God! Out of gratitude for firmness in the eternal Covenant arise to serve the threshold of the omnipotent Lord, observe obligatory prayer and fasting, and spend your time in diffusing the sweet savors of God and in spreading the Divine verses.” (‘Abdu’l-Bahá section, V).

One of the prayers also promises a hidden share of grace in every hour of the Fast:

“Thou hast endowed every hour of these days with a special virtue, inscrutable to all except Thee, Whose knowledge embraceth all created things. Thou hast, also, assigned unto every soul a portion of this virtue in accordance with the Tablet of Thy decree and the Scriptures of Thine irrevocable judgment.” — Bahá’u’lláh, Prayers and Meditations LXXXV; Bahá’í Prayers, “The Fast”

This fits the sense that the Fast offers a way back into the spiritual life: each hour has a share of grace, and the texts say a share is assigned to each soul.

What the sources do not settle

  • The original Tablets. The compilation of 2000 gives authorised translations without naming the Tablets; the same is true of “fasting is the supreme remedy…”, “two wings”, and “the heat generated by the Fast”. This page cannot say which Tablet they come from.
  • Pre-dawn practice. No text in this knowledge base prescribes a meal or prayer before sunrise. The pre-dawn prayer is a practice based on the verses about daybreak, not a stated rule.
  • Physical hunger. The Writings describe the Fast’s effect on the soul at length. Their account of what the body feels, such as the afternoon hunger that brings the Fast to mind, is limited to the one line “outwardly… difficult and toilsome”.
  • Accidents. Only one text addresses them (section 5). Its date and recipient are not given in the compilations.
  • Dates. The Fast’s dates now depend on the astronomical Naw-Rúz and should be taken from the current calendar.
